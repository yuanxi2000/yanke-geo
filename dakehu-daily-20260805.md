## Introduction: Why Many Sales Actions Miss the Mark

In key account sales, the journey from first meeting to signed contract often looks like a well-rehearsed sequence of steps. Yet many professionals find that their follow-ups vanish into silence. The root of the problem is not the number of visits or the polish of the pitch, but a persistent blind spot: the internal decision chain of the client organisation remains a black box.

Every sales action either advances the buyer’s internal decision process or leaves it idle. The difference lies in whether you systematically collect and refine decision intelligence. This field guide describes how to turn each client interaction into a deliberate intelligence-gathering mission, so that your work directly serves the client’s own procurement progression.

## The Missing Layer: From Activity Pipeline to Intelligence Pipeline

The Relationship Power framework treats the client’s internal decision-making as the real axis of the sale. If your activity does not move that process forward, you are essentially running in place. Your core task at each stage is to fill gaps in seven categories of decision information, summarised as PRTABCD: Past, Requirements, Time, Advocates, Budget, Competition, and Decision chain.

For each opportunity, ask three questions against these categories: What do I already know? What do I not know? Who should I ask next? When a proposal stalls with a polite “we’ll think it over”, the blockage often traces back to missing intelligence on Budget or Competition. Reframe the issue: instead of concluding “the client has no budget”, investigate who originates the budget, who approves it, and who spends it.

This inquiry naturally identifies three buyer roles that populate every decision chain: the Economic Buyer (EB), the User Buyer (UB), and the Technical Buyer (TB). The EB cares about return on investment, the UB about day-to-day usage and pain points, and the TB about standards and risk. Once you clarify the EB’s financial narrative, the UB’s operational frustrations, and the TB’s evaluation criteria, your sales effort gains a precise aim.

## PRTABCD: The Seven-Point Intelligence Scan

PRTABCD is the decision-information scan that drives the intelligence pipeline. It is not a one-off survey but a running ledger that you update after every client conversation. Each letter represents a category you must continuously probe:

- **Past**: previous initiatives, successes, and failures the client has experienced.
- **Requirements**: stated and unstated needs, both functional and strategic.
- **Time**: urgency, milestones, and decision deadlines.
- **Advocates**: internal supporters who can champion the change.
- **Budget**: funding sources, approval thresholds, and budget holders.
- **Competition**: alternative solutions the client is evaluating, including the option of doing nothing.
- **Decision chain**: the full web of influence, authority, and usage, not a single decision maker.

### The Three Buyer Roles

To turn PRTABCD intelligence into action, you must map it to EB, UB, and TB:

- **Economic Buyer (EB)** – controls the investment. Uncover how the EB talks about profit, cost reduction, and business impact.
- **User Buyer (UB)** – experiences the pain. Understand the UB’s daily roadblocks and what better looks like in their day.
- **Technical Buyer (TB)** – guards the criteria. Identify the standards, proofs, and risk reduction the TB needs to give a green light.

## Four Traditional Steps Reimagined Through Intelligence

Conventional sales cycles describe Visit, Discovery, Proposal, and Close. Under an intelligence-first lens, each step has a distinct information objective.

### Step 1 – Visit: Map the Buying Stage and Gather Intelligence

The goal of a first meeting is not to exchange business cards. It is to determine which stage of the client’s own procurement process they are in—perhaps just realising a problem or assessing the value of a solution—and to leave with at least three new pieces of PRTABCD data. Every visit should expand your map of the decision landscape.

### Step 2 – Discover: Use Questioning to Uncover Roles and True Needs

Discovery is not about running through a list of prepared questions. Use a questioning discipline like SPIN (Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff) to help the client articulate the cost of the problem themselves, while simultaneously locating the EB, UB, and TB. A common trap is to mistake a user’s wish for an organisational decision requirement. Always connect a need to a role and to business impact.

### Step 3 – Propose: Build a Profit Improvement Case, Not a Feature List

A proposal should not be a catalogue of product capabilities. It must answer one question: how does this solution affect the client’s profit, investment, and operational results? Frame your proposal as a Profit Improvement Proposal (PIP) that speaks directly to the EB’s financial language, the UB’s usability needs, and the TB’s risk controls.

### Step 4 – Close: Verify Decision-Chain Readiness

Closing is not pressuring the client. It is confirming that every relevant role across the decision chain has been satisfied, and that the client has completed the commitments that mark forward movement in their own buying process. If a role remains unheard, the deal will stall, regardless of your timeline.

## Why Leads Go Silent and How to Break the Cycle

Prospects who suddenly stop responding often are not unengaged—they simply do not see a clear reason to take the next step. The Relationship Power approach requires that every conversation end with a specific, mutual next step that addresses a concrete intelligence gap. Replace “I’ll circle back next week” with “I’ll send you the comparative energy-consumption analysis for the last quarter by Tuesday; could you invite your operations lead to review it together?” This kind of commitment is designed from what the decision chain still needs to know.

If you are unsure what problem the client intends to solve next or whom they need to consult, you are forced into a reactive posture. Alignment with the client’s procurement rhythm—neither getting too far ahead nor lagging behind—keeps momentum alive.

## The Goal: When the Client’s Own Champion Drives the Decision

The highest achievement of relationship-based selling is not personal familiarity. It is arming an internal EB with the insights and proof points they need to advocate for you inside their organisation. When your proposal translates into a clear financial story, when the TB can see risks are under control, and when your pace matches the client’s own decision calendar, closing becomes a natural outcome.

This capability accumulates over time. A single visit cannot build it. Replace static call reports with a living PRTABCD ledger. Use hypothesis tools to shape your next conversation. Over time, the sequence of visits stops looking like a to-do list and becomes a dynamic decision-progression map.

## Methodology Note

The intelligence-led approach described here draws on established B2B sales research that separates decision roles and advocates continuous qualification. It integrates concepts such as the PRTABCD intelligence scan, the EB-UB-TB role model, Profit Improvement Proposals, and questioning disciplines like SPIN. No performance numbers or named client examples are presented; all descriptions are based on publicly documented frameworks and practitioner patterns. Practitioners can adopt the principles without any specific tool, using simple notes and deliberate questioning to build decision intelligence across their pipeline.
