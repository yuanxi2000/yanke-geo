A customer's expansion announcement gives a sales team a reason to investigate. It does not establish a purchase opportunity. The useful next step is to connect the planned investment with operating costs, identify what remains unknown, and help the customer evaluate the economics.

The supplied source describes Liliang Diamond's long-term plan for 3,000 units of equipment, alongside cost-reduction projects involving a self-built 110 kV substation, self-built solar generation, and direct connections to wind-generated green electricity. These directions create a practical sales question: how would expansion and electricity-cost changes affect investment, unit costs, and margins?

This guide turns that question into a working process covering financial value, discovery, decision relationships, purchasing stages, and negotiation.

## Separate reported plans from confirmed decisions

The source attributes the planning information to East Money. It supports the stated equipment-scale ambition and the direction of associated electricity-cost projects. It does not establish selected suppliers, contract values, signing dates, decision-makers, or achieved savings.

Maintain three categories in your account notes:

- Publicly reported information: what the supplied report actually states.
- Customer-confirmed information: what the customer has verified, with its source recorded.
- Sales hypotheses: interpretations that still require evidence.

Keep unknown factual fields blank and record the question needed to resolve each one. Do not let a plausible hypothesis migrate into the confirmed category through repetition.

This distinction affects resource allocation. Treating an expansion plan as an order can move a team into proposals and pricing before the customer has established the investment case or purchasing responsibility.

## Build one business case for expansion and electricity

Financial value means translating an offering into the customer's investment, profit, and operating economics. Here, equipment scale raises questions about future electricity demand, substation capacity, the share of green electricity, and the applicable price structure.

Use a three-layer worksheet to connect these questions.

| Layer | What to capture | What to validate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Investment | Equipment, substation, solar, and wind-connection spending; timing | Scope, amounts, and investment schedule |
| Operations | Electricity demand, existing charges, possible price changes, and operating costs | Customer inputs and expansion assumptions |
| Profit | Electricity cost per unit and implications for gross margin | Production assumptions and cost allocation |

The worksheet can contain unresolved questions. Its purpose is to make the calculation inspectable, including the limits of the available information.

### Model expansion and savings together

Do not calculate energy savings against a static operation while discussing a larger future equipment base. Additional production can increase electricity consumption and absorb part of the savings from lower electricity prices or efficiency measures.

Start with the customer's expansion schedule and an electricity-demand range. Label the assumptions behind that range and ask the customer to verify them. Then examine how the proposed electricity arrangements could change costs under those assumptions.

Distinguish total electricity expenditure from electricity cost per unit of output. Expansion can change both, and a useful investment discussion needs to show how each connects to the customer's economics.

Use ranges where inputs remain uncertain. Explain which assumptions drive the result and how they will be checked. A polished single number cannot substitute for verified inputs.

## Use discovery questions in a deliberate sequence

The source uses SPIN: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions. Apply the sequence to help the customer describe the operating problem and judge the value of addressing it.

1. Situation: What is the current substation capacity? How is the electricity bill structured? What share of electricity currently comes from green sources?
2. Problem: After expansion, where could electricity capacity become constrained? Which part of the electricity cost is hardest to control?
3. Implication: How would a change in electricity prices affect unit costs? Which product lines and margins would be affected?
4. Need-payoff: If the electricity arrangements reduced costs, how much investment could those savings cover, and over what period?

Avoid jumping straight to a return-on-investment question. Without the operating baseline, an implication question can sound formulaic. Without an understood business impact, a payoff question can sound like a sales pitch.

Record the customer's wording. It provides a basis for checking the business case and deciding whether the next action matches the customer's actual concerns.

## Map the decision chain without filling gaps with guesses

Account relationships depend on professional insight, decision information, and verifiable trust. The source's PRTABCD structure helps expose missing information:

- P: Past activity and account history.
- R: Requirements.
- T: Timing and key milestones.
- A: Arm, meaning the person within the customer organization who embraces or supports your involvement.
- B: Budget.
- C: Competitors and alternative approaches.
- D: Decision, meaning the decision chain across people and departments.

For each category, record what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question.

In this case, establish who owns electricity expenditure, who owns expansion progress, where the budget sits, and who is advancing the substation and solar projects. These are questions to investigate; the public planning information does not answer them.

Keep A and D distinct. An internal supporter is not the whole decision chain. Likewise, identifying one senior person does not explain how several departments will participate in a purchasing decision.

## Match your next action to the customer's buying stage

The source describes two views of progress. The seller's five stages are contact, needs discovery, proposal, negotiation, and closing. The customer's seven purchasing stages are:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Confirming responsibility.
5. Choosing between building internally and buying externally.
6. Selecting solutions and suppliers.
7. Committing and signing.

These views have a many-to-many relationship. They should not be forced into a one-to-one sequence.

The reported planning information is more consistent with problem recognition or value assessment than with confirmed supplier selection. That is a working interpretation, not evidence of the customer's actual internal stage.

Check progress through customer behavior: has someone taken ownership of the objective, has there been budget activity, and is there a defined next step with a date? Visit counts and seller confidence do not establish purchasing progress.

Choose one action that addresses the current decision task. If value remains unverified, work on the operating assumptions and investment calculation before advancing to a detailed proposal.

## Prepare alternatives before discussing concessions

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best available action if agreement cannot be reached. Preparation should cover executable alternatives, limits, authority, interests, and a record of the discussion.

The customer's stated self-build and direct-connection directions deserve attention as potential alternatives to an external offer. Their practical availability and scope still need verification. Delaying investment or choosing another supplier may also be alternatives.

Ask what an external purchase could provide beyond the customer's own approach. The source identifies speed, certainty, and operations and maintenance capability as questions worth exploring, not established supplier advantages.

Once the customer confirms a relevant difference, commercial terms have a basis for exchange. Without that understanding, negotiation can become a sequence of price reductions detached from customer value.

## Actionable account checklist

Use this checklist before committing further proposal resources:

- [ ] Separate public information, customer confirmations, and sales hypotheses; update the record weekly.
- [ ] Translate the planned equipment scale into an electricity-demand range with explicit assumptions.
- [ ] Review expansion spending, electricity costs, and unit economics in one worksheet.
- [ ] Conduct the four-part discovery interview and record the customer's wording.
- [ ] Complete the seven PRTABCD categories, including a next question for each gap.
- [ ] Identify the customer's likely purchasing stage and the behavioral evidence supporting it.
- [ ] Select one next action appropriate to that stage.
- [ ] Examine self-build, delay, and supplier alternatives for practical feasibility.
- [ ] Leave unknown factual fields blank and document how they will be verified.

## FAQ

### Does the 3,000-unit plan establish an order?

No. It establishes the reported long-term equipment-scale plan. It does not confirm procurement scope, a supplier appointment, a contract, or a delivery schedule.

### Can lower electricity prices guarantee lower total bills?

No such outcome is established here. Expansion may increase consumption. Evaluate electricity prices, total demand, and unit production costs together before describing the financial effect.

### What should a proposal say when financial inputs are incomplete?

Identify the customer-provided inputs, calculation assumptions, data sources, and validation method. Present ranges where appropriate. Leave unconfirmed amounts and counterparties blank rather than inventing precision.

### What is the most useful immediate sales action?

A structured interview is the source's recommended starting point. Clarify electricity demand, bill structure, expansion timing, and cost objectives, then update the decision map and choose one appropriate follow-up.
