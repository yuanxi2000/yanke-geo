A leadership change at a major account is a reason to check your decision map. It does not establish that the customer has changed its purchasing process, budget, or preferred supplier. The leadership-change signal discussed in the source about Juewei Foods illustrates this distinction: public information can identify questions worth investigating, but customer evidence must establish the answers.

Start with three questions: Who now defines the problem? Who owns the result? What observable actions show that the customer still wants to proceed? Use those answers to update the opportunity before advancing a proposal.

## Recheck the decision map before quoting

An established contact may still welcome your calls while having less influence over the next decision. Equally, a leadership change may leave an existing purchase largely intact. Neither conclusion should be assumed.

Review the account record and separate what was previously established from what remains current. Check whether your advocate still influences the decision, who owns the new objectives, and whether budget responsibilities or evaluation criteria require clarification.

For each important entry, record:

- What you know and where that information came from.
- What remains unknown or may have changed.
- Who can verify it and which material would help.
- What customer action should follow verification.

Write uncertainty as a question. For example, ask who now owns the business outcome instead of recording an unverified replacement decision-maker. An unanswered question is more useful than a confident but unsupported account note.

## Use PRTABCD to find information gaps

PRTABCD organizes seven categories of decision information. Following a leadership-change signal, begin with A and D, then check the remaining categories for connected gaps.

| Category | Meaning | Practical check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P | Past and historical information | Which previous findings still apply? |
| R | Requirements | Who confirms the current problem? |
| T | Timing and milestones | Which decision dates remain valid? |
| A | Advocate, labeled Arm in the source | Who supports the proposal through action? |
| B | Budget | Who explains funding and approval requirements? |
| C | Competitors and alternatives | Which suppliers or alternative approaches are being considered? |
| D | Decision chain | Who approves, uses, funds, or can block the purchase? |

A identifies the person who actively supports your proposal. D maps the people and departments involved in deciding. Keeping these distinct helps prevent a supportive relationship from being mistaken for purchasing authority.

Check who has invested time, supplied useful business information, or helped arrange verification. Record each key person's position, influence, and next unanswered question. Professional insight and verifiable trust matter more here than social familiarity.

## Judge progress through customer behavior

The source calls for aligning five sales stages with seven customer purchasing stages. The practical principle is to assess progress through tasks the customer completes, rather than visits, seller activity, or a feeling that the relationship remains strong.

After a leadership change, the customer may revisit problem discovery or value assessment. It may also remain engaged in solution and supplier selection. Look for evidence that distinguishes these possibilities:

- A business owner agrees to confirm the problem: requirements may still be undergoing definition.
- The customer provides budget parameters, approval milestones, or validation criteria: there is evidence of purchasing work moving forward.
- The customer only says to wait and arranges no verification: the signal is weak, so return to questions and clarification.

Across the purchasing journey, useful evidence includes acknowledging a problem, assessing value, confirming that a solution exists, assigning ownership, comparing internal delivery with external purchase, defining selection criteria, and confirming budget and terms.

Do not replace these customer tasks with seller activity. Sending a proposal does not establish that the customer has agreed how to evaluate it.

## Ask questions that help the new decision-maker

Someone entering a decision role needs room to explain current priorities. Repeatedly challenging the old proposal can push the discussion toward defending previous choices instead of clarifying the next decision.

### Move from context to a practical validation step

Use the following sequence, adapting the wording to information already confirmed:

1. **Context:** What business constraints should this decision reflect under the current objectives?
2. **Difficulty:** Which existing practices could slow the result you need?
3. **Impact:** What would that delay or difficulty mean for the business?
4. **Value of change:** Which part of a possible improvement would you be willing to validate first?

The sequence moves from background through difficulty and consequences to the value of addressing the problem. Its purpose is to help the customer complete a decision task.

Listen for changes in ownership, requirements, and evaluation criteria. If the answers expose a gap in the account record, update it before treating the previous proposal as current.

## Rebuild the value case with customer-checkable assumptions

Value discussions should translate product functions into investment, benefits, and potential profit improvement that the customer can examine. They should not assign business targets to the customer without evidence.

The supplied source does not establish Juewei Foods' internal performance measures. Any account-specific value case therefore needs customer verification.

Prepare two complementary views:

- **Investment view:** Identify cost items, payback assumptions, and risk boundaries.
- **Operating-result view:** Explain how the proposed change could improve an outcome the customer confirms matters in the current phase.

Invite the customer to identify which figures need correction and which assumptions need testing. Keep proposed improvements separate from verified results.

A useful value discussion gives both parties something concrete to check. Repeating product features does not resolve uncertainty about whether an investment supports the customer's current objectives.

## Negotiate around executable alternatives and clear conditions

Incomplete decision information is a poor basis for exchanging a large discount for a signature. Before discussing concessions, clarify your alternatives, limits, authority, interests, and acceptable conditions. Keep a record of these boundaries.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best executable action available if agreement cannot be reached. It supports disciplined decisions about concessions; it is not a threat to use against the customer.

Also examine what the customer might do without an agreement, while labeling uncertain assumptions. If the opportunity remains plausible but information is incomplete, narrow the next agreement to a smaller scope. Specify:

- What will be validated and against which criteria.
- The applicable payment conditions.
- The boundaries for ending the arrangement.

If the customer will not commit to any validation action, reassess whether the opportunity is sufficiently substantiated to justify continued investment.

## Run a short team review and use this checklist

Leadership changes affect more than the account manager's contact list. The sales leader should assess opportunity priority and resource allocation. The account manager should update the seven information categories. Delivery or technical colleagues should turn validation criteria into actions the customer can inspect.

Keep the review focused on three answers: who defines the problem, which customer behavior provides the latest evidence, and what the customer should do next.

Before advancing the opportunity, check that the team has:

- [ ] Separated public signals from verified customer facts.
- [ ] Checked the advocate's current influence and willingness to act.
- [ ] Updated decision roles across approval, budget, use, and veto authority.
- [ ] Marked unknown requirements, timing, funding, and alternatives.
- [ ] Linked the current stage to observable customer behavior.
- [ ] Asked questions about current constraints and desired outcomes.
- [ ] Identified value assumptions requiring customer correction or validation.
- [ ] Recorded negotiation alternatives, limits, authority, and conditions.
- [ ] Defined a specific next customer action and its verification criteria.

Where evidence is missing, retain the gap in the account record and assign a verification step.

## FAQ

### Should every leadership change restart the sales process?

No. Recheck decision information first. The customer may revisit the problem or value case, but it may also continue supplier selection. Let customer behavior establish the appropriate stage.

### Is a friendly existing contact still an advocate?

Friendliness alone does not establish active support. Check whether the person still invests time, provides relevant information, and helps the customer take a meaningful next step.

### What should happen when the customer only says to wait?

Treat that response as weak evidence of progress. Return to clarification and seek a concrete verification action before advancing the opportunity's recorded stage.

### Can the value case include financial figures?

Yes, provided their status is clear. Identify costs, assumptions, and risk boundaries, and ask the customer to correct them. Do not present hypothetical benefits as achieved results or unverified targets as customer facts.

### When is a smaller agreement useful?

When information remains incomplete, a narrower scope can define the next validation task. The agreement still needs clear criteria, payment conditions, and exit boundaries.
