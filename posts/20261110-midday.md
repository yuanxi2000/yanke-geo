A bid award is a result. It does not, by itself, establish a durable customer relationship or a reason for the customer to buy again. For project-based key accounts, the work after selection is to turn an accepted proposal into delivery, acceptance, and a credible basis for future discussions.

The supplied source describes Gree central air conditioning winning a centralized procurement project for equipment-room cooling. Its public summary does not provide a contract-signing date. This guide uses that limited event as a starting point for a broader operating question: how should a sales team distinguish documented progress from confidence based on an award notice?

## Locate the project from both sides

Equipment-room cooling involves several customer interests. Users care about cooling headroom and stability. Operations teams care about fault response and spare parts. Procurement cares about price comparisons and process. Finance cares about budgets and depreciation. Passing one evaluation does not establish that these groups have agreed on the next purchase.

PSS5 uses two views of progress: the seller's activities and the buyer's purchasing work. Their stages have different levels of detail and form a many-to-many relationship. They should never be forced into matching numbered steps.

| Perspective | Stages in sequence | Evidence to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seller | Contact, needs, proposal, negotiation, close | Technical handover records, confirmed acceptance criteria, written discussion of expansion scope |
| Buyer | Recognize the problem, assess value, establish that a solution exists, assign responsibility, decide whether to build or buy, select a solution and supplier, commit and sign | Customer-initiated meetings, participation from additional departments, internal deadlines and concrete commitments |

An award notice usually places the buyer somewhere between supplier selection and commitment, while the seller may record the opportunity as closed. That is a documentary position, not a complete account of customer readiness.

Look for behavior: has the customer shared plans for the next group of equipment rooms, brought operations into a technical handover, or asked about delivery schedules and spare-parts arrival? If these signals are absent, the award alone cannot establish readiness for delivery or expansion. Record each side's position separately and identify the evidence needed for its next transition.

## Translate cooling capacity into operating value

A requirement to meet cooling capacity may support an evaluation, but it gives the customer little language for defending expenditure when budgets tighten. A useful value discussion connects equipment performance with measures the customer can report internally.

Relevant measures in the source include power supported per cabinet, business losses associated with heat-related throttling, annual electricity costs, and maintenance hours. These are areas to investigate, not promised improvements.

The seller can help define the measures and calculation methods. The inputs must come from the customer's operating records. Where records are missing, identify the gap instead of filling it with an attractive estimate presented as fact.

A one-page value statement should explain the operating issue, identify the records used, show the calculation, and distinguish established figures from unresolved assumptions. Its purpose is to make the customer's reasoning inspectable, not to manufacture a favorable business case.

## Map the people, process, and unknowns

PRTABCD is a seven-part scan of decision information. Use it after the award to expose what the team still does not know:

- **P — Past:** What relevant history is known about the account or project?
- **R — Requirements:** What needs have been established, and what remains unclear?
- **T — Timing:** What deadlines and internal milestones govern the work?
- **A — Arm:** Which specific person supports you inside the customer organization?
- **B — Budget:** What is known about the available budget and its basis?
- **C — Competition:** Which competitors or alternative approaches remain relevant?
- **D — Decision:** Which people and departments participate in the decision chain?

For every entry, record what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question. An empty next-question field signals that the next visit lacks a clear information objective.

### Separate the internal supporter from the decision chain

A identifies a concrete person who supports you. D describes a process involving multiple people and departments. One supportive contact does not establish who approves spending, confirms acceptance, or participates as a routine procedural requirement.

The source highlights A and D as particularly easy to neglect after an award. Revisit both before treating a friendly relationship as evidence of organizational commitment.

## Design follow-up questions that reveal consequences

A broad question about whether everything is going well invites a broad answer. SPIN provides four question types that can make a follow-up more useful:

- **Situation:** What equipment quantities and load patterns describe current operations?
- **Problem:** Which season or operating activity places the greatest strain on the current arrangement?
- **Implication:** What business consequences follow an unplanned outage or heat-related throttling?
- **Need-payoff:** What would earlier refurbishment or expansion help the customer avoid or improve?

Choose two types for a visit and explore them properly. Running through every category mechanically can leave the team with many answers but little usable understanding.

For example, a discussion of load patterns can establish the operating situation; a subsequent implication discussion can clarify why a difficult period matters to the business. Let the customer describe the consequence. Then connect the answer to the value statement and the relevant decision participants. Do not substitute a seller-generated benefit claim for the customer's account of its own operations.

## Negotiate exchanges of conditions

Centralized procurement can leave prices tightly constrained. Further discounting does not, by itself, establish a basis for long-term cooperation. The source frames negotiation around four considerations: power, time, information, and alternatives.

Before negotiating, write down your BATNA: the best alternative action available if an agreement cannot be reached. Make it executable rather than aspirational. Also document your limits, authority, interests, and negotiation record.

Conditions available for discussion may include:

- Delivery schedules and acceptance milestones.
- Spare-parts response arrangements and their duration.
- Maintenance coverage.
- Priority for a subsequent expansion phase.

Treat these as possible subjects for exchange, not benefits already secured. Establish which concessions you can authorize and what you need in return. A customer request for a lower price should therefore prompt a discussion of the complete package of conditions, supported by preparation about timing, information, and available alternatives.

## An actionable post-award checklist

Use this checklist for each important project:

- [ ] Record the seller stage and buyer stage separately.
- [ ] Attach customer behavior evidence to each stage judgment.
- [ ] Confirm who will join the technical handover and what acceptance criteria apply.
- [ ] Complete all seven PRTABCD entries, including information sources and unanswered questions.
- [ ] Identify the internal supporter and map the wider decision chain.
- [ ] Prepare a one-page value statement using customer operating records.
- [ ] Select two SPIN question types for the next discussion.
- [ ] Document the BATNA, limits, authority, interests, and negotiation record.
- [ ] Agree on the next concrete action and clarify the customer's internal timing.

Two recurring errors deserve attention during review. First, shipment or contract signature does not establish successful delivery acceptance. Second, repeated visits and warm conversations do not establish purchasing progress. A customer sharing an internal milestone can be more informative than the number of meetings held.

## FAQ

### Does winning the bid prove the relationship is secure?

No. It shows that the proposal and price passed the evaluation at that time. Continued purchasing requires its own evidence. The supplied public summary also leaves the signing date unspecified, so an award should not be rewritten as a confirmed contract-signing event.

### What customer actions deserve the most attention?

Look for customer-initiated meetings, the involvement of additional departments, disclosure of internal deadlines or budget assumptions, and willingness to discuss concrete acceptance, expansion, or maintenance scope. These actions provide a firmer basis for assessing progress than seller activity alone.

### Must the five seller stages match the seven buyer stages?

No. They describe different work. A seller may be negotiating while the buyer is still assessing value. Mark both positions from evidence, then decide whether the next conversation should advance the proposal or resolve an earlier purchasing question.

### What if operating data is unavailable?

Help the customer define the calculation and identify the records required. Keep the missing inputs visible. Do not replace unavailable customer data with a favorable number and present it as verified value.

### Why lead with the system capability?

A project award raises two immediate questions: where does the project stand, and what must happen next? The dual view organizes those questions. Value, relationship, questioning, and negotiation capabilities then support the evidence gathering and actions needed to move forward.
