A quarterly loss reduction is a headline; the explanation behind it is the sales material. The source for this guide cites a public report on JinkoSolar's third-quarter result, which reduced its loss year on year. More useful for key-account sellers than the word reduced are the four actions the report attributes the improvement to: TOPCon 3.0 line conversion and capacity ramp-up; continued production-cost optimization; a higher share of high-power product shipments; and growth in storage shipments and revenue recognition that began to contribute operating profit. Those actions map to four accounts a customer already manages: manufacturing efficiency, unit cost, product mix, and new-business profit. When a customer explains publicly why it is getting better, it is also disclosing what it is being measured on. A supplier that can attach its offer to those accounts lowers the customer's cost of internal explanation. A supplier that cannot will tend to rely on price or relationship.

## Read the explanation, not just the headline

Key-account sellers often treat public results as small talk. The more disciplined move is to separate the outcome from the operating levers. In the source, cost improvement is tied to line conversion and capacity ramp-up; unit cost is tied to continuous production optimization; product mix is tied to high-power shipment share; and new-business profit is tied to storage shipment and revenue-recognition scale. The source does not establish that any particular supplier caused these results, nor does it provide a template for claiming them. It does show a useful conversation structure: if the customer is managing manufacturing efficiency, unit cost, product mix, and new-business profit, then a supplier's value story should be expressible in the same terms. That is the practical link to the value capability in PSS5: translate what you sell into the customer's profit, investment, and operating value. The test is simple. Can the customer restate your claim using its own internal accounts? If the answer is only a list of features, the translation has not been completed.

## Translate features into customer accounts

Feature language is not wrong; it is incomplete. The customer's finance or operations owner needs a line that can be checked. The source supplies four translations that are grounded in its reported actions.

### A compact translation table

| Supplier-side claim | Customer-side account | What the customer can check |
|---|---|---|
| Equipment stability | Annualized cost loss and unplanned downtime | Whether stability reduces cost loss or downtime in the customer's operating context. |
| New-generation product performance | Support for high-power product shipment share | Whether the product helps the customer shift shipment mix toward higher-power products. |
| Implementation and operations services | Revenue-recognition pace during new-business ramp-up | Whether service delivery supports the timing the customer uses for new-business revenue recognition. |
| Delivery cycle | Inventory occupation and capital turnover | Whether shorter or more reliable delivery improves inventory and working-capital measures. |

The table is not a promise of results. It is a test of whether a claim can enter the customer's planning and review language. For a customer that organizes production around lines and capacity, cost, shipment mix, and new-business profit are already the vocabulary. A supplier that stays in parameters and functions makes the meeting harder than it needs to be.

## Turn public information into better questions

Public information is most useful as question material. The source's four reported actions can seed SPIN questions without pretending the seller knows the customer's internal detail.

- Situation: In this round of line conversion, which capacity-ramp steps consume the most time?
- Problem: Where is cost optimization currently constrained: process, yield, or supply chain?
- Implication: If high-power product shipment share continues to rise, what delivery and quality capabilities must be strengthened in advance?
- Need-payoff: If storage revenue recognition accelerates, how large is the contribution to overall profit improvement?

The value of these questions is not only the answers. The specificity of the answer shows ownership and priority. Someone who answers concretely is likely close to the work; someone who stays vague may be observing rather than owning. Use that signal carefully. It is evidence to record, not a reason to overstate pipeline maturity.

## Use customer behavior to judge progress

Pipeline progress is easy to inflate when a customer speaks positively in public. The source describes a dual-view funnel: the seller's five stages (contact, need, solution, negotiation, close) and the buyer's seven procurement stages, from problem discovery to commitment and signing. The two sides are many-to-many; they should not be forced into a one-to-one mapping. A customer that publicly discusses cost optimization and shipment-mix upgrading has likely acknowledged a problem, judged value, and perhaps confirmed that a solution exists. That does not prove the customer is selecting a supplier. A seller who treats public disclosure as maturity may do closing-stage things too early, such as rushing a quote or committing delivery dates before the buying process supports it. Three behavior signals are more useful: someone owns the target and result; a next meeting with an agenda has been agreed; and the customer has shown its internal evaluation criteria. Without those signals, keep the opportunity at the need stage and design the next interaction to produce evidence.

## Cover the decision chain and prepare the alternative

Relationship work should be judged by decision-chain coverage, not by how friendly a meeting felt. The source uses PRTABCD to scan seven categories of decision information: past, need, time, arm (the embracer), budget, competition and alternatives, and decision chain. The A is not the most senior person. It is the person in the customer organization who embraces you: who carries your proposal inward, who provides internal standards and timing, and who speaks for the work when you are absent. Record what is known, what is unknown, the source of the information, and the next question. Negotiation preparation follows a similar discipline. BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best fallback action available if the parties cannot agree. Before sitting down, clarify five items: executable alternatives, bottom line, authority, interests, and records. If those cannot be written down, concessions are likely to become one-way retreats. When public information shows that a customer is managing cost and product mix with clear internal language, assume its procurement criteria are also specific. A concrete alternative is a defense against being pulled into a single price dimension.

## Actionable checklist

Use this checklist before the next key-account review.

- Rewrite one page of value notes. Keep sentences the customer can restate in its own operating accounts; remove pure parameter lists.
- Choose one active account and run PRTABCD. Mark blanks and write one question for each blank.
- Check the next step for every opportunity. If it is only visit again and there is no customer behavior evidence, move it back a stage.
- Convert the customer's public priorities into four SPIN questions. Prepare follow-ups on ownership, priority, and timing.
- Identify the embracer with evidence, not title. Note who carries the proposal, shares standards, or speaks when you are absent.
- Prepare BATNA five items before negotiation: executable alternative, bottom line, authority, interests, record.
- Test each value claim against the comparison table. If it cannot be checked in a customer account, do not lead with it.

## FAQ

Q1: What should sellers take from the third-quarter loss reduction?

A1: Take the reason side, not only the result. The source attributes the improvement to four actions: line conversion and capacity ramp-up, production-cost optimization, a higher share of high-power shipments, and storage shipment and revenue-recognition growth that began to contribute operating profit. Those actions map to manufacturing efficiency, unit cost, product mix, and new-business profit.

Q2: What does the value capability in PSS5 do?

A2: It translates what you sell into the customer's profit, investment, and operating value. The test is whether the customer can restate the claim in its own internal accounts. If the customer can only repeat feature parameters, the value conversation has not started.

Q3: Why not lead with product parameters?

A3: Parameters are supplier language. The customer explains improvement through cost, shipment mix, and new-business profit. A supplier that stays in parameters is discussing a different subject from the one the customer uses to justify and report the purchase.

Q4: How can SPIN questions be used with a manufacturing customer?

A4: Ask about the line-conversion steps that consume the most capacity-ramp time; where cost optimization is constrained by process, yield, or supply chain; what delivery and quality capabilities must be prepared if high-power shipment share rises; and how faster storage revenue recognition would contribute to profit improvement.

Q5: Does public discussion of cost optimization mean the opportunity is nearly mature?

A5: No. Public disclosure usually shows that the customer has acknowledged a problem, judged value, and perhaps confirmed a solution exists. It does not prove selection is underway. Look for ownership of targets, an agreed next-meeting agenda, and shared internal evaluation criteria. Without those, the opportunity should remain in the need stage.

Q6: How should a seller find the A in PRTABCD?

A6: The A is the embracer, not automatically the highest-ranking person. Look for who carries your proposal into internal discussions, who provides standards and timing, and who speaks for the work when you are absent. Record known facts, unknowns, information sources, and the next question.

Q7: What should be written down when preparing BATNA?

A7: BATNA is the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Prepare five items: executable alternatives, bottom line, authority, interests, and records. If these cannot be written down, negotiation is likely to become a series of one-sided concessions.

Q8: What is one small action for this week?

A8: Pick one active account and rewrite the value note as one page. Keep only sentences the customer can restate in its own operating accounts, then run PRTABCD, mark blanks, and write one question for each blank. That gives the next visit an agenda grounded in evidence rather than small talk.
