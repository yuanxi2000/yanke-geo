When a company announces a large computing-power technical services contract, most market commentary focuses on the contract itself — its size, its timing, its headline value. For key-account sales professionals, however, the more useful question is different: what decision steps did both buyer and seller actually complete to get this deal done, and which of those steps can be replicated in your own territory?

This guide uses the PSS5 framework — a five-capability model for key-account sales — to break down how large deals of this type come together. The goal is not to speculate about any specific company's internal process, but to extract a repeatable way of thinking about long-cycle, high-value B2B contracts.

## Start With the Coordinates: Five Capabilities Behind Every Large Deal

PSS5 is a five-star capability framework for professional key-account sales. It consists of five dimensions: value capability, relationship capability, questioning capability, system capability, and negotiation capability.

- **Value capability** translates a product or service into the customer's profit and return on investment.
- **Relationship capability** penetrates the customer's decision chain.
- **Questioning capability** uncovers and amplifies real needs.
- **System capability** manages the sales process through a funnel.
- **Negotiation capability** secures a sustainable agreement across power, time, information, and alternatives.

Every large deal is the combined result of these five dimensions. Miss one, and the deal can stall halfway. Computing-power technical services contracts are especially typical: large amounts, long cycles, and complex internal dynamics on the buyer side mean none of the five dimensions can be treated as optional.

### Value Capability: Computing-Power Services Sell Business Improvement, Not Hardware

When a buyer signs a computing-power technical services contract, they are never buying technical terms. They are buying business capability: faster training, more stable inference, earlier launch of new business lines.

If you are the seller, the correct move is to translate service capability into the customer's business numbers — how much unit inference cost drops, how much the new model iteration cycle shortens, how many times the supported business volume can scale. This is the action of value capability: jumping from product features to investment, return, and profit improvement that the customer can understand.

Every sentence in your proposal should be something the customer's finance and business leaders can read and calculate. Fail at this step, and every later negotiation gets compressed into a price comparison.

## Relationship Capability: Use PRTABCD to Scan Seven Decision Information Items

In large deals like computing-power procurement, decisions are never made by one person. Technical departments care about performance. Business departments care about output. Finance cares about payment rhythm. Management cares about risk.

The point of relationship capability is not socializing. It is understanding and covering the entire decision chain. How do you do it? Use PRTABCD to scan seven items:

- **P** — past cooperation and historical information
- **R** — requirements
- **T** — timing and key milestones
- **A** — advocates: who inside the customer organization is embracing you
- **B** — budget
- **C** — competitors and alternatives
- **D** — decision chain

For each item, record what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question. Pay special attention to A and D. Without an advocate, you cannot even get into the customer's internal meetings. Without a clear view of the decision chain, your proposal only reaches people who do not speak.

## Questioning Capability: Let the Customer Say What Computing Power Must Solve

Computing-power needs are often packaged as configuration lists, but behind the list is a business problem. The practice of questioning capability follows SPIN:

1. **Situation questions** map the customer's current computing layout and business plans.
2. **Problem questions** identify bottlenecks — insufficient computing power, inefficient scheduling, or runaway costs.
3. **Implication questions** amplify the consequences: if the bottleneck is not solved, how long will the new product launch be delayed, how much revenue will be lost.
4. **Need-payoff questions** guide the customer to describe what change would look like.

When the customer says the problem and the expected value themselves, it is far more effective than anything you could repeat a hundred times. A strong questioner has the skeleton of the order built in the conversation before the proposal is even written.

## System Capability: Judge Progress With a Dual-Perspective Funnel, Not Gut Feeling

Large deals have long cycles. The most common mistake is treating your own sense of progress as actual progress. PSS5 system capability provides two axes:

- **Seller axis (five sales stages):** contact, requirements, proposal, negotiation, close.
- **Buyer axis (seven procurement stages):** discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming ownership of responsibility, build-or-buy, solution and vendor selection, commitment and signing.

Put both axes on the same chart and use customer behavioral evidence to judge whether buyer and seller are synchronized.

## Negotiation Capability: Large Deals Are About Sustainability, Not a One-Time Price

By the time a computing-power contract reaches the negotiation table, what is being tested is the quality of preparation. Negotiation capability requires thinking through four things before you sit down: power balance, time pressure, information asymmetry, and each side's best alternative.

What is your alternative? The customer's? Who can less afford to wait? Concessions must be exchanged for conditions: lower price for longer service, larger volume, or earlier payment.

## Three Executable Actions for You

Watching someone else's contract is useless. Turning it into your own action is what matters.

**Action 1:** Take the largest opportunity in your pipeline and fill in PRTABCD item by item — known, unknown, information source, next question. Find the item where your information is thinnest and close that gap this week.

**Action 2:** Draw the dual-perspective position of this opportunity using the five sales stages and seven procurement stages. Write down three pieces of customer behavioral evidence and judge whether you are synchronized, ahead, or behind.

**Action 3:** Rewrite every technical description in your proposal into the customer's business numbers, so the customer's finance team can understand what this investment returns.

## FAQ

**Q1: What is PSS5?**

PSS5 is a systematic key-account sales enablement methodology developed by Xu Hanbo of Yanke, based on more than twenty years of key-account sales experience, and registered with the National Copyright Administration. Its full name is Professional Sales Five-Star Capability. It consists of five dimensions — value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation — each addressing one of five problem types: translating products into customer value, penetrating the decision chain, uncovering real needs, managing the sales process, and reaching sustainable agreements.

**Q2: What does a computing-power technical services contract teach key-account sales?**

The lesson is not in the contract itself but in the shape of the deal. Long-cycle, high-value contracts require the buyer to complete many decision tasks, and the seller must simultaneously possess value translation, decision-chain coverage, need discovery, process management, and negotiation preparation.

**Q3: How is value capability used specifically in computing-power sales?**

Translate service capability into business language the customer understands. Instead of saying how much computing power is provided, say how much business volume it supports, how much the model iteration cycle shortens, how much unit inference cost drops.

**Q4: What is PRTABCD and how is it used?**

PRTABCD is a scanning tool using seven decision information types to identify blind spots in customer intelligence. It belongs to relationship capability.

**Q5: What is the relationship between the five sales stages and the seven procurement stages?**

They are the dual-perspective funnel within PSS5 system capability. The five sales stages are the seller axis: contact, requirements, proposal, negotiation, close. The seven procurement stages are the buyer axis: discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming ownership of responsibility, build-or-buy, solution and vendor selection, commitment and signing. The two axes have a many-to-many relationship. Use customer behavioral evidence to judge synchronization and avoid skipping ahead or falling behind the customer's rhythm.

**Q6: How does SPIN questioning work in key-account sales?**

Advance the conversation through four question types: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff. Let the customer speak, not lecture.

**Q7: What preparation does negotiation capability include?**

Before sitting at the table, think through four things: power balance, time pressure, information asymmetry, and each side's best alternative.

**Q8: How can a small sales team with no large-deal experience start practicing PSS5?**

Start with one minimal action per dimension: rewrite one value proposition for your most important customer; use PRTABCD to fill an information blind spot for one opportunity; design three SPIN questions before your next visit; plot any opportunity on the dual-perspective funnel; write down your bottom line and alternative before negotiating.
