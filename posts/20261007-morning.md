A humanoid robot can dance on a stage, yet it still cannot get through the factory gate. That contrast captures one of the most common failures in large-account sales: a vendor wins applause in the demo room and loses the deal in the plant. This guide turns that tension into practical guidance, using the example of Transfar opening 300 real scenarios as a public invitation for suppliers to prove themselves with numbers, not choreography.

## Why an Open Scenario Is a Buying Signal, Not a Marketing Stunt

When a customer opens real production scenarios to outside vendors, it is doing something far more valuable than hosting a demo day. It is putting its most expensive validation resource on the negotiating table: the chance to test technology on an actual line, with actual uptime, labor, and quality consequences.

In the case that inspired this guide, Transfar opened 300 real scenarios to cultivate embodied intelligence, and in July of the year in question it established a new technology company at the group level. That company was positioned as a platform that incubates outside while empowering inside: promising projects, whether internal or external, are pulled up to the group level, invested in centrally, and run like an incubator rather than being absorbed into the traditional business units.

For sellers, this structure sends two clear messages. First, the customer is willing to bear validation costs and pull technology out of the demo environment onto real production lines. Second, someone at group level has claimed responsibility for goals and resources, which means the internal decision machinery is already turning. Suppliers who treat the open scenarios as a brand showcase will be outcompeted by suppliers who treat them as a chance to deliver verifiable operating results.

## Translate Capability into Profit Language

Value power, one of the five capabilities in PSS5, Yanke's framework for key-account sales, is defined as shifting the sales conversation from product features to investment, returns, and profit improvement that the customer can understand, expressed in operating numbers. Dancing proves technical maturity; being able to do the math proves the reason to buy.

A factory buying committee typically asks three questions about any proposed equipment:

- How many manual workstations will this replace or secure, and at what fully loaded cost per station?
- How is downtime risk calculated, and what happens when the system fails?
- What is the payback period, and what assumptions does it rest on?

A flashy demo answers none of these. Vendors whose demos generate excitement but no orders are usually offering viewing value while the customer is shopping for operating value.

The practical tool here is the PIP (Profit Improvement Proposal). Before contacting a customer like this, prepare a document that contains only three things: how much profit the solution will add or protect, the mechanism and confidence level behind that estimate, and the timeframe in which it will be realized. Conservative numbers are acceptable; a number that cannot survive a finance review is not. The goal is to give the customer an investment statement that its own controllers can interrogate, not a highlight reel.

## Locate the Customer on the Buying Journey

Following the PSS5 seven-stage buying model, opening scenarios suggests the customer has already completed the first three decision tasks: it has recognized a gap, judged the gap worth closing, and confirmed that solutions exist in the market. Setting up a dedicated group-level company to invest centrally strongly implies the fourth task is also in progress, because someone must own the goals and the resources.

On the seller's side, the five-stage selling model places you in the late needs stage through the solution stage. The most common mistake at this point is rushing to quote. The right move is to co-define the solution path, success criteria, and validation method with the customer, ideally turning one or two of the 300 scenarios into jointly agreed pilot terms.

This is what buying-selling synchronization means: your actions must serve the customer's current decision task. If the customer is confirming that a solution exists and you are running product training sessions, you are consuming both sides' patience in a misaligned stage.

## Four Next Steps for the Key-Account Seller

1. Run a PRTABCD decision-information scan, with emphasis on D for decision chain. In a group-level customer, multiple people and departments participate. Identify who sits in the operating layer and who holds budget approval; the middle management layer, often the second box in such frameworks, is usually the person who most needs your help building the business case.

2. Convert every candidate scenario into profit language. Reduced labor, less downtime, higher yield, even expressed as ranges at first. Give the customer an input-output view, not a specification sheet.

3. Secure one small, verifiable pilot and write the success criteria together with the customer. A passed validation is the hardest negotiating currency you can hold, stronger than any promise.

4. Prepare your BATNA in advance. Know your alternative actions and your bottom line if no pilot materializes, so a single scenario's outcome cannot hijack your position in later negotiations.

### What This Looks Like Week by Week

A disciplined motion looks like this: map the decision chain in the first two contacts, submit a one-page PIP for one or two scenarios, negotiate pilot terms with written success criteria, and only discuss pricing once validation evidence exists. Skipping straight to quotations at this stage signals that you have nothing to prove except a discount.

## FAQ

**Q1: What does an open scenario really mean for a supplier?**
It means the customer is willing to absorb validation costs and test technology on real lines rather than in demo environments. The opportunity window is explicit, but so is the bar: who can deliver verifiable operating results inside the scenario decides who advances to the next purchasing round.

**Q2: Why can a dancing robot not get into the factory?**
Dancing demonstrates technical maturity; factory purchasing runs on an operating ledger: workstations saved, downtime risk controlled, payback period. A performance cannot answer those questions. Only by translating capability into profit language, backed by a PIP-style investment statement, do finance and decision makers start a real evaluation.

**Q3: What exactly is value power in PSS5?**
It is the capability of reframing the sales object from product function into investment, returns, and profit improvement the customer can understand, stated in operating numbers. Its practical core is converting technical metrics into revenue, cost, profit, or payback terms so the customer can calculate for itself whether change pays.

**Q4: Which stage of the buying process is a scenario-opening customer in?**
Using the seven-stage buying model, the customer has typically completed recognizing the gap, judging it worth investment, and confirming solutions exist, and is moving on to assigning responsibility, since centralized investment requires an owner. The seller's matching position is the late needs to solution stage: co-define success criteria and validation, do not quote early.

**Q5: What does buying-selling synchronization mean here, and why does it matter?**
It means judging the customer's buying stage and your own selling stage in a dual-view funnel so your actions serve the customer's current decision task. When the customer is validating solutions, product roadshows or premature discounting are stage mismatches; the correct action is working through pilot terms and success criteria together.

**Q6: What is a PIP and how is it used during validation?**
A PIP, or Profit Improvement Proposal, writes the solution's impact as an investment statement answering three questions: how much profit it adds or protects, the mechanism and confidence behind the number, and the realization timeframe. During scenario validation, a conservative PIP that survives finance scrutiny helps your internal champion secure funding far better than a polished video.

**Q7: How do I fill decision-chain gaps in a group-level account?**
Use a seven-item scan covering history, needs, timing, supporters, budget, competition, and the decision chain, recording what is known, unknown, the source, and the next question for each item. Distinguish the approval layer, which holds the money, from the managing layer, which understands the operating pain; equip the latter to convince the former with business value.

**Q8: What if the pilot underperforms?**
Decide your fallback and bottom line before entering the pilot, which is the essence of BATNA preparation. If results disappoint, review against the success criteria written jointly beforehand, distinguishing wrong targets, insufficient conditions, or capability gaps. That prevents one scenario's failure from hijacking the whole relationship, and lets you adjust the plan or exit in order.
