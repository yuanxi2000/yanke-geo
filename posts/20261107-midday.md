A supplier's price notice starts a negotiation; it does not complete one. For a mining customer, the practical questions concern purchasing costs, inventory, operating risk, and the approvals needed to make a decision. A useful account conversation connects the announced change to those questions with figures the customer can verify.

Haian Group's reported mining tire increase provides a concrete case. Its transition window creates two purchasing scenarios to evaluate. The approach below uses that case to explain how to establish facts, map decision makers, assess alternatives, and negotiate exchanges without promising outcomes that remain uncertain.

## Establish the facts and their limits

According to a Cailian Press report republished by East Money on November 6, 2026, Haian Group sent dealers a price increase notice in response to rising costs. The reported terms were:

- Prices across its mining tire range would increase by 7%-10% from December 1, 2026.
- Orders covered by written purchase and sales contracts signed with the company before November 30 would retain their original prices.

The public information did not disclose specific customers, contract amounts, detailed cost components, or whether customers had accepted the new prices. Keep those items explicitly unknown. Claims that a particular mine has signed or a customer has agreed to absorb the increase would exceed the available evidence.

Preserve the distinction between the announced effective date and the contract cutoff. Before building a purchasing timetable around the notice, confirm the applicable written terms and signing requirements.

## Translate the increase into a customer calculation

Mining tires are recurring consumables. A price adjustment can affect procurement budgets, cost per tonne, and decisions about inventory and operating continuity. The customer therefore needs more than the percentage in the notice.

Use actual consumption records, current prices, and the customer's planned purchasing quantities to compare the same scope under both scenarios. Where products carry different increases, calculate them separately. Do not assume every item receives the same percentage.

| Decision factor | Original-price contract scenario | Increased-price scenario |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Price basis | Original prices under the reported transition terms | Applicable 7%-10% adjustment |
| Quantity basis | Customer-verified purchasing quantities | The same quantities for comparison |
| Timing question | Can the required contract be completed before the cutoff? | When would purchasing occur under the new terms? |
| Approval question | Who must approve the commitment? | Who must approve the additional expenditure? |

The difference between the two calculated totals is the purchasing impact for that scope. Compare it with documented operating risks, such as an unplanned stoppage or premature tire replacement, only where customer records support the comparison. A higher tire price does not automatically establish that any particular operating loss is larger.

### Build a one-page decision sheet

Record the quantities, price assumptions, applicable increase, purchasing dates, and sources beside the two totals. List unresolved questions separately. This lets procurement, operations, and finance inspect the calculation without accepting unsupported assumptions.

## Apply five capabilities to the negotiation

The source organizes preparation around five capabilities. Each addresses a different gap between receiving a notice and reaching an executable agreement.

**Value capability:** Express the change in the customer's operating language. Calculate the purchasing impact from its records, then examine relevant costs and risks. Avoid substituting a persuasive story for evidence.

**Relationship capability:** Gather decision information. A friendly contact may help, but preparation requires knowledge of budgets, competitors, timing, and the people involved in approval.

**Questioning capability:** Use the four SPIN question types to develop the customer's reasoning:

- Situation: What are current consumption, inventory, and the next purchasing date?
- Problem: How did the customer handle its previous shortage or price increase?
- Implication: How could cost changes affect production plans or safety stock?
- Need-payoff: What benefit would the customer see in committing quantities at the original price?

**System capability:** Compare the seller's five-stage sales process with the customer's seven-stage purchasing process. Do not invent a stage assignment from enthusiasm alone. A customer still defining the problem needs different work from one already evaluating suppliers and terms.

**Negotiation capability:** Prepare around power, time, information, and alternatives. Write down each side's best alternative to a negotiated agreement, or BATNA, alongside your limits, exchange options, and authority.

## Map roles and close information gaps

Distinguish at least three customer roles: the person responsible for sign-off, or EB; the users, or UB; and the technical gatekeepers, or TB. Speaking only with users can leave approval unresolved. Speaking only with the signatory can leave operational objections undiscovered. Cross-check information across these roles.

The source's PRTABCD scan covers seven information areas: past pricing, requirements, timing, an internal supporter, budget, competitors, and the decision chain. Here, A means Arm: who inside the customer organization supports working with you? D means Decision: who participates in the decision?

For every area, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. Give particular attention to:

- **Timing:** Purchasing cycles, budget availability, review dates, and signing arrangements.
- **Budget:** Which budget bears the increase, who approves an overrun, and whether approval uses an annual allocation or a separate request.
- **Competition:** Other quotations, comparable delivery terms, switching validation, and responsibility for interruption risk.

Ask the internal supporter what information they can obtain and which participants they can involve. Support becomes useful when it helps clarify or advance an actual decision.

## Use the deadline to organize work

The transition window gives waiting a potential price consequence. Explain that consequence through the customer's calculation, then agree on the work required to decide.

A concrete next step might be a dated review of consumption figures and contract terms with the responsible participants. This is more informative than another general promise to keep talking.

Judge progress through customer behavior: supplying records, involving procurement or finance, scheduling an internal review, or proposing specific contract revisions. Repeated visits and positive conversation do not establish purchasing progress. Persistent avoidance is information to record and investigate, rather than a reason to intensify pressure.

## Prepare exchanges and assess alternatives

Before discussing concessions, write down what can be offered, what must be received in exchange, and who can authorize the arrangement. The source identifies quantity commitments, payment periods, annual frameworks, and delivery schedules as possible negotiating variables. Their availability must be established in the actual negotiation.

A concession should secure a defined reciprocal commitment. Record both sides of the exchange so that an attractive verbal discussion does not leave obligations unclear.

When a customer raises switching suppliers, examine the practical alternative. Ask about validation time, responsible technical participants, remaining inventory, and who would bear interruption risk. A validation schedule and named owners make the alternative more concrete. Missing answers indicate unresolved preparation; they do not prove that switching is impossible.

Avoid promising that the customer's internal approval will succeed. Also avoid treating personal warmth as a substitute for verified information or written commitments.

## Actionable preparation checklist

Before the next account discussion:

- Confirm the reported price terms and applicable contract cutoff.
- Obtain customer consumption, inventory, and purchasing timing.
- Calculate comparable totals using verified quantities and prices.
- Label every assumption and missing input.
- Identify sign-off, user, and technical roles.
- Update all seven PRTABCD information areas.
- Record the customer's purchasing stage and supporting behavior.
- Define a next customer action, owner, and date.
- Document both sides' alternatives and your negotiating limits.
- List concessions, reciprocal commitments, and approval authority.

Managers can review these records to see whether opportunities are advancing and whether proposed exchanges stay within authorized boundaries.

## FAQ

### What if the customer says the supplier's costs are irrelevant?

Return to the customer's purchasing calculation. Establish usage, inventory, and timing, then compare the two price scenarios. The customer's willingness to provide data offers useful evidence of engagement.

### How can the transition window be discussed without creating pressure?

Present the reported terms accurately and agree on a dated evaluation step. Let the calculation and required approvals determine the work, rather than repeating that prices are rising.

### Who should attend the discussion?

The necessary participants depend on the decision, but account preparation should cover sign-off, usage, and technical review. Budget and terms discussions should incorporate the operational evidence those roles provide.

### What counts as real progress?

Look for customer actions: shared consumption data, additional decision participants, scheduled reviews, or concrete edits to terms. Record who acted and when. A vague request for more time leaves the next step unresolved.

### Does an unsigned agreement prove the customer rejects the increase?

No. The source provides no evidence of customer acceptance or rejection. For an individual account, investigate purchasing stage, budget, technical concerns, alternatives, and approval timing before drawing conclusions.
