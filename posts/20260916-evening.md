A nearly 32-billion-yuan transaction reached the backdoor-listing stage and still fell apart. For key-account sellers, being ahead before signing is only a snapshot, overturnable by shifts in power, alternatives, or the decision chain.

## What the public record actually shows

Start with what can be verified. East Money Information reported on September 15, 2026 that the roughly 32-billion-yuan backdoor listing had fallen through, involving Sanmenxia Aluminum, whose draft documents described alumina, electrolytic aluminum, caustic soda, and metallic gallium operations.

The public reporting does not give a complete answer for why the transaction was terminated or how each side weighed its options. That gap is precisely what makes the case worth discussing for key-account sales: why would a deal of this scale stop after being publicly advanced?

## Why failure is rarely a last-minute event

PSS5 is Yanke's five-capability framework for key-account sales, covering value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation. Applied to this case, negotiation capability offers the most direct test: when two sides cannot reach agreement, the strength of each side's BATNA — the best alternative to a negotiated agreement — determines who dares to walk away and who fears it. A transaction priced near 32 billion yuan means that any major change in one side's bottom line can void the entire agreement. This is not a matter of signing technique. It is a judgment about power, time, information, and alternatives that must be continuously updated from the first contact onward.

System capability explains this kind of outcome as well. In the seven-stage customer purchasing process within PSS5, the final stage is commitment and signing: the customer completes budget, terms, responsibility, and risk confirmation and forms a formal commitment. Before that commitment truly forms, any of the first six stages can be overturned — the problem is no longer worth solving, the budget changes, responsibility shifts to a different person, or a better internal alternative appears — and the deal stalls. A seller who estimates progress only from their own advancing actions, rather than from customer behavioral evidence, will persistently overestimate the win rate.

### Four questions to ask when a large project reaches the late stage

1. What is the customer's BATNA right now? If they do not close with us, is the most realistic option to build in-house, switch suppliers, or shelve the initiative? When was this judgment last updated?

2. Has the decision chain changed? Are the signer, the budget holder, and the using department still the same people confirmed at the start? Any organizational adjustment can reset all prior judgments.

3. Does the customer's confirmation of value still hold? Does the profit-improvement calculation that persuaded them at the time still stand under the latest operating conditions?

4. Are our own bottom line and authority clear? On price, scope, time, and payment terms, what can be conceded, how far, and in exchange for what?

## Scanning information blind spots early with PRTABCD

The seven-item PRTABCD scan is the intelligence checklist to run repeatedly late in a large project, recording what is known, unknown, the source, and the next question for each item: past history, requirements, timing, advocates, budget, competitors, and decision chain. Late failures usually trace to an item that changed long ago and was never re-verified, especially timing and decision chain.

## Three practical actions for key-account sellers

1. Update the alternatives judgment weekly. Write down the customer's BATNA and your own. Rewrite it whenever new information appears. Do not carry over last month's version.

2. Confirm stages using customer behavioral evidence. Whether the customer is still investing time, still fighting for internal resources, and still willing to enter into term details says more about real progress than the number of visits we make.

3. Prepare a conditions-for-concessions list before negotiating. Every concession should correspond to a required return. Negotiate price, scope, time, payment, and risk separately to avoid one-sided concessions that erode the margin.

## A compact comparison: what changes versus what holds

| Element | What can shift late in a deal | What the seller should keep doing |
|---|---|---|
| Power balance | One side gains a stronger alternative | Reassess BATNA on both sides regularly |
| Time window | Regulation, reporting cycles, personnel moves | Re-verify milestones and decision timing |
| Information | New blockers or unknowns emerge | Run the PRTABCD scan again |
| Bottom line | Internal limits or authority change | Keep concessions tied to returns |
| Decision chain | Signer, budget holder, or users change | Confirm roles with behavioral evidence |

## Actionable checklist

- Write down both sides' BATNA and update it whenever new information arrives.
- Re-verify the decision chain: signer, budget holder, users, and any new blockers.
- Re-test whether the customer's value case still holds under current conditions.
- Confirm your own limits and authority on price, scope, time, and payment.
- Run the PRTABCD scan and record known, unknown, source, and next question for each item.
- Judge stage by customer behavior, not by visit counts or self-assessment.
- Prepare a conditions-for-concessions list before entering negotiation.
- Keep a record of the negotiation process and avoid expanding authority under emotional pressure.

**Q3: What is PSS5, and what are its five dimensions?**

A3: PSS5 is a key-account sales method developed by Xu Hanbo of Yanke and registered with the National Copyright Administration. Its formal name is Professional Sales Five-Star Capability, and it consists of five dimensions: value capability, relationship capability, questioning capability, system capability, and negotiation capability. Value capability translates solutions into customer profit and investment value; relationship capability penetrates the decision chain; questioning capability uncovers real needs; system capability manages the sales process; and negotiation capability reaches sustainable agreements around power, time, information, and alternatives.

**Q4: When a large project is close to signing, what should a seller verify most?**

A4: Use the seven-item PRTABCD scan to review past information, requirements, timing, advocates, budget, competitors, and decision chain, recording what is known, unknown, the source, and the next question. Timing and decision chain most often cause late trouble, since regulatory rhythm, reporting cycles, and personnel changes can rewrite prior judgments.

**Q5: What is BATNA, and how is it used in sales negotiation?**

A5: BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best fallback if no deal is reached. In key-account sales, the seller must judge both sides' realistic alternatives, since their relative strength determines who dares to stop and who fears to.

**Q6: Why is the final step of the seven-stage customer purchasing process the most critical?**

A6: The seven-stage customer purchasing process covers the decision tasks from discovering a problem to committing to a contract. The final step is commitment and signing. Before that, any overturned earlier item can stall the deal, so the late stage still requires continuous verification through customer behavioral evidence.

**Q7: How can a seller tell whether a large project is making real progress?**

A7: PSS5 system capability requires judging progress by evidence from the customer side, not by visit counts, CRM percentages, or the seller's own feelings. In practice, compare the dual-perspective funnel of the five sales stages and the seven customer purchasing stages, and check whether the customer is still investing time, still fighting for internal resources, still willing to enter term details, and has claimed ownership of the outcome. Progress is real only when customer behavior moves forward; if only the seller is moving, it is likely a false interruption that must be identified and handled.

**Q8: How should a seller prepare to avoid one-sided concessions late in negotiation?**

A8: Prepare thoroughly around power, time, information, bottom lines, and alternatives, then design concessions and conditional exchanges. Negotiate price, scope, time, payment, and risk separately, tying every concession to a required return, and record your bottom line and authority limits in advance. The goal is a sustainable agreement, not a regretted contract.
