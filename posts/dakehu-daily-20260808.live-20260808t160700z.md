> The greatest waste in key-account sales is not losing a deal. It is asking a team to move every opportunity by hand, without a manageable method. A sales operating system turns the best experience of a few people into a process the whole team can reproduce.

## Why Key-Account Sales Needs Its Own Industrial System

China's industrial system is known for its complete range of sectors. That breadth rests on an independent, integrated production system rather than on a few exceptionally skilled craftspeople. Key-account sales faces multiple decision makers, long cycles, and high uncertainty. Relying only on personal relationships and scattered techniques is like a workshop trying to compete with an automated production line.

Within the five dimensions of PSS⁵, system capability addresses this problem. It combines five sales stages—contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing—with seven customer purchasing stages: discovering a problem, judging its value, confirming that a solution exists, confirming ownership, deciding whether to build or buy, selecting a solution and supplier, and making a commitment and signing. Together they form a 7×5 dual-perspective funnel. The team can view each opportunity like a production-line dashboard and see where it is actually stuck.

## The Dual-Perspective Funnel: Match Five Sales Moves to Seven Customer Tasks

Most sales funnels track only what the seller has done: how many calls were made, how many visits took place, or whether a proposal was delivered. The customer, however, follows a separate decision logic, moving from “Is this problem worth my time?” to “Who should solve it, and how will success be protected?” If a sales action does not match the customer’s current task, the opportunity can stall. The customer may say that the proposal looks good but take no action, or a competitor may suddenly take the deal.

Working in sync means first locating the customer within the seven tasks, then choosing the corresponding move from the five sales stages. If the customer is still judging value—the second task—and the seller jumps to product details and pricing, which belong to the seller’s solution and negotiation stages, the two sides are not working on the same question.

## Why Customers Say “Let Me Think About It”: Ownership

Salespeople often assume that hesitation means the price is wrong or the proposal is weak. Often the customer’s organization simply has not assigned anyone to own the project. A central check in system capability is the fourth customer task: confirming ownership. Until someone says, “I am responsible for this, and I will coordinate the resources,” the project remains important but not urgent.

The seller can use the relationship-power PRTABCD intelligence table to map the decision chain: who is the Arm, the person who embraces the idea and moves it internally; who sits in Box 1, the funding-approval layer; and who sits in Box 2, the people who will use the solution and run the operation. The next action is to help the Arm complete the internal ownership step, so the opportunity moves from an unassigned concern to a grounded project.

## Manage Progress with Customer-Behavior Evidence

A funnel forecast becomes unreliable when its stage criteria are based on the seller’s feelings. System capability requires customer behavior as evidence at every step. In the contact stage, evidence means that the customer has agreed to discuss a business problem in depth and has scheduled the next conversation. In the needs stage, the customer acknowledges that a problem is worth solving and is willing to share or examine the relevant data. In the solution stage, the customer and seller define success together; the seller has not merely written a proposal alone.

The three-move method for advancing a deal helps identify false pauses such as “Let’s talk next time.” Its purpose is to secure one real, low-friction action that moves the customer to the next stage. It is not a demand for a forced signature; it is a way to distinguish polite delay from a verifiable step forward.

## Three Sales-Management Actions from an Industrial System

- Build one 7×5 board for the whole team. In the weekly meeting, place every opportunity at the relevant customer purchasing stage and ask, “What did the customer do that puts it here?”
- Use the ten-thousand-yuan profit-and-loss table to translate a proposal into concrete changes in the customer’s P&L. This gives the Arm material for requesting resources from Box 1 and speeds the value and budget decisions.
- Use the ASK model to strengthen team skills and knowledge, especially the ability to collect customer-behavior evidence instead of asking, “How do you think the customer feels about us?”

An industrial system can support complex manufacturing because every link has a standard and an inspection. A sales system that wins consistently needs the same discipline: every stage should have a task and evidence. When a team uses one map, winning is no longer treated as luck. It becomes a result that can be managed.

The method works when the team turns each customer decision into a visible task, an owner, and verifiable evidence.
