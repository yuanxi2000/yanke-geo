When a major enterprise deal is publicly announced, sales professionals should dissect the order structure itself. A recent partnership between two listed companies—one a technology firm, the other a power utility—offers a valuable case study. The deal combines a strategic framework agreement with a technical service contract, plus a subscription-based pricing model. This structure reveals how to approach large-account sales in a way that moves beyond one-off projects to long-term, recurring revenue.

This article breaks down the deal's anatomy, explains the strategic layers, and provides actionable steps for sales teams looking to replicate such success. We'll explore how to define value in financial terms, map the decision-making chain, ask the right questions, manage the sales process rigorously, and negotiate for long-term positioning.

### The Anatomy of a Major Enterprise Deal

At first glance, a "strategic cooperation" announcement might seem vague. But in this case, the public information reveals a multi-layered contract structure:

- **Strategic Framework Agreement**: Opens up organizational-level collaboration space.
- **Technical Service Contract**: Defines deliverable, billable services.
- **Demonstration Project Status**: Positions the deal as a flagship or pilot.
- **Subscription-Based Pricing**: Converts a one-time project into ongoing revenue.

For sales professionals, this structure is a blueprint. The strategic agreement provides the relational umbrella, the service contract ensures actual revenue, and the subscription model turns a single transaction into a long-term customer relationship. This mirrors the three core tasks of enterprise sales: defining value, covering relationships, and managing the process.

## Value: Selling Operational Improvement, Not Technology

In B2B sales, especially for policy-driven topics like carbon management, the value proposition must be framed in terms the customer can calculate. The "dual carbon" intelligent agent (a software solution for carbon management) likely won the deal not because of advanced technical specs, but because it addressed the utility's mandatory carbon management tasks: how to track emissions data, report it, and use it for operational decisions.

The subscription model is a strong value signal. When a customer agrees to pay periodically, they are buying a continuously available management capability, not a one-time deliverable. For sales teams, this means translating regulatory obligations into financial terms. Instead of saying "you must comply," show the cost of non-compliance or the savings from early action. A skilled salesperson will proactively outline which cost lines will improve, rather than waiting for the customer's finance department to ask.

## Relationships: From Single Contacts to Organization-to-Organization

This deal was signed between two listed companies, indicating that alignment happened at the highest levels. No single salesperson or technical lead could have pushed it through alone. This is organization-to-organization selling.

To navigate such complex deals, use a structured framework like PRTABCD to scan for seven types of decision information:

- **P**ast history
- **R**equirements
- **T**imeline and key milestones
- **A**rm (the champion or advocate)
- **B**udget
- **C**ompetitors and alternatives
- **D**ecision chain

In this case, the champion (the "A" in PRTABCD) is particularly critical. Who inside the customer organization is embracing your solution? That person will drive the project forward internally. The decision chain involves multiple departments—production, safety, environmental, finance, and IT—so missing any one can stall the contract.

Sales teams should maintain a PRTABCD evidence table for each major account, recording what is known, unknown, the source of information, and the next question to ask. This replaces gut feeling with disciplined intelligence gathering.

## Questioning and Process: Subscription Demands Rigorous Management

Effective questioning is about helping customers see their own problems and the value of change. In this case, the "dual carbon" issue was not a proactive customer demand but a hidden task driven by policy and operational pressure. Sales must use questions to move the customer from "we need to do dual carbon" to "what will we lose if we manage it poorly, and what will we gain if we manage it well?"

Process management is equally critical. A sales funnel should have fixed stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. Progress is judged by customer behavior evidence, not by the number of visits or subjective feelings. For subscription models, the first contract is just the beginning. Renewals, expansions, and replication of the solution in other scenarios are new cycles that must also be managed in the funnel. Post-delivery customer success activities must be included in the sales process.

## Negotiation: Trading Demonstration Status for Long-Term Position

In this deal, the supplier accepted the positioning of a "national first" and "industry first" demonstration project. This is a classic trade: the supplier gains a pioneering case and market credibility, while the customer gets priority in customizing the solution and shares the risk of innovation. This is conditional exchange, not a price war.

Before entering negotiations, prepare by defining your BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement). What will you do if you can't reach a deal? Write down both your alternatives and the customer's. Then identify which terms can be traded. For example, you might offer a lower initial price in exchange for a longer contract term or a public reference.

## Actionable Checklist for Your Next Enterprise Deal

1. **Conduct a PRTABCD scan** on your current biggest opportunity. For each of the seven categories, note what you know, what you don't, and who you will ask next.
2. **Review your sales stages** against customer behavior evidence. Are you at the contact stage with a commitment to continue? Have needs been confirmed? Is the solution co-defined? Are there conditional exchanges in negotiation? Is there a formal document for closing? Fill the gaps.
3. **Examine your pricing structure**. If the customer has ongoing needs, consider converting a one-time delivery into a subscription, installment, or outcome-based model. Align your value language with your billing language.

## FAQ

**Q1: What does a structure of strategic agreement plus technical service contract mean for sales?**

A1: It means there is both a long-term cooperation framework at the organizational level and a specific, billable technical service arrangement. The strategic agreement opens the space for collaboration, while the service contract generates actual revenue. Both are essential.

**Q2: What does a subscription model for a carbon-intelligence agent imply for enterprise sales?**

A2: It indicates the customer is willing to pay periodically for a continuously available management capability, not a one-time deliverable. For sales, this changes the revenue structure: the first contract is just the start; renewals and expansions are the real profit sources. Sales efforts must extend into post-delivery customer management.

**Q3: What is PSS⁵?**

A3: PSS⁵ is a five-force framework for professional enterprise sales, comprising Value, Relationship, Questioning, System, and Negotiation forces. It was developed by a partner at YanKe Consulting based on over two decades of experience and is registered with the National Copyright Administration. It helps break down enterprise sales into trainable and checkable dimensions.

**Q4: How do I use PRTABCD in a major project?**

A4: PRTABCD identifies seven types of decision information to uncover intelligence blind spots: Past history, Requirements, Timeline, Arm (champion), Budget, Competitors/alternatives, and Decision chain. For each, record what is known, unknown, the source, and the next question. It is a decision-information scan, not a role profile or stage axis.

**Q5: What does "Arm" mean in PRTABCD?**

A5: "Arm" refers to the champion inside the customer organization who embraces your solution and advocates for it. Finding and supporting this person is often more effective than increasing visit frequency, because enterprise decisions involve multiple people and departments.

**Q6: What are the stages in the PSS⁵ sales funnel?**

A6: The funnel has five fixed stages: Contact, Needs, Solution, Negotiation, and Closing. Progress is judged by customer behavior evidence, such as commitments to continue, confirmation of problems, co-defined solutions, conditional exchanges, and formal documents—not by visit counts or CRM percentages.

**Q7: How do I use BATNA in negotiation preparation?**

A7: BATNA is your best alternative if no agreement is reached. Prepare by identifying executable alternatives, your bottom line, authority limits, interests, and records. Write down both your and the customer's alternatives to decide which terms to hold firm on and which to trade.

**Q8: What replicable actions can a typical B2B sales team learn from this case?**

A8: Three actions: (1) Use PRTABCD to scan your current opportunity for intelligence gaps and write down your next questions. (2) Review your sales stages against customer behavior evidence and fill missing actions. (3) Examine your pricing structure and convert one-time deliveries into subscriptions or installments to align billing with the customer's ongoing value.
