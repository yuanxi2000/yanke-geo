A positive clinical-trial headline can open a useful customer conversation. It cannot establish that a customer has a problem, sees sufficient value in addressing it, or intends to buy. For key-account sales teams, the practical task is to turn public information into questions that customers can answer and next steps they willingly support.

CSPC Pharmaceutical Group's reported positive Phase III topline results offer a useful case. The source describes prusogliptin tablets combined with dapagliflozin and metformin for type 2 diabetes. The primary endpoint was change from baseline in glycated hemoglobin, or HbA1c, at 24 weeks. Those facts provide a discussion boundary; they do not establish a commercial opportunity.

This guide uses five connected sales capabilities to show how to work within that boundary: value, relationships, questioning, process management, and negotiation.

## Establish the fact boundary first

Start with a short fact card containing only what the supplied source supports:

- Organization: CSPC Pharmaceutical Group.
- Indication: type 2 diabetes.
- Regimen: prusogliptin tablets with dapagliflozin and metformin.
- Development stage and reported result: Phase III, with positive topline results.
- Primary endpoint: change from baseline in HbA1c at 24 weeks.

The source does not attach a transaction counterparty, contract amount, or event date to this account. Leave those fields unfilled. Do not turn them into an implied purchase, scheduled transaction, or commercial commitment.

Likewise, preserve the distinction between a reported trial result and outcomes in routine clinical use. The supplied material does not provide numerical results sufficient to construct a clinical or financial benefit calculation. Keep the description at the level actually supported.

| Information | What it supports | What still needs evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive Phase III topline results | A reason to discuss the development | Relevance to a particular customer |
| HbA1c endpoint at 24 weeks | A defined topic for data interpretation | Connection to the customer's operating priorities |
| Customer participation in a follow-up discussion | A specific next step | Budget ownership and purchasing intent |

## Translate the endpoint into a customer value question

Value capability connects an offering with the customer's operating, investment, and profit considerations. Here, that means asking which business concern, if any, makes the endpoint relevant.

Possible discussion areas in the source include patient-management efficiency, treatment adherence, and product-portfolio competitiveness. Treat these as hypotheses to explore. The customer must identify the relevant concern and explain the connection.

Prepare a one-page value brief with three clearly separated parts:

1. Publicly reported trial facts.
2. Customer activities that might be affected.
3. Data and assumptions requiring customer confirmation.

Identify the information source for every entry. A customer hypothesis should be labeled as such; an unanswered question should remain unanswered. Exclude benefit estimates without supporting information, and avoid presenting an unconfirmed causal chain as an established business case.

The useful output is a clearer value question, such as: which existing operating measure would the customer examine to decide whether this development deserves further attention?

## Map missing decision information with PRTABCD

Relationship capability combines professional insight, decision information, and verifiable trust. A contact list alone does not explain how an organization reaches a decision.

Use the seven-part PRTABCD scan to organize what the team knows:

- P, past information: relevant account history and prior discussions.
- R, requirements: the needs the customer actually recognizes.
- T, timing: important milestones and decision windows.
- A, Arm: the person embracing or supporting your involvement.
- B, budget: available information about funding and its ownership.
- C, competition: competitors and alternative approaches.
- D, decision: the decision chain across people and departments.

For each category, record four things: known information, unknown information, its source, and the next question. Assign someone to resolve each important gap.

A and D deserve particular care. A identifies who supports your involvement; it does not automatically establish purchasing authority. D describes a chain involving multiple participants, rather than a single decision-maker. Job titles are starting points for questions, not proof of decision behavior.

Useful follow-up questions include who is initiating the internal discussion, who interprets the data for business colleagues, and who defines the budget assumptions.

## Use SPIN to make the conversation specific

Questioning capability helps the customer describe a problem and assess the value of change. SPIN provides four types of questions: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff.

### Move from the current workflow to a desired improvement

Begin with the situation: how does the current patient-management process work, and who reviews the relevant data? Then explore problems: where does the existing approach encounter difficulty?

Ask about implications only after a difficulty has been identified. What does that difficulty mean for follow-up, medication management, or cost? Finally, ask a need-payoff question: if the process improved, which result would the customer want to see first?

These are discovery questions, not claims that the trial regimen will resolve the customer's difficulties. Keep that distinction visible throughout the discussion.

The first conversation does not need to prove the entire value proposition. It should identify a concrete issue the customer is willing to examine further. If the answer remains unclear, record the customer's own wording and revisit it. Do not replace uncertainty with a polished conclusion.

## Track seller activity and buyer progress separately

Process management uses two perspectives. The seller's five stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. The customer's seven purchasing stages are:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing value.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Confirming responsibility for the issue.
5. Deciding between building internally and buying externally.
6. Selecting solutions and suppliers.
7. Committing and signing.

These perspectives have a many-to-many relationship. A meeting does not move the customer forward automatically, and repeated visits cannot substitute for evidence of a decision.

The clinical headline may provide an opening to discuss value or whether a solution exists. It does not justify advancing an opportunity to proposal, negotiation, or close.

Record observable customer behavior: arranging a data-interpretation meeting, involving the department that would use a solution, sharing current workflow data, or bringing the budget owner into the discussion. Interpret each action according to what it demonstrates. Participation in one meeting does not establish every later purchasing stage.

The next sales action should help the customer complete its current decision task.

## Gather negotiation information before designing concessions

Negotiation capability considers power, time, information, and alternatives. When those elements are unclear, a headline offers little basis for choosing prices, discounts, or payment conditions.

Before designing concessions, establish the customer's requirement scope, who defines the budget assumptions, the relevant timetable, and available alternatives. These questions also reveal whether there is a sufficiently defined commercial discussion to negotiate.

Once the information is available, the team can consider concessions and reciprocal commitments. Until then, focus on resolving the gaps rather than treating news interest as bargaining power.

## Run an actionable team checklist

Use the development as a bounded account exercise. A manager can review the following outputs together:

- [ ] Create a fact card using only supported trial information; leave unprovided transaction fields empty.
- [ ] Complete all seven PRTABCD categories with knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions.
- [ ] Assign an owner to each important information gap.
- [ ] Draft three customer-specific SPIN questions covering the current situation, implications, and desired improvement; use problem questions to clarify any difficulty identified.
- [ ] Record the seller's stage and the customer's stage separately.
- [ ] Attach customer-behavior evidence to each stage assessment.
- [ ] Agree on one customer-supported next step, such as a data discussion, workflow interview, or budget clarification.

Apply four review gates: facts are traceable, key participant information is sufficiently clear, questions address a concrete customer situation, and stage assessments have behavioral evidence. If a gate fails, return to discovery before preparing a proposal.

## FAQ

### Does a positive topline result count as a purchase signal?

No. It is reported research information. Purchasing progress requires customer evidence about the problem, value, responsibility, selection, and commitment. The headline alone supplies none of those decisions.

### Can the team estimate customer savings from the endpoint?

The supplied material does not support a savings calculation. First establish the relevant operating measure, obtain supporting customer information, and confirm the proposed connection. Leave unsupported estimates out of the value brief.

### What if an enthusiastic contact cannot explain the decision chain?

Record the enthusiasm and the missing information separately. Use the relationship scan to identify other participants, budget responsibility, and unresolved decision questions. Support from one contact does not complete the account picture.

### What makes a useful next commitment?

Choose a specific action the customer agrees to undertake. A data-interpretation meeting, workflow interview, or clarification of budget ownership can provide evidence for the next assessment. Record what the action is intended to clarify.
