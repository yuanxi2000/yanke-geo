A large contract feels like a win. It is not revenue yet. The announcement of a major blade supply agreement is a useful reminder for anyone selling to key accounts: a signed contract creates a project backlog, and that backlog turns into recognized revenue only as the customer's installation schedule advances. The gap between signature and realization is a second battlefield, and it is where value selling either holds up or falls apart.

## Why Contract Backlog Is Not Revenue

When a customer signs a large supply agreement, the seller's pipeline looks healthier. But in capital-intensive industries such as wind power, revenue recognition depends on the customer's build-out pace. The seller does not control turbine installation timelines. The customer does. The contract may be worth a substantial figure, but the cash and the booked revenue arrive in installments tied to delivery, acceptance, and grid-connection milestones.

This is a general pattern, not a one-company issue. Any long-cycle B2B sale where the customer's own operations determine when the product is consumed will show the same separation between signature and cash. Treating the signature as the finish line distorts forecasts, strains production planning, and weakens collection discipline.

## Where Value Selling Operates

Value selling is often described as a skillset for winning the deal. In practice, its more demanding work happens after the deal is won. The five-dimension view of key-account capability places value at the center, supported by relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation capabilities. Value, in this framework, means converting the sales conversation from product features into an investment the customer can read on their own books: revenue, cost, profit improvement, payback period, or opportunity cost.

Wind turbine blades illustrate the point. Blades are one of the highest-value components in a turbine's cost structure. That share of total cost is itself a value argument: the more a component weighs in the customer's cost base, the more procurement attention it receives. Whether a supplier wins a large contract often comes down to how they talk about the product. Describing airfoil profiles and specific strength speaks to engineers. Describing cost per megawatt, delivery scheduling, and the effect on grid-connection timing speaks to the customer's ledger.

### Monetization as a Daily Discipline

Monetization is the operational form of value selling. It has three steps: identify the customer's operating areas the solution can affect, quantify the improvement, and translate the result into financial language such as revenue, cost, profit, payback, or opportunity cost. In the blade business, the affected areas include turbine cost, delivery scheduling, and installation progress. Each can be converted into a change on the manufacturer's books. When the numbers are not available, the answer is not to guess. It is to return to questioning capability and design questions that surface the data.

### Structuring a Profit Improvement Proposal

A profit improvement proposal, or PIP, is a written case that frames the solution as an investment decision the customer can approve. Three questions give it structure:

- How much profit can this add or protect? This establishes the magnitude of the investment.
- What is the evidence and the mechanism? This addresses credibility.
- Over what time frame will it be realized? This addresses the realization rhythm.

A quotation answers price. A PIP answers why the investment is worth making, how it will be delivered, and when the customer will see the effect. For long-cycle businesses where revenue recognition depends on customer progress, the time-frame question is not optional.

## The Role of Customer Behavioral Evidence

The final stage of a typical sales process is closing, which includes formal commitment, signing, and handover to implementation. On the customer's side, commitment and signing is only one step in a longer procurement process. Execution after signing determines when revenue is booked. Mapping the seller's stages against the customer's procurement stages on a single view, and judging progress by customer behavioral evidence rather than seller optimism, is how the two sides stay in sync.

Adequate backlog does not guarantee timely revenue. Installation plans sit with the customer. So the scanning work after signing cannot stop. A structured decision-information review helps here. Two items deserve the most attention:

- **Time and key milestones.** When are the customer's installation plan, grid-connection milestone, and settlement milestone set?
- **Decision chain.** This is a multi-person, multi-department structure. Who controls scheduling, who controls acceptance, and who controls payment?

Each item should be logged with what is known, what is unknown, the source of information, and the next question to ask. The result is a checkable tracking sheet for realization rhythm rather than a vague sense that things are progressing.

## Negotiating the Rhythm

Rhythm risk can be placed on the negotiating table. When the customer's schedule shifts, it affects the seller's revenue and capacity planning. That is a variable that can be handled in contract terms. Instead of arguing only about unit price, the parties can design conditional exchanges around delivery batches, milestone alignment, price adjustment mechanisms, and payment terms.

Negotiation capability in this framework emphasizes reaching sustainable agreements based on a clear understanding of power, time, information, bottom lines, and alternatives. Writing rhythm into the agreement is more effective than chasing progress after the fact. The best alternative to a negotiated agreement, or BATNA, is part of this preparation. In long-cycle supply negotiations, alternatives are not limited to switching suppliers. They can include adjusting delivery batches or phasing deliveries. Making the alternative explicit clarifies where concessions are possible and which conditions cannot be relaxed.

## A Practical Comparison

| Element | Before Signing | After Signing |
|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | Winning the commitment | Realizing the value |
| Value language | Features and specifications | Customer investment, profit, payback |
| Key evidence | Proposal and pricing | Customer behavioral milestones |
| Information tracked | Decision criteria and stakeholders | Time milestones and decision chain |
| Main risk | Losing the deal | Revenue timing slipping |
| Negotiation lever | Price and scope | Delivery batches, milestones, payment terms |

## Actionable Checklist

- Confirm which of the customer's operating areas your solution affects, and quantify each one.
- Translate quantified improvements into financial language: revenue, cost, profit, payback, or opportunity cost.
- Draft a one-page PIP that answers the three questions: profit magnitude, evidence and mechanism, and time frame.
- Map your sales stages against the customer's procurement stages on one view.
- Track progress using customer behavioral evidence rather than internal optimism.
- Maintain a decision-information log covering time milestones and the decision chain.
- For each tracked item, record what is known, what is unknown, the source, and the next question.
- Prepare your BATNA before entering negotiation, including delivery-batch and phasing alternatives.
- Write rhythm-related terms into the agreement: delivery batches, milestone alignment, price adjustment, and payment mechanisms.
- Revisit the PIP and tracking sheet at each milestone to confirm the realization rhythm is holding.

## FAQ

**What does a large blade contract have to do with B2B sales in other industries?**

The pattern is general. A large signed contract creates backlog, and actual revenue recognition depends on the customer's execution progress. Treating signature as the endpoint distorts forecasts. Incorporating realization rhythm into daily tracking gives production planning and collections a firmer basis.

**What does value capability mean in this five-dimension framework?**

It means converting the sales object from product features into an investment, return, and profit improvement the customer can understand, and explaining the value using business numbers. In practice, this means talking less about parameters and more about the customer's ledger: which revenue, costs, and profits are affected, by how much, and when.

**How does a PIP differ from a quotation?**

A PIP frames the improvement as an investment case answering three questions: how much profit can be added or protected, what the evidence and mechanism are, and over what time frame the result will appear. A quotation only answers price. For long-cycle businesses, the time-frame question must be explicit, or the signature remains only a book milestone.

**How does monetization work day to day?**

Identify the customer's operating areas your solution affects, quantify the improvement with the customer, and convert it into financial language. Where numbers are unavailable, return to questioning capability and design questions to surface the data, then rewrite the value proposition with numbers.

**What information should be tracked after signing?**

Use a structured decision-information review. Focus on two items: time and key milestones (installation plan, grid-connection milestone, settlement milestone) and the decision chain (who controls scheduling, acceptance, and payment). Log knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions for each item.

**What can be done in negotiation when revenue depends on customer progress?**

Put rhythm risk on the table and design conditional exchanges around delivery batches, milestone alignment, price adjustment, and payment mechanisms, rather than negotiating only on unit price. Prepare BATNA, bottom lines, authority, interests, and records in advance. In long-cycle supply scenarios, alternatives can include adjusting delivery batches or phasing deliveries.

**How should BATNA be prepared for long-cycle supply negotiations?**

Clarify the best alternative action available if no agreement is reached. In long-cycle supply, this may include reworking delivery batches or phased delivery rather than switching suppliers. Making it explicit shows where concessions are possible and which conditions cannot be relaxed.

**Where should someone start to strengthen value capability?**

Read dedicated material on value capability, then practice with a live deal: list the customer's operating areas the solution affects, convert each into financial language, and write a one-page PIP. Then use the decision-information review to fill in the customer's time milestones and decision chain, and check the realization rhythm against customer behavioral evidence. A value proposition only produces revenue after signing when it becomes a follow-up action.
