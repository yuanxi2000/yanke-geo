AI content announcements can sound like a technology story. For key-account sales, they are more useful as an operating story. This guide adapts one public case: China Literature Group announced an AI premium video matrix and plans to release more than 20 IP-adapted games over three years. The first animation projects combine web-novel IP, AI technology and professional animation capability in a three-party collaboration. The sales implication is narrow but practical: when a customer expands content capacity, the buying rationale must come from the operating account, not from technical vocabulary. The person who can convert AI investment into project volume, cadence and risk gets into the solution discussion.

## Separate confirmed facts from inference

Only part of this case is public. The confirmed points are the AI premium video matrix, the three-year plan for more than 20 IP-adapted games, and the three-party collaboration model for the first animation projects. Budget scale, decision chain, existing suppliers and acceptance standards are not disclosed. They are interview questions, not proposal premises.

A common error is to write the proposal as if the unknowns were settled. A safer method is to use the confirmed facts as anchors and keep an explicit question list beside them. The public announcement tells you the customer is discussing content capacity. It does not tell you who signs, which budget line pays, how success will be judged, or whether the customer will build, buy or use a mixed model.

## Turn AI capability into the customer's operating language

Technical claims tend to be heard as cost items: model capability, generation speed, toolchain. Operating claims are heard as investment and return: how many IP projects the same team can advance, whether the time from greenlight to launch can be compressed, which genres can be tested at small cost, and who owns compliance, quality consistency and rework.

The sales task is translation, not invention. The customer must provide the baseline. The seller should not write conclusions on the customer's behalf. Four lenses are useful:

- Capacity: how many IP projects can run in parallel in a year, and whether the bottleneck is people, compute or review. The customer must confirm.
- Cost: how the cost structure for art, animation and voice changes per project, and which part AI actually covers. The customer must confirm.
- Cadence: which milestones decide the path from greenlight to launch, and which can be validated earlier. The customer must confirm.
- Risk: who owns genre compliance, quality consistency and rework. The customer must confirm.

A compact comparison makes the difference visible:

| Decision topic | Technical pitch | Operating-account pitch |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | Model features, generation speed and toolchain | Same team can advance how many IP projects, and on what cadence |
| Evidence | Seller claims about capability | Customer baseline on capacity, cost, cadence and risk |
| Customer action | Ask for a demonstration | Supply baseline numbers and name who benefits |
| Risk | Treated as a tool or model issue | Compliance, quality consistency and rework ownership are named |
| Negotiation | Price comparison | Scope, schedule, acceptance and responsibility trade-offs |

### Build a one-page fact-and-question sheet

Before the next meeting, put confirmed facts on the left and open questions on the right. For each open item, record the source of current information, the date, and the next question. In the meeting, convert each unknown into a question rather than a slide assertion. Do not let a proposal claim a budget owner, an incumbent supplier or an acceptance standard that the customer has not confirmed.

## Map the account before you demonstrate

When a customer organization spans literary IP, animation and games, one contact rarely covers the decision chain. The PRTABCD scan uses seven categories of decision information: Past, Requirement, Time, Arm, Budget, Competition and alternatives, and Decision chain. For each category, write what is known, what is unknown, where the information came from, and what to ask next.

In this case, Requirement can be written as expanding IP adaptation capacity. Time can be written as a three-year schedule. Several other categories remain blank. A blank is not a failure; treating a blank as known is the failure. Arm means the person or business line that is embracing you, not a job title on an approval form. Write down how far that support goes, what evidence supports it, and what question would test it.

## Ask four kinds of questions to turn intent into numbers

A senior customer saying 'we also need to do AI' is an attitude, not a requirement. Four question types can move it toward a calculable account: situation, problem, implication, and need payoff.

- Situation: How many IP-adapted projects are initiated in a year, and where do they most often get stuck?
- Problem: Which genres have been dropped because of cost or cycle time?
- Implication: If one project slips by a quarter, what does that mean for scheduling and revenue recognition?
- Need payoff: If early validation cost falls and more projects can advance, who benefits?

If the answers are adjectives, the account is not ready for a proposal. If the answers include numbers and named owners, the proposal has a landing point. The distinction is not rhetorical. It tells you whether the customer has completed value judgment or is still expressing interest.

## Use behavioral evidence to place the customer in the buying process

The purchasing seven-stage view and the sales five-stage view form a dual-view funnel. The two axes are many-to-many, not mechanically aligned. A public announcement that a direction has been set suggests that problem finding and value judgment are likely complete. It does not prove that the customer has confirmed a solution, assigned responsibility, decided whether to build or buy, selected a vendor, or reached commitment.

Place the account by behavior, not by meeting count. Four signals are more reliable: the customer gives budget language, arranges a cross-department review, states acceptance criteria, or allows you to meet the user side. Without such evidence, the project remains at its current stage. A sales leader should not accept a visit count or a CRM percentage as proof of progress.

## Negotiate scope, schedule and responsibility, not only price

Content customers often buy across business units, so negotiation tends to focus on scope, schedule, acceptance and responsibility. Prepare power, time, information, bottom line and alternatives before discussing price. BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best action each side can take if no deal is reached. A useful preparation records executable alternatives, bottom-line limits, authority, interests and the decision record.

The customer also has alternatives. Conditions can be exchanged only when both sides understand what happens without agreement. If your alternatives are unclear, concessions tend to fall on price alone. If they are clear, you can trade scope, timing, acceptance criteria and responsibility boundaries.

## Action checklist for the next account conversation

- Create a one-page sheet with confirmed facts on the left and open questions on the right; check it before the meeting.
- Schedule a question-led conversation before any demonstration, and use the four question types to obtain baseline numbers.
- Complete a PRTABCD scan for the account, including source and date for every known item and a next question for every blank.
- Identify the embracer and rate the evidence of support; do not treat politeness as endorsement.
- Check buying-stage evidence: budget language, cross-department review, acceptance criteria and access to users.
- Prepare BATNA notes, bottom line, authority and possible condition trades before negotiation.
- Mark the account as a multi-line decision project and assign someone who can understand IP, production and distribution language.
- Do not convert public facts into proposal assumptions about budget, decision rights, incumbent suppliers or acceptance.

## FAQ

**What can sales use directly from the announcement?**
Use only the confirmed public points: the AI premium video matrix, the plan for more than 20 IP-adapted games over three years, and the three-party collaboration for the first animation projects. Budget, decision makers and incumbent suppliers are not public and belong in a question list.

**Why can't 'we want to do AI' go straight into a proposal?**
It is an attitude, not a requirement. Use questions to replace it with numbers: annual project volume, bottlenecks, and the impact of a delayed launch. Without numbers, a proposal usually becomes comparison material.

**What does the A in PRTABCD mean?**
A is Arm, or embracer. It answers who in the customer organization is embracing you. It is not a bargaining chip or a job-title label. Record the level of support, the source of that information, and the next question.

**If the customer has announced a direction publicly, is needs confirmation still necessary?**
Yes. A public announcement covers problem finding and value judgment at most. Solution confirmation, responsibility, build-versus-buy, selection and contracting still require customer behavior evidence. Follow the customer's current decision task, not your own process calendar.

**How is value capability different from a quote?**
A quote says what you sell and for how much. Value capability says what changes in the customer's operations after the investment, using investment, return and profit improvement. The customer still supplies the baseline numbers.

**How should a team handle conflicting information from multiple business units?**
Map each item into the seven PRTABCD categories and note its source and time. Do not force early agreement. Treat conflicts as questions for the next round. IP, production and distribution groups may see the same project differently.
