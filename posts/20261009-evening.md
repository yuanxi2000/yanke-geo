A sports brand signing a multi-year marketing partnership with a league may not look like sales training material. But one word carries the lesson: "multi-year." When a buyer commits resources across several years, it's making one of the hardest and most valuable decisions in key-account sales. The deal isn't won by improvising at the table. It's won by arranging five negotiation levers — power, time, information, bottom line, and alternatives — long before the conversation begins. This guide uses that headline as a clean discussion sample. It does not guess at the terms, amounts, or internal dynamics of any specific deal. Instead, it treats "multi-year" as a contract form and asks what you can verify inside your own accounts.

## What "Multi-Year" Changes in a Negotiation

The PSS5 system view places the customer's seven-stage purchasing process and the seller's five-stage sales process on the same map. The customer's seven stages are: discovering a problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming responsibility ownership, deciding whether to build or buy, selecting a solution and supplier, and committing and signing. Commitment and signing is stage seven. A multi-year commitment only becomes discussable after the buyer has answered the earlier six questions in a way that survives internal scrutiny. The corresponding sales five stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. Negotiation is stage four; closing is only the handoff action in stage five. The weight of a multi-year agreement is that it tests all the prior work at once during negotiation. Multi-year contracts give sellers predictable revenue but raise buyer switching costs. Such deals aren't signed from one presentation; buyers verify stability and delivery. Ask yourself: how much flexibility does this promise take away, and is there an exit path?

## How the Five Negotiation Levers Work in a Multi-Year Deal

PSS5 negotiation power is defined as designing concessions and conditional exchanges on the basis of clearly identified power, time, information, bottom line, and alternatives, in order to pursue a sustainable agreement. In a multi-year cooperation, each lever has a question to answer.

### Power, Time, Information, Bottom Line, Alternatives

- **Power: who depends on whom more.** Before signing, the buyer usually has several candidates; after signing, the two sides depend on each other. Clarify what leverage you hold before signing and after performance begins. Do not trade a weak pre-signing position for a contract structure that leaves you more passive during delivery.
- **Time: multi-year terms turn time into a bargaining chip.** If you commit to a longer service period, exchange it for a longer purchasing period or a larger share from the other side. A concession on time should never be given away for free.
- **Information: how much do you know about the other side's operating plans, budget rhythm, and expansion or contraction intentions over the next few years?** Long-cycle cooperation amplifies information asymmetry. Signals missed today become risks during performance.
- **Bottom line: how prices adjust, where the service scope boundary sits, and how early exit is handled.** Write down what can be conceded and what cannot before sitting at the table, then check each item during negotiation. This prevents the atmosphere of the room from pushing you past your own limits.
- **Alternatives: BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement — the best fallback action available if the two sides cannot reach agreement.** Preparation requires clarifying executable alternatives, bottom lines, authority, interests, and records. The more concrete your alternative, the more confidently you can push multi-year terms forward.

Before these five levers are complete, do not rush the multi-year clause onto the table. Even the most elegant clause design will only postpone conflict into the performance period if the levers are empty.

## Fill Seven Intelligence Gaps Before You Sit Down

A multi-year agreement has a far larger intelligence gap than a single order. The PRTABCD seven-item scan is designed to close it. For each item, write down what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question. The seven items are:

- **P — Past/historical information:** have the two sides cooperated or competed before, and what does the other side's history of supplier changes indicate?
- **R — Requirements:** what are the other side's operating goals for the coming years, not just this year's purchasing list?
- **T — Time and key milestones:** where are the other side's budget cycle, decision window, and renewal or renegotiation points?
- **A — Adopters:** who inside the customer organization is embracing you and willing to endorse multi-year cooperation, and what is the boundary of that person's influence?
- **B — Budget:** who controls cross-year money, how is it approved, and has it been cut before?
- **C — Competitors and alternatives:** what other partners can the other side choose, including the possibility of building an internal team?
- **D — Decision chain:** a multi-year agreement touches many people and departments; what do business, finance, and legal each care about, and who holds a veto?

The output of the scan is not just a document. It is a question list: unknown items become questions for the next visit. The more complete the intelligence, the further the clauses can go.

## Three Actions for Your Own Accounts This Week

1. **Layer customers into single orders, annual frameworks, and multi-year agreements.** Count how many top accounts are stuck at the first layer.
2. **Pick one worth upgrading.** Use PRTABCD to write down knowns and unknowns, prioritizing T (time and key milestones) and D (decision chain).
3. **Prepare a concession list before your next quote.** Mark each concession with the condition you want in return, and write both sides' alternatives.

Back to the headline: we can't see clause details, but "multi-year" shows that most of the outcome is decided before anyone sits at the table. Arrange the five levers, complete the seven intelligence items, and write the concession list in detail; only then can your key account turn a single deal into cooperation that continues for years.

## FAQ

**Q1: What is PSS5?** PSS5 is Yanke's five-capability framework for key-account sales, used in training, assessment, and coaching: value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation. This article uses negotiation as its main axis.

**Q2: Why study a sports partnership headline?** "Multi-year" is universal. Most B2B orders settle by project or year. A multi-year commitment indicates the buyer has reached the commitment stage and will bear switching costs. The sample avoids confidential information.

**Q3: How does a multi-year deal differ from a single order?** A single order negotiates price, scope, and delivery. A multi-year agreement also negotiates annual scope, price adjustments, service standards, and early exit. Any blank becomes a dispute.

**Q4: How do you use BATNA?** Write your executable alternative, estimate the other side's, then set your bottom line, confirm authority, and prioritize which clauses must be held.

**Q5: How does PRTABCD help?** It records knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions across past information, requirements, time, adopters, budget, competitors, and decision chain. Long cycles make any blank dangerous, especially in the decision chain.

**Q6: How do the customer's seven stages and seller's five stages relate?** They are many-to-many. Judge progress by customer behavioral evidence, not visit counts. Confirm each side's stage before negotiating.

**Q7: Does this apply to smaller suppliers?** Yes, even more. Use a profit improvement plan to show how much profit can be increased or preserved, the mechanism, and the time frame.

**Q8: What is the first step to upgrade an existing customer?** Layer customers, fill intelligence with PRTABCD (focusing on T and C), then prepare a concession list before the next quote.
