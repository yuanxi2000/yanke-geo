Large customer investments rarely begin when a store opens or a logistics center goes live. In key-account sales, those visible events are usually the point at which much of the buying work is already complete. The source article uses lululemon's Wuhan logistics center and store openings or refreshes in several cities to make this point: the launch is the result of a chain of purchases spanning site selection, renovation, equipment, warehousing, systems, and distribution. A salesperson who waits for the announcement often arrives when the remaining conversation is maintenance buying rather than requirement shaping.

## Treat Customer Actions as Outcomes, Not Starting Guns

A store opening follows site evaluation, lease negotiation, renovation, equipment purchase, and hiring. A logistics center follows site planning, racking and sorting equipment, transport contracts, and systems integration. These earlier actions are where a salesperson can still influence requirements, evaluation criteria, and responsibility. The practical discipline is to read the customer's investment as a stage signal: when the customer moves, your next action should match the stage the customer is actually in.

## Separate Seven Procurement Tasks from Five Sales Stages

The customer's purchase can be broken into seven decision tasks:
- Problem discovery
- Value judgment
- Confirmation that a solution exists
- Confirmation of ownership and responsibility
- Build-versus-buy decision
- Solution and supplier selection
- Commitment and signing

The sales side has five stages:
- Contact
- Needs
- Solution
- Negotiation
- Close

The two axes are many-to-many. One sales stage can meet more than one procurement task, and the customer's position is determined by behavior, not by the salesperson's enthusiasm. The source describes a 7×5 coordinate table; its purpose is to mark each cell as synchronized, leading, or lagging. That is more useful than asking whether a deal feels warm.

### A Compact Evidence-to-Stage Comparison

| Customer evidence | More likely procurement task | Sales stage to align | Practical response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shares internal data such as order volume, sales per square foot, or fulfillment time | Value judgment | Needs | Quantify the gap in the customer's profit and investment terms; do not rush to a solution |
| Asks about delivery cycle, acceptance standards, or exception handling | Solution and supplier selection | Solution | Clarify evaluation criteria, scope, acceptance, and risk |
| Invites you into an internal meeting or connects you to a business owner | Confirmation of ownership | Needs or solution | Identify the owner, resources, and decision chain; bring verifiable evidence |
| Repeatedly pushes price without discussing scope or time | Still before confirming a solution | Needs, not negotiation | Ask questions to surface the problem and value; avoid unilateral concessions |
| Says it may build the capability in-house | Build-versus-buy decision | Solution or negotiation | Compare capability, time, risk, and total cost on one basis |

## Use PSS5 to Divide the Work

PSS5 separates key-account selling into five capabilities, and they do not do the same job in one opportunity:
- Value capability translates store or logistics spending into the customer's profit and investment language. It answers what the money returns.
- Relationship capability maps the decision chain: who is embracing you, who is delaying, and what information is still missing.
- Questioning capability uses four question types in sequence: situation, problem, impact, and need-payoff. The goal is for the customer to state the gap.
- Systems capability uses a dual-view funnel to locate the customer's stage and decide what evidence the next visit must bring back.
- Negotiation capability trades scope, time, payment, and risk together. It does not concede price in isolation.

## Turn Information Gaps into a Working Table

PRTABCD scans seven categories of decision information: past, need, timing, advocate, budget, competition, and decision chain. Record four columns for each: what is known, what is missing, where the information will come from, and the next question to ask. If a row cannot be filled in, that row becomes the task for the next customer conversation. This turns notes into a working intelligence plan rather than a diary of visits.

## A Practical Weekly Checklist

- [ ] Re-rank active opportunities by customer behavior evidence. Downgrade any advance that has no evidence behind it.
- [ ] Add one gap record to each project: identify whether the unclear item is past, budget, decision chain, or another PRTABCD category.
- [ ] For a customer expanding stores, ask how the next batch of sites will be selected and who will accept fulfillment requirements.
- [ ] Put the evidence you need from the next meeting on the calendar. Do not count visits as progress.

## FAQ

### Is it too late after a customer announces a store or logistics center?

Not automatically. First locate the customer within the seven procurement tasks. Opening and launch are results; site selection, renovation, equipment, and systems integration happen earlier. If the customer is already selecting suppliers, you may still influence evaluation criteria and acceptance terms. If the customer is still judging value or confirming ownership, use operating numbers to help clarify the gap. The answer depends on behavioral evidence, not the announcement date.

### How is the dual-view funnel different from a normal sales funnel?

The five sales stages describe your own actions. The seven procurement tasks describe decisions the customer must complete. The relationship is many-to-many, so one sales stage may correspond to two different customer tasks. Judge position by what the customer does: shares data, brings you into internal meetings, or asks about acceptance standards. Counting visits can mistake your activity for customer progress.

### Does repeated price pressure mean the customer is in negotiation?

Not necessarily. Customers can ask about price repeatedly while still confirming whether a solution exists, often to compare budget options. Check two things: whether the customer discusses scope, time, payment, and risk with you, and whether acceptance standards and responsible owners are on the table. If the conversation is only about unit price, ask more questions instead of moving quickly to concessions.

### How can I confirm that someone inside the customer owns the goal and resources?

Look for where resources and result responsibility sit. Verifiable signs include someone providing internal data, arranging a cross-department meeting, specifying acceptance standards, or being willing to sign a document. Hearing that the project is important is not enough. Put these points into the decision-chain row of PRTABCD; the missing cell tells you what to ask next. If you cannot get an answer, the position may not be far enough along.

### Is relationship capability just more dinners and meetings?

No. It is the use of professional insight, decision information, and verifiable action to work through the decision chain. Know who supports you, who delays, who sets standards, and who signs. Keep each commitment and leave a record. Social familiarity cannot replace an understanding of the customer's decision tasks, and it does not produce internal information by itself.

### Where should questioning start?

Start with situation questions: establish current practice and data. Then ask problem questions so the customer identifies where the difficulty lies. Use impact questions to make the consequence concrete enough to calculate. Finish with need-payoff questions that let the customer describe the benefit of solving it. Do not skip the sequence; skipping turns the conversation into a script, and customers notice a sales pitch.

### What if the customer plans to build the capability in-house?

There is still an opportunity, but the comparison basis must change. At the build-versus-buy task, the customer is comparing capability, time, risk, and total cost. Help put in-house labor and launch time into the same cost table, and write out the scope, pace, and responsibility boundaries of an external option. The customer makes the decision; your value is making the comparison clear.

### How should I concede in pricing without damaging margin?

Before conceding, write down the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, your bottom line, your authority, and the interests you can trade. Then exchange conditions: give price only for scope, payment rhythm, delivery time, or risk sharing. An unconditional price cut can pull later projects into the same low-price pattern.
