Supply-chain cooperation announcements can look like press-release material to a sales team. The more useful reading is operational: a fresh-milk agreement involving production capacity, cold-chain distribution and store delivery shows where value, risk and decision-making sit. According to Eastmoney public reporting, Mixue Group reached a strategic cooperation on fresh-milk supply. The public headline expects fresh milk to cover nationwide stores by 2028. The public summary also mentions production-side investment in fresh-milk lines, HPP high-pressure processing lines and a Yunnan factory. Supporting work includes three-temperature same-park warehousing, three-temperature same-truck delivery, and an unloading upgrade from tailgate handover to in-store delivery. Those facts support a disciplined account conversation; they do not support claims about completed coverage, contract value, store-level returns or internal assessment.

## Use a five-capability lens to turn news into judgments

The source applies a five-capability lens to key-account sales: value, relationship, questioning, system and negotiation. Used carefully, it turns a news item into four judgments: why the customer is acting, who participates in the decision, what must be asked, and what must be exchanged in negotiation.

### A compact comparison of the five capabilities

## Value: translate store coverage into an operating account

For Mixue, fresh-milk coverage is not an isolated purchasing action. More stores mean raw-material stability, cold-chain continuity and unloading efficiency affect store output and operating rhythm. A seller who says fresh milk is good leaves the customer hearing a product. A seller who explains how short-shelf-life ingredients reach stores on store rhythm and reduce receiving disputes is closer to an operating conversation. Value work starts with a written hypothesis: which ordering, delivery or receiving problems may exist; which are worth describing with operating numbers; which need confirmation. Public information gives no single-store return or contract amount, so treat benefits as hypotheses until customer evidence exists.

## Relationship: map the decision chain beyond procurement

This kind of cooperation touches production, procurement, quality control, cold chain and store operations. PRTABCD's seven decision-information items include A for Arm and D for Decision. A asks who will explain value internally; D reminds you that many people and departments decide, with procurement as one interface. For each key person, ask three questions: Which supply segment worries you most? Which store metric does it affect? What evidence would make a solution credible? Specific answers give the relationship a footing and show who to ask next.

## Questioning: surface the real constraints of short-shelf-life supply

Short-shelf-life ingredients and cold-chain assurance are the entry point. Use SPIN's four question types:

- Situation: How do stores order, receive and handle fresh milk now?
- Problem: Which stores are most likely to see late delivery or receiving disputes?
- Implication: What rework and complaints follow a stock-out or temperature exception?
- Need-payoff: What operating attention could be freed if supply were more stable?

Ask about the customer's business, not product features. Record the customer's own words and turn them into the next evidence list.

## System: judge stage progress by customer evidence

Large-account progress should not rest on a feeling that the deal is close. System capability uses a two-view funnel: the sales five stages and the customer's seven purchasing stages. The sales stages are contact, need, solution, negotiation and close. The customer stages ask whether the customer recognizes the problem, judges value, confirms a solution, assigns ownership, compares build versus buy, enters solution and supplier selection, and completes commitment and signing. The two axes are many-to-many; visit counts cannot replace customer behavior evidence. Public information can confirm the cooperation and supporting investment. It cannot confirm that nationwide store coverage is complete. In review, require at least one customer-behavior proof per stage: the customer adds store acceptance standards, the operations lead joins a pilot, or payment terms appear in meeting minutes. No evidence means the stage stays put.

### Public facts versus unsupported conclusions

Use a three-part review to keep the team honest:

- Fact: what the public report actually states.
- Hypothesis: what may matter to the customer and still needs confirmation.
- To verify: what the customer must answer or show.

This keeps the team from turning strategic cooperation into a claim of completed rollout or a closed sale.

## Negotiation: make trade-offs explicit

Negotiation capability builds sustainable agreements from power, time, information and alternatives. In supply-chain cooperation, price is only one condition. Delivery cadence, acceptance standards, cold-chain responsibility, exception handling and payment milestones may all be exchangeable. List the conditions first, then ask the customer which must be in the contract, which can be tested in a pilot, and which should be shared. The public report gives no amount or contract term, so do not invent them. Strategic cooperation is a signal that the customer is willing to move together. It is not proof of budget, timetable, acceptance owner or next action.

## Actionable checklist for the next account review

Turn the news into four actions:

1. Write a one-page value hypothesis. List possible problems in fresh-milk supply, cold-chain delivery and store receiving; mark which need operating numbers and which need confirmation.
2. Draw the decision chain with PRTABCD's seven items. Show what is known, unknown, the source and the next question; mark A as Arm and D as Decision.
3. Build a SPIN interview guide. Use no more than three questions per key person, then record the customer's exact words.
4. Compare sales five stages with customer purchasing seven stages. Before any stage moves, find one piece of customer-behavior evidence.

Keep public facts, customer quotes and unverified assumptions in separate notes. The aim is not to sound informed about a headline; it is to judge whether your own account is moving.

## FAQ

**What is public in this case?** The public facts are the strategic fresh-milk cooperation, the headline expectation of nationwide store coverage by 2028, and the stated production and cold-chain supporting work. Internal assessment, single-store returns, store counts, contract amount and completion of nationwide coverage are not in the public record. Keep them as questions, not conclusions.

**How should value capability be used?** Translate coverage into store operations: stock availability, cold-chain continuity, receiving disputes and operating attention. Write hypotheses and confirm them with customer questions or pilot evidence. Do not present assumed returns as results.

**Why look beyond procurement?** The decision touches production, procurement, quality, cold chain and store operations. A procurement contact is one interface. Use A and D from PRTABCD to find the internal advocate and map the wider decision chain.

**How do we know a project is advancing?** Require customer behavior, not visit frequency. Match sales stages to the customer's purchasing stages and ask for evidence such as added acceptance standards, an operations lead in a pilot or written payment terms.

**What should be negotiated besides price?** Delivery cadence, acceptance standards, cold-chain responsibility, exception handling and payment milestones. Decide which conditions belong in the contract, which need a pilot, and which require shared responsibility.

### Why strategic cooperation is not a closed sale

A strategic cooperation signals willingness to proceed. It does not confirm budget, a timetable, an acceptance owner or the next action. Public reporting can confirm cooperation and supporting investment; it cannot confirm that nationwide coverage is complete. Treating a signal as a closed deal distorts stage judgment and resource decisions.
