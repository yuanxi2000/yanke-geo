When i Moutai changed the release schedule for six Feitian Moutai products from fixed days to twice-daily drops, the surface story was about timing. But beneath that adjustment lies a deeper principle: rules are a form of value design. The platform's first-half revenue of 40.26 billion yuan suggests that this kind of rule-based value conversion has proven effective at scale. For key-account sales professionals, the takeaway is clear: your delivery cadence, service rules, and pricing structure are not just appendages to a transaction—they are integral parts of the value you offer.

This analysis draws on publicly reported facts about the rule change and the platform's overall operating data. It does not claim a proven causal link between the two; rather, it uses the event as a lens to explore how sales teams can think about value beyond product features.

## What the Event Tells Us

The core change is straightforward: instead of concentrating purchases on fixed days, i Moutai now offers two drops per day, spreading out the opportunity to buy. This alters the perceived availability of the product. While the specific decision-making process behind the change hasn't been disclosed, the direction is clear—rules are being adjusted to follow customer behavior rather than internal convenience.

For key-account sales, this highlights a frequently overlooked dimension of value. Many sales teams equate value with translating product features into customer benefits. That's not wrong, but it's incomplete. Value also includes the rules you use to allocate that value to customers. By changing the frequency of drops, i Moutai changed the opportunity structure for customers, reshaping the tension between scarcity and accessibility.

### Lesson 1: Treat Delivery Cadence as Part of Your Value Proposition

Selling the same Feitian Moutai product, a fixed-day drop versus twice-daily drops creates a completely different customer experience. The former fosters a sense of scarcity through concentrated buying frenzies; the latter lowers the barrier to access and broadens participation. The product hasn't changed, but the rules have—and so has the customer's perception of value. This aligns with the PSS⁵ value capability definition: shifting the sales object from product features to customer-understandable investments, returns, and profit improvements.

Translated to key-account sales, ask yourself three questions:

1. Is your payment schedule, delivery cadence, and service response rhythm defined by your convenience or by your customer's operational rhythm?
2. Does your quotation and contract language make it easy for the customer to see when they'll recoup their investment?
3. Is there any step where a simple rule change—like shifting from quarterly reports to reports aligned with the customer's decision milestones—would noticeably increase the customer's willingness to move forward?

Incorporating cadence into your value proposition often requires no additional cost but can significantly alter customer perception.

## Lesson 2: Let Data Speak to Make Value Credible

What makes the i Moutai story persuasive is the supporting operational data—40.26 billion yuan in revenue, a 274.2% year-on-year increase. Without results data, a channel rule adjustment is just a management action; with data, it becomes validated value design. This is what the value capability demands: using operational numbers to substantiate value.

The most common mistake in the proposal stage is relying on adjectives to describe benefits: improve efficiency, reduce costs, enhance experience. The correct approach is to tie each benefit to a metric the customer can verify: which specific expense is saved, which capacity is freed up, which operational cycle is affected. When writing a profit improvement proposal (PIP), you must answer three things: how much profit can be added or preserved, what the mechanism and confidence level are, and within what timeframe. Value propositions without numbers get filtered out when circulated internally, because your contact needs them to persuade budget approvers.

## Lesson 3: Rule Adjustments Should Follow Customer Behavior Evidence

The shift from fixed days to twice-daily drops can be interpreted as a response to observed user participation patterns. Concentrated drops compress demand into a few time points, while daily drops spread the opportunity, allowing more genuine demand to be met. Although the report doesn't disclose the decision process, the direction is consistent: rules follow customer behavior, not internal habits.

For sales, this means your engagement cadence should also follow evidence of customer behavior. The PSS⁵ sales framework divides the process into five stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. Each stage should be validated by customer-side exit evidence, not by visit counts or self-perception. For example, if the customer proactively brings your materials to an internal meeting, that's evidence the needs stage is progressing. If they start discussing acceptance criteria and payment terms, that signals movement toward negotiation. When you're unsure where you stand, rather than guessing, return to the customer's seven-stage buying process and identify which decision task they're currently stuck on.

## FAQ

**Q1: What exactly did i Moutai change?**

The core change is in the release rhythm: moving from concentrated fixed-day drops to twice-daily drops that spread out the opportunity to purchase. The full description of the event and data is in the opening section; the boundaries of data interpretation are explained in the next question.

**Q2: How does a channel rule change relate to key-account sales?**

The connection lies in the logic of value design. i Moutai used a change in drop frequency to redistribute customers' perception of availability, demonstrating that rules themselves are part of the value proposition. Similarly, key-account sales can adjust delivery cadence, communication frequency, pricing structure, and other rules to change customer perception and willingness to move forward without increasing costs.

**Q3: What does PSS⁵'s value capability specifically mean?**

In the PSS⁵ framework, value capability refers to shifting the sales object from product features to customer-understandable investments, returns, and profit improvements, and using operational numbers to substantiate value. It requires salespeople to answer three questions: how much profit can be added or preserved, what the mechanism and confidence are, and within what timeframe. Value propositions with only adjectives and no numbers struggle to gain support within the customer organization.

**Q4: Why is the 40.26 billion yuan figure important for this analysis?**

It provides a reference point for the platform's overall operating scale. However, note that this figure reflects the platform's overall performance and does not directly prove that this specific rule change caused the growth. Treating it as causal evidence would exceed the boundaries of the data. Sales professionals should apply the same standard to their own proposals: distinguish correlation from causation, and ensure benefit claims are verifiable with clear boundaries.

**Q5: How can I practically incorporate delivery cadence into my value proposition?**

Start by mapping your customer's key operational and decision milestones. Then align your reporting frequency, delivery milestones, and payment terms to those milestones. For example, shift from a fixed quarterly report to a special report aligned with the customer's decision points, or break a one-time delivery into smaller batches that allow the customer to quickly validate value. The principle is to make it easier for the customer to access and quantify value, not to make your internal processes more convenient.

**Q6: How can a salesperson determine the true position of a project in the funnel?**

Use customer behavior evidence, not visit counts or self-perception. The PSS⁵ sales framework has five stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. For each stage, look for customer-side evidence: if the customer proactively brings your materials to an internal meeting, that indicates progress in the needs stage. If they start discussing acceptance criteria and payment terms, that signals movement toward negotiation. If you can't find corresponding behavior evidence, your stage assessment is unreliable.

**Q7: What is the difference between a profit improvement proposal and a regular quotation?**

A regular quotation answers questions about product and price. A profit improvement proposal answers questions about return on investment: how much profit this investment can add or preserve, what the mechanism and confidence are, and within what timeframe. Its audience includes not just the person handling the purchase but also the management layer that approves budgets. Proposals with operational numbers, verification methods, and timeframes survive internal circulation.

**Q8: Rule-based adjustments don't require big investments, so why do many sales teams fail to implement them?**

Because rule design requires understanding customer behavior, which is harder than changing prices. To see when customers have genuine needs, where they drop off, and what commitments lead to next steps, you need continuous behavioral observation and documentation. Many teams' visit logs only record what they did, not what the customer did. Without behavior evidence, it's impossible to design rule adjustments that follow the customer.
