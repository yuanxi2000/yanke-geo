Long-cycle key-account sales advance when customers can verify progress. A persuasive vision may open a conversation, but it cannot settle questions about technical reliability, financial value, internal ownership, or delivery risk. Each unresolved question represents work the customer still needs to do before committing.

The supplied account of BOE's manufacturing iterations offers a useful reference: difficult technical work progressed through successive improvements and identifiable milestones. For sales teams, the transferable lesson is to organize complex opportunities around evidence that helps customers make their next decision.

This guide turns that principle into a practical approach to qualification, stakeholder relationships, value discussions, negotiation, and pipeline review.

## Replace broad promises with agreed milestones

A project that stretches across years creates ample room for expectations to drift. Salespeople may describe progress through meetings and proposals while customers remain uncertain about whether the solution will work.

Break the project into outcomes the customer can recognize and evaluate. For each milestone, agree on the question it must answer, the acceptance criteria, and the people responsible for reviewing the result. A technical demonstration becomes meaningful when it resolves a purchasing concern.

The source describes BOE's integration of capabilities such as high refresh rates, high resolution, and low-frequency driving. These illustrate how specific technical developments can mark progress. They do not establish a universal milestone sequence for other projects.

### Define the evidence before starting the work

For each proposed milestone, record:

- The customer concern being addressed.
- The result or behavior that would demonstrate progress.
- The agreed verification method and acceptance criteria.
- The customer stakeholders who must review the evidence.
- The decision that becomes possible if the criteria are met.

After each iteration, revisit the value proposition and communication priorities. A successful test may resolve one concern while revealing another department's requirements.

## Match sales activity to the customer's decision

A seller's process and a customer's purchasing process describe different kinds of progress. Sending a proposal does not establish that the customer has confirmed responsibility, budget, or a preference for buying externally.

The source distinguishes five sales stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. It also identifies seven customer purchasing stages: recognizing a problem, assessing its value, confirming that a solution exists, assigning responsibility, deciding whether to build or buy, selecting a solution and supplier, and committing through agreement.

Use both views during opportunity reviews. Establish the customer's current decision task, then choose the sales activity that helps complete it.

| Customer decision task | Useful sales focus | Evidence to examine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assess the problem's value | Explore operational and financial consequences | Customer-confirmed impact assumptions |
| Confirm that a solution exists | Agree on a relevant validation milestone | Reviewed test findings |
| Decide whether to build or buy | Examine ownership, risk, and timing | Customer discussion of alternatives |
| Select and commit | Resolve acceptance criteria and commercial terms | Signed test reports or agreements |

These are practical illustrations of alignment, not a rigid correspondence between stages. Solution discussions and negotiation may overlap as new evidence changes the customer's assessment.

## Build relationships through professional understanding

In technically demanding sales, relationships depend partly on the salesperson's ability to understand the customer's problems. Access to senior people is useful, but it does not replace a shared understanding of reliability, stability, and operational risk.

A salesperson discussing lower power consumption should understand why it matters in the customer's application. Potential implications might include battery life or battery cost, but the relevant connection must be established with that customer.

Use the source's PRTABCD decision-information tool to structure discovery:

- **Past:** What history shapes the current decision?
- **Requirement:** What does the customer actually need?
- **Time:** What deadlines and dependencies matter?
- **Arm:** Who is an internal supporter of progress?
- **Budget:** What funding information is known or unresolved?
- **Competitor:** What competing suppliers or alternatives are being considered?
- **Decision:** Who participates in the decision chain?

For every category, record known information, remaining gaps, the information source, and the next question. Keep assumptions visible.

Revisit the stakeholder picture after each iteration. Research, production, and quality teams may become involved at different points. An internal supporter can help move the project forward, but that person's support should not be mistaken for agreement across every affected department.

## Translate technical features into business value

A feature becomes commercially useful when the customer can connect it to an operational consequence and a financial effect. Listing specifications alone leaves that work unfinished.

Follow a clear chain: technical change, operational effect, business consequence, and potential financial value. Identify which links are supported by evidence and which still require validation.

For example, lower power consumption might support longer battery life or a different battery cost requirement. The source presents these as possible business implications. It supplies no customer-specific savings calculation, so any financial estimate requires customer data.

Use SPIN questioning to develop that understanding:

- **Situation:** What process constraints and competitive pressures exist?
- **Problem:** Where does the current approach fall short on stability or reliability?
- **Implication:** How do those shortcomings affect yield, rework, delivery, or reputation?
- **Need-payoff:** What would improve if the problem were resolved?

Treat the answers as inputs to a value case. Do not turn an untested assumption into a promised return.

The source's PIP, or profit improvement proposal, frames the solution as an investment explanation: how much profit might be added or protected, through what mechanism, with what degree of confidence, and over what period. Where the necessary inputs are missing, specify what must be learned before calculating value.

## Negotiate alternatives and risk allocation

Long projects carry uncertainty about technical choices, market prices, schedules, and commercial terms. Negotiation therefore includes deciding how the parties will handle risk.

Prepare a BATNA: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. For the seller, this might involve other prospective customers or an inventory turnover plan. For the buyer, alternatives might include another supplier or building internally. Their feasibility needs investigation.

Test both sides' assumptions. If payment terms become difficult, assess the consequences of declining the order, including the implications for capacity utilization. Explore the customer's alternatives and their associated time and implementation risks.

Use this analysis to evaluate acceptable terms and executable alternatives. A BATNA supports judgment; it should not become a threat.

When the parties lack enough evidence for a broader commitment, consider a small pilot with agreed evaluation criteria. It may help build trust and data. Its usefulness depends on whether the findings can inform the larger decision.

## Make evidence part of routine management

A repeatable process requires shared stage definitions and consistent review habits. Otherwise, different salespeople may attach different meanings to the same pipeline label.

During pipeline reviews, examine customer behavior alongside seller activity. Internal meeting records and signed test reports can help show what the customer has evaluated or accepted. A meeting count alone cannot establish that a purchasing decision has advanced.

Keep the process visible from initial opportunity through payment collection. Pair training with practical coaching, and turn useful practices from completed work into operating procedures. The aim is to make informed judgment available across the team.

## Actionable opportunity checklist

Before the next account review:

- Identify the customer's current purchasing decision task.
- Check whether the planned sales action addresses that task.
- Define the next milestone and its acceptance criteria.
- Name the stakeholders who will evaluate the result.
- Update PRTABCD information, sources, gaps, and next questions.
- Separate validated value inputs from assumptions.
- Confirm internal support across the departments now involved.
- Review both parties' alternatives and commercial limits.
- Record customer behavior that supports the proposed stage.
- Agree on the next action and responsibility.

## FAQ

### What counts as evidence of progress?

Evidence should relate to the customer's decision. Examples in the source include internal meeting records and signed test reports. Assess what each item demonstrates rather than treating its existence as automatic qualification.

### How can a team recognize misalignment?

Compare the seller's intended action with the customer's unresolved task. Discussing final pricing while the customer is still assessing whether the problem merits investment indicates a mismatch.

### Does technical credibility require becoming an engineer?

The source calls for enough domain understanding to explain the customer's pain points and the solution's business relevance. This means being able to connect technical discussions to purchasing concerns and identify where further clarification is needed.

### What if financial value cannot yet be calculated?

Document the proposed value mechanism and the missing inputs. Continue discovery and validation. An unsupported savings figure would weaken the evidence-based approach.

### Can a pilot resolve a stalled negotiation?

It may help when uncertainty is preventing commitment. Agree first on what the pilot must establish and how its findings will inform the next decision. A pilot does not guarantee a wider agreement.
