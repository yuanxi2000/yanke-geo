A fleet of drones, robotic dogs, and unmanned boats is easy to describe. Explaining why a customer should invest in coordinating them requires a different conversation: how patrol work is assigned, how incidents are detected, how responses are coordinated, and how results are reviewed.

The supplied account of Huashu Media's public information describes different device types brought into unified dispatch and coordinated operations, with patrol outcomes framed around the integration of people, machines, and the operating environment. That provides a useful sales analysis case. It does not establish a financial return or a completed customer investment case.

For key-account sellers, the practical task is to translate technical coordination into outcomes the customer can verify. This guide follows five capabilities: value creation, relationship development, questioning, systematic sales management, and negotiation. Each should produce evidence that supports the customer's next decision.

## Establish the facts before interpreting the opportunity

Start by separating the public information from the questions a customer must answer.

The supplied source describes drones, robotic dogs, and unmanned boats operating within a shared dispatch arrangement. It connects that coordination to patrol work involving people, machines, and their environment. It does not provide a counterparty, transaction amount, or date. Leave those details unspecified.

Unified dispatch creates questions worth investigating; it does not answer them automatically. A customer still needs to establish where coordination matters, which gaps deserve investment, and how any improvement would be accepted.

Keep two columns in working notes: verified facts and items requiring customer confirmation. Put proposed benefits in the second column until the customer has confirmed the relevant baseline and evaluation criteria. This keeps a technical announcement from becoming an unsupported business case.

## Translate equipment capabilities into an investment case

Value creation means connecting a product or service to the customer's profit, investment, and operating priorities. Begin with the existing patrol task rather than equipment specifications.

Ask the customer to describe which work people perform, which tasks fixed equipment handles, and where mobile equipment fills gaps. Then examine handoffs. Where does an inability to coordinate equipment create repeated work or disconnected information?

Possible measures from the source include:

- Patrol coverage.
- How incidents are detected.
- Personnel input.
- Equipment utilization.
- The number of coordination interactions during response.

These are candidate measures, not promised improvements. The customer must confirm their definitions and current baseline before they appear as established figures in a proposal.

### Write an investment explanation before presenting a price

A PIP, or profit growth plan, should explain how much profit an investment could add or protect, the mechanism and confidence behind that expectation, and the period over which it could occur. It is an investment explanation, not a product quotation.

If those answers remain unknown, record the questions explicitly. Ask which patrol outcomes matter enough to justify investment and what evidence would make the improvement credible. The source supplies no figures with which to calculate a return.

## Map the decision chain with PRTABCD

Relationship development rests on professional competence, decision information, and dependable actions. In this setting, the task is to understand and cover the decision chain.

The source identifies user departments, technical teams, finance, and procurement as relevant participants in projects involving different equipment types. Support from one contact does not establish that the wider organization can move forward.

PRTABCD provides a compact information scan:

| Element | Information to establish |
| --- | --- |
| P: Past information | Relevant history and previous decisions |
| R: Requirements | Needs the customer recognizes |
| T: Timing | Key dates and decision milestones |
| A: Advocates | People actively embracing the proposed approach |
| B: Budget | Available funding and budget responsibility |
| C: Competition and alternatives | Competing approaches and other available options |
| D: Decision chain | How people and departments participate in decisions |

For every element, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. An advocate is someone embracing the approach. The decision chain may include multiple people and departments; it should not be reduced to one presumed decision-maker.

Record separately who uses the equipment, who owns the patrol outcome, who controls the budget, and who participates in acceptance. Update the evidence after each interaction. Courteous feedback alone does not show that a decision has advanced.

## Use SPIN to let the customer describe the gap

Questioning should help the customer identify a real need and understand its consequences. The SPIN sequence moves through situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions.

Use four questions as a starting point:

1. **Situation:** How are patrol tasks currently divided among people and equipment?
2. **Problem:** Where does limited coordination cause repeated work or breaks in information?
3. **Implication:** How do those breaks affect personnel input, equipment use, and response coordination?
4. **Need-payoff:** If unified dispatch improved those gaps, what measures would establish that the investment was worthwhile?

The purpose is to elicit the customer's account, not steer them toward a rehearsed answer.

When a customer says there is already plenty of equipment, explore how devices hand work over, how anomalies are confirmed, and how results are reviewed. When the customer is still considering the opportunity, identify which decision remains unresolved. Additional equipment descriptions may not answer that question.

## Judge progress through the customer's decision tasks

Systematic sales management uses two perspectives together: the seller's process and the customer's procurement process.

The five sales stages are contact, needs discovery, proposal, negotiation, and closing. The seven customer procurement stages are recognizing a problem, assessing value, confirming that a solution exists, confirming responsibility, deciding whether to build or buy, selecting a solution and supplier, and commitment and signing.

These perspectives have different levels of detail. Their relationship is many-to-many, so avoid forcing each sales stage into a single procurement stage.

Look instead for observable customer behavior:

- Will the customer arrange an on-site validation?
- Are user departments, technical teams, and finance participating together?
- Has the customer clarified budget responsibility and acceptance criteria?
- Has the customer begun comparing internal development with external procurement?

Visit counts, verbal praise, and proposal length cannot substitute for this evidence. A quotation may be premature while the customer is still establishing the problem. Equipment introductions may be insufficient once the customer is selecting solutions and suppliers.

Choose the next sales action by the customer's current decision task. Where evidence is missing, make obtaining it the next action rather than assuming the project has progressed.

## Prepare negotiation around conditions and alternatives

Before discussing a discount, clarify scope, acceptance criteria, payment, implementation responsibilities, and risk allocation. Negotiation preparation should also address relative power, time, information, limits, and alternatives.

A request for a lower price should open a discussion about what conditions can change. The source suggests scope adjustments, payment changes, additional staged acceptance, or a longer cooperation period as possible subjects for exchange. These are options to examine, not concessions to promise automatically.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Preparation should identify executable alternatives, limits, authority, interests, and records. Establish what the customer could do without an agreement and what terms the seller can accept.

Without that preparation, a discussion about coordinated patrol outcomes can narrow into comparisons between individual equipment prices. Keep proposed exchanges connected to the agreed scope and investment case.

## FAQ

### What is the central sales lesson in this case?

The equipment list starts the discussion. The investment case depends on customer-confirmed patrol responsibilities, coordination gaps, and acceptance measures.

### Can the public information support a savings estimate?

It supplies no financial figures or customer baseline. Any estimate requires customer-confirmed inputs and an explanation of the mechanism and timeframe.

### Does support from one contact show that the opportunity is progressing?

Support is useful information, but progress requires evidence about the broader decision chain, budget responsibility, acceptance, and the customer's next decision.

### Should the two funnels be matched stage by stage?

No. The five sales stages and seven procurement stages have a many-to-many relationship. Match actions to customer decision tasks using observable evidence.
