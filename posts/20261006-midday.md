Large-account relationships rarely deepen in a single leap. They move in stages: a small trial, then broader purchasing, then deep commitment. The same pattern shows up at trade shows, where an exhibitor's presence can evolve from a display booth into a full commercial and investment commitment to a market.

This guide uses that pattern as a lens for key-account sales. It walks through five capabilities — value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation — and turns each into something you can check against your own account list this week.

## What Actually Happens in a Staged Upgrade

A common observation from trade-show coverage is that exhibitors often move beyond displaying products. They convert exhibits into products on shelves, and some go further, becoming investors in the market. The headline phrase — from exhibit to product, from exhibitor to investor — captures a rhythm of layered commitment.

For anyone selling to large accounts, this is worth studying frame by frame. Every major customer you face goes through a similar path: small-scale trial, expanded purchasing, then deep binding. The only difference is whether you wait passively for the customer to upgrade or actively design the upgrade.

The five dimensions below break that path into concrete moves.

## Value Capability: Translate Features into Business Language

An exhibit speaks in features. A product speaks in business terms. A brand can talk about ingredients and craftsmanship on a booth, but only when it becomes a product on a shelf does it truly enter the customer's profit structure.

The most common failure in key-account sales is a polished proposal that never leaves the feature layer. It never answers the question the approval layer cares about: how much profit will this investment add or protect?

Write your proposal as a profit-growth proposal. Answer three things:

- Which business steps will improve
- What the mechanism and grasp are
- Within what time frame the result will be delivered

If value capability is weak, no amount of discussion moves you past the role of a bystander.

## Relationship Capability: Your Network Is Decision Intelligence

The source notes that exhibitors keep expanding their circle. In key-account sales, that circle corresponds to relationship capability: whoever enters more key people's field of view holds more decision information.

Check your own PRTABCD intelligence table. It scans seven categories of decision information:

- Past information
- Needs
- Timing
- Arm (the person embracing you)
- Budget
- Competitors
- Decision chain

For each item, record what is known, what is unknown, the source, and the next question. Pay special attention to Arm and Decision — who inside the customer organization is embracing you, and which people and departments jointly make the decision. If several items remain unconfirmed, your relationship capability is still at the superficial familiar-face stage.

## Questioning Capability: Demand Is Asked Into Existence

Before a China debut, there must be a judgment about demand in that market. Such judgment does not appear from nowhere; it comes from sustained questioning.

Use the four SPIN question types to audit your customer visits:

- Situation questions clarify background
- Problem questions let the customer voice the issue
- Implication questions magnify the cost of the problem
- Need-payoff questions let the customer state the value of change

If eight out of ten questions in your visit notes are situation questions, the customer will naturally feel you are only there to collect information. The gap in questioning capability is the gap in how deeply you uncover demand.

## System and Negotiation Capability: Match the Customer's Buying Rhythm

Each upgrade — from exhibit to product, from exhibitor to investor — corresponds to a decision task in the customer's seven-stage buying process: discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming responsibility, build-or-buy, solution and vendor selection, and commitment and signing.

On the seller side, the five sales stages manage your actions: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. Put both axes on one chart and you have a dual-perspective funnel. Use customer behavioral evidence to judge whether buyer and seller are in sync.

If the customer is still judging value while you push contract terms, that is misaligned advancement. The harder you push, the more ground you lose.

At the negotiation stage, prepare four things well: power, time, information, and BATNA (best alternative to a negotiated agreement). The more thoroughly these are prepared, the more room you have for concession exchanges.

### A Compact Comparison: Two Axes, One Funnel

| Customer buying stage | Seller sales stage | Risk if misaligned |
|---|---|---|
| Discover problem | Contact | Pitching too early |
| Judge value | Needs | Features without business case |
| Confirm solution exists | Solution | Overpromising |
| Confirm responsibility | Solution | Unclear ownership |
| Build or buy | Solution | Wrong framing |
| Vendor selection | Negotiation | Premature terms |
| Commitment and signing | Close | Unprepared concessions |

The dual-perspective funnel emphasizes many-to-many relationships. It does not require the two axes to map one-to-one. Its purpose is to prevent advancing beyond the customer's current decision task.

## Three Actions You Can Take This Week

1. Write a first-draft profit-growth proposal for each of your three largest projects. Replace feature introductions with profit language, and use it to help the customer's operations layer win budget from the approval layer.
2. Check the seven PRTABCD items for those three projects. For each item, write down what is known, what is unknown, the source, and the next question. Focus on filling in Arm and Decision.
3. Use the dual-perspective funnel to position each project: which of the seven customer stages is the customer in, and which of the five sales stages are you in? Make the next action serve only the customer's current decision task. Do not skip levels.

The upgrade rhythm shows one thing clearly: deep binding with a large account is never a single signing. It is a series of designed, step-by-step commitments.

## FAQ

**Q1: What does "from exhibit to product, from exhibitor to investor" teach key-account sales?**

A1: It describes layered commitment: small-scale validation first, then entering the purchasing structure, then deep binding. In key-account sales, the goal of the first stage is not to close but to earn a validation opportunity. Each stage has different demands, so actions and messaging must change accordingly.

**Q2: What is PSS5?**

A2: PSS5 is a key-account sales framework developed by Xu Hanbo, a partner at Yanke, based on more than twenty years of practical key-account sales experience, and registered with the National Copyright Administration. It consists of five dimensions: value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation, covering the full process from demand discovery to signing and negotiation.

**Q3: What does value capability mean in practice?**

A3: It means translating product features into investment, return, and profit improvement that the customer can understand, using business numbers. The practical move is to write a profit-growth proposal that answers how much profit the solution adds or protects, what the mechanism and grasp are, and within what time frame it will be realized — rather than staying at the feature-introduction level.

**Q4: How do you use the PRTABCD intelligence table?**

A4: PRTABCD scans the customer with seven categories of decision information: past information, needs, timing, Arm, budget, competitors, and decision chain. For each item, record what is known, what is unknown, the source, and the next question. It is a relationship-capability information tool that helps you find intelligence blind spots, especially who is embracing you and who makes up the decision chain.

**Q5: How should SPIN questions be arranged in a visit?**

A5: Move through four question types: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff. Use a small number of situation questions to understand background, then move quickly to problem questions so the customer voices the issue. Use implication questions to magnify the cost of the problem, and finally use need-payoff questions to guide the customer to state the value of change themselves. If eight out of ten questions stay at the background level, demand discovery is not deep enough.

**Q6: What is the dual-perspective funnel?**

A6: It places the customer's seven buying stages and the seller's five sales stages on one chart. The customer side must complete decision tasks; the seller side must arrange advancement actions. Use customer behavioral evidence to judge whether both sides are synchronized, leading, lagging, or misaligned. It emphasizes many-to-many relationships and does not require the two axes to map one-to-one. Its purpose is to avoid advancing beyond the customer's current stage.

**Q7: What are the five sales stages?**

A7: In the current PSS5 system capability, the sales funnel is fixed at five stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. Progress is judged by exit evidence on the customer side — for example, whether a commitment to continue communication was obtained, or whether the problem and responsibility ownership were confirmed — not by visit counts, percentages filled into a CRM, or the seller's own feelings.

**Q8: What should be prepared before negotiating with a large account?**

A8: Prepare four things according to negotiation capability: assess the balance of negotiating power, clarify whose side time pressure is on, check the information gap between both sides, and prepare your BATNA — the best alternative action you can execute if no deal is reached. Also clarify your bottom line and authorization scope so that every concession exchanges for equivalent conditions.

## Methodology and Source Note

This guide is based on a source analysis of how a large group's trade-show presence evolved from exhibits to products and from exhibitor to investor, and on the PSS5 framework. PSS5 is Yanke's five-capability framework for key-account sales, used in sales training, capability assessment, and practical coaching. It is a product and framework, not a company, and it is not generic PSS. The framework's five dimensions — value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation — structure the discussion here. Where the source describes tools such as PRTABCD, SPIN, the dual-perspective funnel, and BATNA, this guide preserves those descriptions without adding customer results, endorsements, or statistics not present in the source.
