> The biggest problem in industrial enterprise sales is not that customers refuse to buy. It is that sales keeps pushing from its own side while the customer has not yet moved through the stage of confirming value.

## Treat the customer's seven buying stages as a map, not the seller's five stages as a progress bar

The PSS⁵ systems framework provides a two-perspective funnel: the seller sees five sales stages—contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing—while the buyer sees seven purchasing stages: recognizing a problem, judging its value, confirming that a solution exists, confirming responsibility, deciding whether to build or buy, selecting a solution and supplier, and committing to the purchase and contract.

The five sales stages answer “What do I need to do?”, while the customer's seven purchasing stages answer “Where has the customer reached?” The two kinds of stages have a many-to-many relationship; they do not correspond mechanically one-to-one.

- **Contact:** Establish an entry point related to the customer's business problem and secure a commitment to continue the conversation
- **Needs:** Confirm the problem, value, and ownership of the responsibility so that a reason to buy takes shape
- **Solution:** Define the way forward, success criteria, and validation method together with the customer
- **Negotiation:** Exchange terms around price, scope, timing, payment, and risk
- **Closing:** Complete the formal commitment, contract, and handoff to implementation

## Use evidence of customer behavior instead of meeting counts to know whether an opportunity is really moving

Many sales reviews still report “whom we met, what we discussed, and that the customer's attitude was positive.” The systems framework requires replacing these with customer-side actions: Has the customer acknowledged a gap between the current state and the target? Has someone taken ownership of the responsibility in an internal discussion? Has the customer compared building with buying? Without these actions, many visits are still only one-sided enthusiasm. Industrial customer decisions usually involve production, equipment, procurement, finance, and other departments; if any one of them is absent, the project may stall.

The PSS⁵ relationship framework's seven-part PRTABCD scan can help complete the information about the customer's decision chain. The systems framework, however, is not concerned with the checklist by itself. It asks whether the information points to a purchasing stage the customer has actually completed. For example, under item D—the decision chain—several departments may be involved, but if the customer has not judged the value, sales should not rush to quote. ## Use the three closing techniques for false interruptions, not to pressure the customer

When a customer says “Let us think about it” or “We are still going through the internal process,” sales should first distinguish a genuine pause from avoidance of an unresolved problem. The three closing techniques in the PSS⁵ systems framework identify false interruptions and seek the next real advance. A real advance is not a verbal promise from the customer. It is the customer's willingness to hold a problem-focused discussion, attend an internal meeting with a clear agenda, or provide a written statement of requirements.

Put every real advance into the two-perspective funnel so the sales team can align during its weekly review: Which cell of the customer's seven purchasing stages does this opportunity currently occupy? Does it match the seller's five sales stages? What customer evidence must be obtained next week? The review then stops being a report of impressions and becomes an evidence check.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does the fourth PSS⁵ force manage?

It manages the entire enterprise-sales process from contact to closing. Its core tool is the two-perspective funnel. The seller sees five sales stages, and the buyer sees seven purchasing stages. Sales must judge whether its actions are synchronized with the customer's current decision task, and must record progress with evidence of customer behavior rather than replacing it with visit counts or the seller's own impression. This makes opportunity progress traceable and focuses team reviews on the real problem.

### Why are the five sales stages and the customer's seven purchasing stages not one-to-one?

The five sales stages answer “What do I need to do?”, while the customer's seven purchasing stages answer “Where has the customer reached?” The customer's decision tasks and the seller's actions have different levels of granularity. The customer may already be deciding whether to build or buy while the seller is still in the solution stage. The relationship is many-to-many: assess both positions separately, then identify the mismatch and the next action.

### How can we tell whether an opportunity is really progressing?

Look for customer-side behavioral evidence. Has the customer acknowledged a gap between the current state and the target? Has the customer claimed responsibility for the target and resources in an internal meeting? Has the customer compared the total cost of building with buying? An opportunity is progressing only when the customer takes such actions. A sales presentation, quotation, or executive visit by itself does not mean that the customer has progressed.

### How do the two-perspective funnel and the seven-part PRTABCD scan work together?

The seven-part PRTABCD scan belongs to the relationship framework and helps complete information about the customer's decision chain. The two-perspective funnel places that information on the stage map and determines the current position. Complete information does not equal stage progress. For example, knowing that several departments are in the decision chain does not mean that the customer has judged value; sales should not rush to quote. One tool fills in intelligence, while the other determines position.

### What should sales do when a customer says “Let us think about it”?

First determine whether it is a false interruption or a genuine pause. The PSS⁵ three closing techniques target a false interruption and seek the next real advance, such as a problem-focused discussion, an internal meeting with a clear agenda, or a written statement of requirements. A real advance must be an action the customer is willing to take, not a verbal promise.

### Why do industrial enterprise projects especially need a two-perspective funnel?

Industrial projects have long cycles and involve many departments; the customer's decision is not completed in a single meeting. Sales can mistake a technical exchange for needs confirmation or a customer visit for solution progress. The two-perspective funnel forces sales to look separately at the buyer's and seller's positions, avoiding premature advancement and avoiding a move into commercial negotiation before the customer has confirmed that a solution exists.

### How should a sales team use stage management in its weekly review?

Mark which cell of the customer's seven purchasing stages each opportunity currently occupies, whether the seller's five sales stages match it, and what customer evidence must be obtained the following week. The review no longer reports that “the conversation went well” or “the attitude was positive”; it checks whether the evidence has changed. This gives the team a shared judgment of real progress and makes resource allocation more accurate.

### What is the most common mistake in judging stages?

The most common mistake is treating a seller's action as the customer's stage—for example, assuming that presenting a solution means the customer has confirmed that a solution exists, or treating “the solution looks good” as entry into negotiation. Stage management requires customer-side evidence for every conclusion. Without evidence, the opportunity cannot move forward. Another mistake is mechanically matching the two kinds of stages and ignoring that the customer may move back and forth among decision tasks.
