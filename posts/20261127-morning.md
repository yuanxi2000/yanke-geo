A major agreement creates a reason to investigate an opportunity. It does not establish where a particular supplier stands in the customer's buying process. The supplied source describes an agreement between Stellantis and Dongfeng that places planned production of two Jeep new-energy products at the Wuhan factory in 2027. For a sales team, that date provides context; it does not prove that a relevant budget, technical standard, or supplier shortlist is ready.

The practical task is to identify the customer's current decision, establish who owns it, and obtain evidence of the next step. This guide applies the source's PSS5 concepts to that work while keeping facts, hypotheses, and unknowns separate.

## Identify the customer's current purchasing task

The source describes seven customer purchasing tasks:

1. Recognize a problem.
2. Assess the value of addressing it.
3. Confirm that a solution exists.
4. Establish ownership and accountability.
5. Decide whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Select a solution and supplier.
7. Commit and sign.

An agreement between cooperating organizations does not establish that every related supplier purchase has reached the final task. A particular opportunity may still depend on product definition, production preparation, technical requirements, or shortlist decisions.

Start with questions that locate the unresolved decision. Who owns the production objective, resources, and results? Are technical standards and validation methods documented? Is funding approved by project, by year, or still awaiting authorization? Who initiates the shortlist, and who confirms the evaluation criteria? If internal development is an option, who owns the capability and schedule risks?

Put confirmed answers into the opportunity record. For unanswered questions, record the unknown, the next question, and a possible information source. A deadline alone cannot answer any of them.

## Map support and the decision chain

A courteous purchasing contact is not sufficient evidence of internal support. Use PRTABCD to scan seven categories of decision information:

- **P — Past:** relevant history.
- **R — Requirements:** customer needs.
- **T — Time:** timing and deadlines.
- **A — Arm:** the person who embraces or actively supports your involvement.
- **B — Budget:** funding and approval status.
- **C — Competition:** competing suppliers and alternative approaches.
- **D — Decision:** the decision chain across people and departments.

Arm does not mean an approver or bargaining leverage. Decision means the chain of participants, not one presumed decision-maker.

In a cross-organization project, possible roles include a business owner, a technical reviewer with veto authority, someone responsible for global market requirements, and someone accountable for factory readiness. These are hypotheses to investigate, not established facts about Stellantis or Dongfeng.

Validate roles through meetings, emails, review records, or formal documents. For a key individual, use MBVS as four lenses: motivation, benchmark, vision, and solution. Explore what matters to that person rather than treating a job title as proof of their position.

## Align selling activity with customer decisions

The seller's five stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. Place these alongside the customer's seven purchasing tasks in a dual-perspective funnel. The purpose is to identify alignment or mismatch, rather than force a one-to-one correspondence.

For example, quoting while the customer is still assessing value may be premature. Continuing introductory conversations after supplier selection has begun may leave the seller behind.

| Observation | What it establishes | Appropriate response |
|---|---|---|
| A meeting or visit occurred | Sales activity took place | Identify the customer's unresolved task |
| Technical review notice or minutes | A review is documented | Confirm its scope, criteria, and owner |
| Written budget or project approval | An approval is recorded | Check its relevance to this purchase |
| Shortlist or tender documents | Selection activity is documented | Confirm requirements and participation |
| Test or validation confirmation | A specific evaluation step is agreed | Record responsibilities and timing |

### Record what can be checked

A useful record states who provided the information, when, where it is documented, and who will do what next. Before signing, relevant evidence can also include confirmed terms, responsibility assignments, and an implementation handover checklist.

Evidence must correspond to the purchasing task under review. If it is absent, retain the current stage and seek a verifiable next action. 'Stay in touch' is not a stage advance. After each follow-up, ask what customer-side evidence was obtained. If none was obtained, preserve the unknown and do not increase the opportunity probability.

## Build a value case the customer can calculate

Value capability translates a product or service into the customer's profit, investment, and operating value. A feature list alone does not explain whether the investment is worthwhile.

Use a PIP, or profit improvement plan, to answer three questions: How much profit could be added or protected? Through what mechanism, and with what basis for confidence? Over what period? This is an investment explanation, separate from a quotation.

Begin by listing the operating activities the proposed solution could affect. Then use the four SPIN question types to develop the customer's account:

- **Situation:** What is the current process, and how is it measured?
- **Problem:** Where does that process encounter difficulty?
- **Implication:** What happens if the difficulty continues?
- **Need-payoff:** What value would the customer place on resolving it?

Do not insert plausible-looking customer figures. Customer-provided numbers can support a calculation once their meaning is confirmed. Seller estimates remain labeled assumptions pending customer confirmation. Record the baseline, mechanism, and timeframe so the value case can be checked.

## Prepare negotiation conditions before discussing concessions

Negotiation preparation covers leverage, time, information, limits, and alternatives. For a project spanning years, discussions may revisit price, scope, payment, delivery responsibilities, and risk allocation.

List potential exchanges before conceding. The source identifies delivery sequencing, acceptance methods, service scope, training support, priority response, and joint validation resources as possible conditions. For each, specify what can change, who can authorize it, and what customer commitment would be required in return.

Write down alternatives as well. The customer might build internally, delay, or choose another supplier. The seller might allocate resources to another project. These are possibilities to assess, not predictions about either organization.

Record limits, authority, and agreed exchanges. The objective is an executable agreement in which both parties understand their next responsibilities throughout the preparation period.

## Use a weekly opportunity checklist

Create a working matrix with the seven customer tasks across one axis and the five seller stages on the other. In each relevant intersection, record known facts, unknown information, and the next question. Update it weekly using customer behavior evidence.

Use this checklist during the review:

- Identify the customer's current purchasing task and its supporting evidence.
- Check that the seller's next action addresses that task.
- Record the source and date of each new fact.
- Use PRTABCD to identify missing decision information.
- Verify whether an apparent supporter enables a concrete next step.
- Separate confirmed responsibilities from role hypotheses.
- Check that the PIP explains profit, mechanism, and timeframe.
- Keep unconfirmed numbers labeled as assumptions.
- Review negotiation limits, alternatives, and conditional exchanges.
- Assign an owner and action for each next step.

The record should make progress traceable. A busy calendar or prominent announcement cannot substitute for evidence that the customer has completed a purchasing task.

## FAQ

### What are the five PSS5 capabilities?

They are value, relationship, questioning, systems, and negotiation capability. Together, they connect customer economics, internal support, purposeful discovery, stage management, and conditional agreement. Apply them according to the project's current obstacle, beginning with systems capability to align customer tasks and seller actions.

### Does a signed cooperation agreement indicate a supplier opportunity is ready to close?

It does not establish that conclusion. Check whether the specific purchase has approved funding, agreed requirements, a selection process, and the relevant commitments. The source's planned production date and product program provide context without proving supplier selection is complete.

### Why are visit counts insufficient?

Visits measure seller effort. They do not establish which purchasing task the customer has completed. A review notice, tender document, or testing confirmation can provide more relevant evidence of a specific customer action.

### How should a verbal promise be handled?

Record it as information awaiting verification. Ask who will approve the action, when it will occur, which criteria apply, and what materials are needed. Seek an observable step such as confirmed minutes, a review arrangement, sample receipt, or terms discussion.

### What if the customer will not share financial numbers?

Keep the relevant values unknown and continue clarifying the operating process, difficulty, consequences, and expected benefit. Do not fill gaps with invented figures. Any seller estimate must remain an explicit assumption until the customer confirms it.
