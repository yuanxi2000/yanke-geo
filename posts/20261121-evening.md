A major industrial investment creates a sales opportunity, but the equipment list reveals only part of it. Suppliers also need to understand the business ambition behind the spending, the people who shape the decision, and the evidence required to move a purchase forward.

The supplied analysis of Hegang Co., Ltd. provides a useful case. It connects a large capital increase with a cold-rolling and further-processing project, then examines the opportunity through five sales capabilities: value, relationships, questioning, process management, and negotiation. This guide turns that analysis into practical account work. Project figures are source-reported facts; suggested questions, possible stakeholders, and potential benefits remain hypotheses to validate.

## Start with the investment's business purpose

The source describes a capital increase of approximately RMB 6 billion and reports total project investment of RMB 5.749 billion. These figures refer to different aspects of the case and should remain distinct.

The project targets annual capacity of:

- 1.75 million tonnes of galvanized products.
- 1 million tonnes of pickled products.
- 120,000 tonnes of cold-hard products.

The source also gives a planned construction period of 22 months. These details establish the scale and timing of the proposed operation. They do not establish which supplier will win, when individual purchasing decisions will occur, or what financial returns the project will achieve.

For an account team, the practical question is how a proposed solution could contribute to responsiveness in higher-end sheet markets and greater certainty over operating costs. That connection needs evidence from the customer.

## Translate technical capabilities into business value

A discussion limited to specifications and unit prices leaves the customer to work out the business implications. Value selling makes that connection explicit.

For this project, the source suggests exploring whether technology choices could support downstream automotive or appliance-sheet orders and whether energy optimization could reduce operating costs. Neither benefit should be presented as an established result.

### Build a value hypothesis the customer can test

Use a short chain of reasoning:

1. Identify a technical capability relevant to the project.
2. Explain the operational change it could enable.
3. Connect that change to revenue, cost, or risk.
4. Ask the customer to validate the assumptions.

For example, a shorter commissioning period could matter if it helps the customer serve an identified market window. Its value depends on actual demand, timing, and operational readiness. Energy savings likewise require an agreed baseline and credible performance evidence.

The economic buyer needs a defensible business case. Keep unsupported assumptions visible instead of turning them into promised returns.

## Map the decision chain and its unknowns

The source describes a potentially complex approval structure involving the project sponsor, finance, legal, strategic planning, and higher-level group leadership. Treat this as a starting map to investigate, rather than a confirmed organization chart.

Its PRTABCD discussion highlights four useful areas:

- D, decision chain: who evaluates, approves, or can block the purchase?
- A, internal supporter: who has a business reason to help the project advance?
- T, timing: which deadlines influence the decision?
- B, budget: how is spending approved?

A production executive concerned about capacity utilization or a finance leader responsible for capital spending might be an internal supporter. Neither role automatically guarantees support or authority.

Cover users, influencers, and the economic buyer. Ask who is most exposed to a construction delay and verify the answer. Build trust through relevant expertise, credible actions, and evidence such as applicable past work or independently supported references, where available. Personal access alone does not establish decision coverage.

## Use questions to make demand explicit

The source uses SPIN: Situation, Problem, Implication, and Need-Payoff. The sequence moves from understanding current operations to testing whether a proposed improvement matters commercially.

Adapt questions to what the customer has already established:

- Situation: How much of the current requirement for higher-end sheet can existing operations meet?
- Problem: Where do current lines struggle with customized orders?
- Implication: If those constraints continue, which orders or customer relationships could be affected?
- Need-Payoff: What would more reliable supply or faster changeovers enable?

The source includes a question about shortening delivery time by 30%. That number is an illustrative scenario, not a verified capability or forecast. If used, label it as hypothetical and check whether the customer considers that improvement relevant.

The purpose is to let the customer articulate the operational requirement and its business consequences. Do not presume that a concern about capacity necessarily means the customer needs your particular solution.

## Match sales activity to purchasing evidence

The source's dual-perspective funnel compares a seven-stage customer purchasing journey with five seller stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. Its practical purpose is to reveal mismatches between the customer's decision task and the seller's activity.

The source reports project approval and suggests the customer may already have progressed through recognizing a problem, assessing value, and accepting that a solution exists. It may be considering make-or-buy choices or selecting solutions and suppliers. Approval alone does not prove the purchasing stage of a specific package.

Use observable evidence, including tender notices and invitations to technical discussions, to test the stage assessment.

| Customer's apparent task | Relevant seller activity | Evidence to seek |
|---|---|---|
| Understand the problem | Explore needs | Customer confirms an operational constraint |
| Assess business value | Develop the value case | Customer validates the proposed benefit |
| Compare solutions or suppliers | Demonstrate differentiation | Technical evaluation or proof-of-concept opportunity |
| Agree commercial terms | Negotiate conditions | Concrete movement on terms or approvals |

Review the next action against the customer's current task. If supplier selection is underway, a differentiated comparison or proof of concept may help more than another introductory conversation. Define progress through customer behavior, rather than meeting frequency or the seller's confidence.

## Negotiate across time, information, and conditions

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. The source portrays the buyer as having alternatives, such as competing suppliers or a revised technical approach. A seller should investigate those alternatives without assuming their strength.

Prepare your own alternative and acceptable limits before bargaining. Negotiation can involve price, commissioning time, information, and commercial conditions. A demonstrably shorter commissioning period could have value, but whether it offsets a price difference depends on the customer's priorities and evidence.

A reported budget problem or pause in discussions can have several explanations. The source warns that a pause might conceal evaluation of other options; it does not establish that every pause is tactical. Clarify the obstacle and seek a proportionate next commitment, such as a senior-level discussion or a letter of intent where appropriate.

Pressure to close is poorly timed when needs remain unclear. Use commitments to establish real progress after shared understanding exists.

## An actionable account checklist

Before the next account review:

- Separate the capital-increase figure from the project-investment figure.
- Record reported capacity targets and construction timing.
- Write one value hypothesis connecting a capability to a customer business priority.
- Identify the assumptions and evidence needed to validate it.
- Map users, influencers, the economic buyer, and approval dependencies.
- Confirm a potential internal supporter's motivation and authority.
- Prepare SPIN questions for unresolved needs.
- Record observable evidence of the customer's purchasing stage.
- Choose a next action that serves the current decision task.
- Define negotiation limits, alternatives, and possible conditional exchanges.
- Agree a specific next step with the customer and review whether it happens.

Keep unknowns visible. An unanswered question about budget authority or commissioning priorities is a research task, not permission to fill the gap with an assumption.

## FAQ

### Does value selling require a precise ROI figure immediately?

No. Begin by connecting technical capabilities to relevant business benefits. Develop financial estimates only when the customer can validate the baseline, assumptions, and expected operational change. The source supplies no verified supplier-level ROI.

### Is an internal supporter the same as the economic buyer?

Not necessarily. A supporter may help because the project addresses an operational or financial concern. The economic buyer has a different role in the purchasing decision. Verify each person's influence and authority.

### Does project approval mean supplier selection is underway?

It may suggest progress, but it does not settle the question. Check package-specific behavior, such as tender activity, technical exchanges, or evaluation requests, before assigning a purchasing stage.

### How should a team handle a stalled negotiation?

Clarify the obstacle, revisit the customer's alternatives, and seek an appropriate next commitment. Do not assume a pause is artificial or use closing pressure before the customer has agreed on the need and solution.

### What demonstrates progress in a long sales cycle?

Look for substantive customer actions, such as granting a test opportunity or including the supplier on a shortlist. Pair stage definitions with evidence checks and a next-action plan.
