Expansion news is most useful as a prompt for disciplined questions, not as proof that a deal exists. A public report about Pengding Holdings building a high-end PCB intelligent manufacturing base for AI servers and high-speed optical modules gives sellers a direction to investigate. The task is to turn that signal into account judgment: why the customer is investing, what products the capacity serves, who owns delivery, and where a supplier can offer measurable value. PSS5 provides a practical review language for that work.

## Separate public facts from unknowns

Begin with a fact table. The known items are narrow: the subject is Pengding Holdings; the public source cited is Eastern Wealth; the summary describes a high-end PCB intelligent manufacturing base aimed at AI servers and high-speed optical modules. Do not upgrade this into budget approval, project approval, supplier selection, or a confirmed timetable.

List unknown items explicitly:

- Who is driving the project, and who owns the operating result?
- Which products and process steps will the new capacity serve?
- What budget owner and approval route govern investment?
- Which departments will accept the outcome?
- What are current suppliers strong at, and what alternatives are being considered?
- Which events trigger the schedule?

Unknowns belong in the next conversation, not in internal assumptions. A public report is a lead; customer behavior is evidence.

## Use PSS5 to organize the account judgment

PSS5 has five dimensions: value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation. Use them as a checklist for preparation, not as a script to push a product. For an expansion account, the sequence matters. Establish the operating problem, map the decision landscape, ask about impact, judge the real stage, and prepare negotiation only when customer behavior supports it.

## Value: translate expansion into an operating account

An expansion cannot stop at a procurement list. Ask what the new line must fix: yield, delivery, energy use, equipment utilization, or process stability. A supplier becomes relevant when it can convert its contribution into revenue, cost, cash occupation, or payback using the customer's own data. Do not fill in the customer's numbers for them.

Make the value usable inside the customer's organization. Equipment suppliers should show how new equipment reduces downtime. Material suppliers should show how their product supports high-speed signal performance. Service providers should show how they shorten line ramp-up. Each claim needs a customer-verifiable metric and a responsible owner. Product parameters are supporting detail, not the main argument. This is how an operations manager can use business language to compete for funding and approval.

## Relationship: close blind spots with PRTABCD

PRTABCD covers seven types of decision information: past, need, timing, arm, budget, competition and alternatives, and the decision chain. For Pengding Holdings, write down what is known first, then separate the gaps.

A is Arm, meaning the person or group in the customer organization who embraces your approach and will speak for it. D is Decision, meaning a multi-person, multi-department decision chain, not a single decision maker. Record information source, known facts, and open questions in an evidence table. Ask who raises the capacity need, who owns process results, who controls budget, who can veto, and who can advocate internally. Do not guess roles from job titles. Relationship strength is proven by behavior and cross-checked information.

## Questioning: use SPIN to deepen the expansion conversation

SPIN offers four question types: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff. Keep situation questions necessary. Ask which products the new capacity serves and where the current line is constrained. Ask which factor is most likely to slow ramp-up: equipment, materials, process, or people. Then ask how one downtime event or one bad batch would affect customer delivery and cash collection. Finally ask what the customer would protect or improve if the issue were resolved.

The goal is to let the customer articulate hidden costs. For example: Which products will the added capacity serve? Which step is the bottleneck? If ramp-up slips, which customer order and cash cycle are exposed? If a supplier can control the problem, which internal metric will be used for acceptance? Each answer should shape the next question and produce a hypothesis to verify. Avoid turning the conversation into an interrogation.

## System: judge the stage with a two-view funnel

The system view combines two axes. The sales side has five stages: contact, need, solution, negotiation, and close. The customer procurement side has seven stages, from discovering a problem to committing to a contract. An expansion report may indicate that the customer is considering investment, but it does not prove the account is already in negotiation.

Use customer behavior as stage evidence. Has the customer opened production data? Invited the supplier into process validation? Clarified budget and acceptance standards? Brought legal and procurement into term discussions? Without such evidence, heat is only heat. Sales actions must match the customer's current decision task. Quoting while the customer is still judging value pulls the conversation toward price. Repeating background while the customer is selecting a solution signals poor preparation. Mark evidence first, then choose the next action.

## Negotiation: prepare alternatives before discussing terms

Before commercial terms, prepare power, time, information, and alternatives. BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best fallback action a negotiator can take if no agreement is reached. Prepare an executable alternative, bottom line, authority limit, interests, and a record. BATNA is not a threat. Do not promise delivery dates or prices without authorization.

For an expansion account, clarify acceptance standards, payment terms, risk sharing, and change mechanisms before trading concessions. Every concession should buy back an executable condition: clearer acceptance language, a more stable demand forecast, a clearer responsibility boundary, or faster decision feedback. Cutting price without conditions turns expansion work into low-margin hardship. Relying only on relationships without terms leaves delivery risk for later. Negotiation should aim at a sustainable agreement that still works after signature.

## Compact PSS5 comparison

| Dimension | Core question | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|
| Value | How does the expansion improve an operating result? | Customer-owned baseline, target metric, owner, payback logic |
| Relationship | Who embraces the proposal, and who decides? | Arm, budget owner, veto roles, decision-chain map |
| Questioning | What problem, impact, and payoff does the customer name? | Bottleneck, delivery or cash impact, acceptance metric |
| System | What stage is the customer actually in? | Data access, process validation, budget clarity, legal involvement |
| Negotiation | What fallback and trade conditions exist? | BATNA, authority, acceptance, payment, risk, change terms |

## Actionable checklist

### Daily account checklist

- Record public facts and unknowns in separate columns; never label a guess as a customer fact.
- Use value to list the operating metrics the expansion may affect, then ask the customer to verify with their data.
- Use PRTABCD to identify the arm, budget owner, veto roles, and decision-chain gaps.
- Use SPIN to ask about the bottleneck, impact, and acceptance metric, not to collect unrelated background.
- Use the two-view funnel to mark customer evidence before deciding whether to propose, quote, or negotiate.
- If the customer has not opened data, stay in questioning and validation.
- If the customer invites process validation, move into solution co-creation.
- If the customer discusses payment, acceptance, and risk sharing, prepare negotiation with a BATNA and authority limits.

## FAQ

Q: What should a seller do first after seeing the expansion report?

A: Record the subject, public source, and known facts. Then list unknowns as questions. Do not assume the project is approved or procurement is underway. Use value to identify possible operating metrics and PRTABCD to find relationship gaps. Ask the customer to validate.

Q: How does value become an operating account?

A: Translate equipment, material, or service contribution into outcomes the customer can verify, such as less downtime, stable yield, more controllable delivery, or lower cash occupation. The customer confirms each number with their own data.

Q: What do A and D mean in PRTABCD?

A: A is Arm, the person or group who embraces you and can advocate for the proposal. D is Decision, a multi-person decision chain. Both need evidence from behavior and cross-checked information.

Q: Why not treat the expansion report as a sales opportunity?

A: It shows direction, not budget approval, supplier choice, or timetable. Treating heat as opportunity can skip needs confirmation and decision-chain mapping. The safer path is questioning, relationship mapping, and stage evidence.

Q: What should negotiation preparation include?

A: Power, time, information, and BATNA. Define the fallback, bottom line, authority, interests, and record. For expansion work, put acceptance, payment, risk sharing, and change mechanisms on the table, and exchange concessions for executable conditions.
