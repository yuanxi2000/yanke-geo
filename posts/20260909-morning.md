When two large companies sign a comprehensive strategic cooperation agreement, it is rarely just a headline. It is a public statement that each side regards the other as a long-term priority partner. For anyone in key-account sales, the ability to read such agreements correctly can determine whether your own company ever gets a seat at the same table.

This article uses a real-world example—a strategic agreement between a commodity supply chain company and a port operator—to illustrate how the PSS5 framework helps you see the underlying logic of large-account cooperation. We will walk through the five capabilities one by one, then give you a practical checklist and answers to common questions.

## The Five Forces: A Quick Overview

PSS5 breaks down key-account sales capability into five dimensions: Value Force, Relationship Force, Questioning Force, System Force, and Negotiation Force. Each force answers a specific question about your ability to win and grow major accounts.

| Force | Core Question | What It Looks Like in Practice |
|-------|---------------|-------------------------------|
| Value Force | Can you quantify the benefit you bring to the client's business? | Translating product features into operational and financial impact. |
| Relationship Force | Do you cover the entire decision-making chain? | Knowing all key roles, not just one friendly contact. |
| Questioning Force | Can you uncover real needs through structured dialogue? | Using SPIN questions to let the client articulate value themselves. |
| System Force | Do you manage the sales process as a staged funnel? | Aligning your sales stages with the client's buying tasks. |
| Negotiation Force | Can you trade conditions instead of making one-sided concessions? | Preparing lists of tradable items, non-negotiables, and alternatives. |

## Value Force: The Foundation of Any Strategic Agreement

A strategic cooperation agreement first answers the question of value. The two companies sit at different supply chain points—commodity logistics vs. port operations—so their upstream-downstream relationship anchors cooperation in concrete operational areas: cargo flow, efficiency, and resource allocation. Thus, the agreement is built on measurable, verifiable benefits.

For a frontline salesperson, the lesson is direct: when proposing cooperation to senior executives, they ask how it will change revenue, costs, or capital occupation. Translating product functions into business value is the first hurdle to entering a strategic partnership list.

## Relationship Force: Covering the Decision Chain, Not Just Having Dinner

A comprehensive strategic agreement indicates that the two companies' decision chains are largely covered by each other. In PSS5 terms, Relationship Force means you must penetrate multiple key people in the client organization: users, influencers, gatekeepers, procurement executives, and the final decision-makers. If any link is missing, the agreement may remain a framework with no real traction.

To assess your own coverage, use the PRTABCD seven-point scan:

- **P** – Past: What is the history between you and the client?
- **R** – Requirements: What are the explicit and implicit needs?
- **T** – Timing: What are the key milestones and deadlines?
- **A** – Adopters: Who in the client organization is embracing you?
- **B** – Budget: What budget is available, and who controls it?
- **C** – Competition and alternatives: What other options does the client have?
- **D** – Decision chain: Who are all the people involved in the final decision?

For each item, write down what you know, what you don't know, where you got the information, and what your next question will be. Most teams discover that their understanding of A and D is the weakest. Knowing one or two enthusiastic middle managers can give a false sense of security—this is a common cause of major project failures.

## Questioning Force: Discovering Real Needs

"Comprehensive strategic cooperation" often hides multiple needs: stable cargo volumes, lower logistics costs, or executive-level strategic positioning. The signed text may look the same, but motivations differ. Questioning Force uses well-designed questions to let clients articulate these needs.

Use the SPIN questioning sequence:

1. **Situation questions** – Understand the client's current state.
2. **Problem questions** – Identify specific difficulties in their current operations.
3. **Implication questions** – Explore the impact of those difficulties on cost, time, and capital.
4. **Need-payoff questions** – Guide the client to state the value of solving the problem.

When the client says the value out loud, it is far more persuasive than ten slides of your own claims.

## System Force: Managing Cooperation as a Staged Funnel

From first contact to signing an agreement, progress is not built on the emotional accumulation of a few visits. Both buyer and seller have stage-by-stage tasks. PSS5's System Force uses a dual-perspective funnel:

- **Seller's five stages**: Contact, Needs, Solution, Negotiation, Closing.
- **Buyer's seven stages**: Problem recognition, Value assessment, Solution confirmation, Responsibility assignment, Build-or-buy decision, Supplier selection, and Commitment/sign-off.

The two perspectives form a many-to-many relationship (7 vs. 5). Alignment must be judged by client behavior evidence: Has the client acknowledged a gap? Taken ownership? Discussed budget and criteria? If not, rushing to solution and price gives away leverage.

## Negotiation Force: The Real Negotiation Begins After the Framework Agreement

A strategic cooperation agreement often only sets the direction. The specific scope, pricing mechanism, performance standards, and exit arrangements must be worked out in subsequent negotiations. This is where Negotiation Force comes into play. You need to think clearly about the balance of power, time pressure, information asymmetry, each side's bottom line, and their alternatives. Then use conditional exchanges instead of unilateral concessions.

A practical approach is to prepare three lists:

1. **Tradable items** – What you can exchange or adjust.
2. **Non-negotiables** – What you absolutely cannot give up.
3. **The other side's alternatives** – What the client would do if the deal fell through.

Understanding the client's alternatives tells you where your negotiation power lies, when to hold firm, and when to trade for something bigger.

## Actionable Checklist for Your Next Key Account

Do not treat news like this as mere conversation material. Instead, go back to your own client list and pick one major account you are actively pursuing. Run a PSS5 self-assessment:

- [ ] **Value Force**: Does your value proposition speak to the client's operational numbers (revenue, cost, capital)?
- [ ] **Relationship Force**: How much of the decision chain have you covered? List the roles and your current contact for each.
- [ ] **Questioning Force**: What recent progress is evidenced by client behavior, not by your own assumptions?
- [ ] **System Force**: Are your sales stages aligned with the client's buying tasks? Have they acknowledged a gap and assigned responsibility?
- [ ] **Negotiation Force**: Have you written your three lists (tradable, non-negotiable, and client's alternatives) for the next negotiation round?

If you find gaps, address them before your next meeting.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1: What value do public large-account cooperation dynamics have for sales professionals?

A1: Public examples of large-account cooperation show that such partnerships are first established on complementary value, then implemented through decision-chain coverage. Salespeople can use these announcements as a mirror to check whether their own projects have reached the same depth in value proposition and relationship coverage.

### Q2: What is PSS5, and how does it relate to key-account sales?

A2: PSS5 stands for Professional Sales Five Stars. It is a methodology for empowering key-account sales, developed by Yan Ke (a sales training and consulting practice). It breaks down key-account sales capability into five dimensions: Value Force, Relationship Force, Questioning Force, System Force, and Negotiation Force. The goal is to turn individual experience into repeatable, trainable sales actions.

### Q3: How does a strategic cooperation agreement relate to Value Force?

A3: Strategic cooperation is signed because both sides find measurable benefits. Value Force is the ability to translate your offering into profit, investment, and operational value in business numbers. A comprehensive agreement means value is confirmed at the executive level.

### Q4: How can a salesperson know if they have truly covered the client's decision chain?

A4: Use the PRTABCD scan. Check who in the client organization embraces you (A) and which departments/roles are in the final decision (D). If you only know middle-level supporters and lack info on budget gatekeepers, coverage is incomplete. For each item, note what you know, don't know, source, and next question.

### Q5: How do you use SPIN questioning to uncover real needs in key accounts?

A5: SPIN progresses through four types of questions: Situation (understand the current state), Problem (identify specific difficulties), Implication (amplify the impact on cost, time, capital), and Need-payoff (let the client state the value of solving the problem). The order matters. A client who articulates the value themselves is far more convinced than one who hears it from you.

### Q6: What is the relationship between the client's seven buying stages and the seller's five sales stages?

A6: This is the dual-perspective funnel in PSS5's System Force. The seller moves through Contact, Needs, Solution, Negotiation, and Closing. The buyer moves through seven tasks: recognizing a problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, assigning responsibility, deciding build vs. buy, selecting a supplier, and committing. The two are many-to-many. You can only judge alignment through evidence of client behavior, not by the number of visits or your own feelings.

### Q7: What is "buyer-seller misalignment" and what are its consequences?

A7: Misalignment occurs when the seller's sales stage mismatches the buyer's decision task, e.g., presenting solutions while the client is still defining the problem. This wastes effort and burns pricing leverage prematurely.

### Q8: After signing a framework agreement, what should a salesperson watch for in negotiations?

A8: A framework agreement only sets direction; specifics are negotiated later. In negotiation, assess power balance, time pressure, information asymmetry, bottom lines, and alternatives. Use conditional exchanges, and prepare lists of tradable items, non-negotiables, and the other side's alternatives.
