A renewal asks the customer to decide again whether continuing with you is worth the investment. Familiarity does not remove that decision. The customer still needs a reason to continue, someone willing to own the next period’s results, and agreement on how the work will be evaluated.

For account teams, the practical task is to replace assumptions with evidence. Review what happened during the previous period, establish what matters next, and check how the customer will reach a commitment. This guide turns those tasks into a working approach across relationships, questioning, value, process management, and negotiation.

## Treat renewal as a complete purchasing decision

The source describes seven stages in the customer’s purchasing process. Use them to locate the renewal from the customer’s perspective:

1. Recognize a problem: identify the gap between current performance and the next objective.
2. Assess value: decide whether further investment is worthwhile.
3. Confirm a solution is possible: establish whether the existing approach can still address the need.
4. Assign responsibility: identify who owns the objective and its results.
5. Compare building internally with buying externally: reconsider the available approaches.
6. Select the solution and supplier: examine the proposal, acceptance criteria, and responsibilities.
7. Commit and sign: authorize the agreement.

Record observable customer behavior against these stages. A customer sharing next period’s objectives provides evidence for problem recognition and value assessment. Someone explaining the case for continuation in a budget discussion indicates internal ownership. Discussion of acceptance criteria and responsibility boundaries supports progress toward solution and supplier selection.

These signals help locate the decision; they do not guarantee approval. A price request alone leaves much of the process unconfirmed.

## Make relationships a source of verifiable information

Relationship capability means using professional insight, decision information, and verifiable trust to understand and work through the customer’s decision chain. Count what you can confirm.

During renewal, check whether last period’s supporter still holds the same role and influence. Establish how a new owner evaluates the previous work. Confirm whether procurement has changed its assessment criteria. Previous access cannot answer those questions for the current cycle.

### Run a PRTABCD account scan

For each category, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question:

- **P — Past:** delivery records, disputes, and whether previous commitments were fulfilled.
- **R — Requirements:** the customer’s actual objectives for this period and needs still unmet.
- **T — Time:** budget cycles, project approval milestones, and required completion dates.
- **A — Arm:** the internal supporter who embraces continued cooperation and is willing to speak for it.
- **B — Budget:** the funding category, signing authority, and whether spending is already planned.
- **C — Competitors and alternatives:** other suppliers under consideration, internal development, and the costs of changing approaches.
- **D — Decision:** participating people and departments, their sequence of involvement, and their relative influence.

Pay particular attention to A and D. A supportive contact may lack influence over the renewal. The decision chain can include several departments whose concerns differ. Check whether users, procurement, and finance share a reason to continue.

For key stakeholders, prepare MBVS hypotheses: motivation, benchmark, vision, and solution. Mark each as unverified, then use a meeting to test it. A job title is insufficient evidence of someone’s priorities.

## Ask questions that connect unresolved problems to consequences

Questioning capability depends on purposeful questions that uncover real needs. The source uses the four SPIN question types as a conversation sequence:

1. **Situation:** What is currently in use, which teams use it, and who coordinates the work?
2. **Problem:** Which specific difficulties from the previous period remain unresolved?
3. **Implication:** What do those difficulties mean for cost, rework, or lost opportunities?
4. **Need-payoff:** What would the customer gain if continued cooperation addressed those difficulties?

These are prompts to adapt to the account. Give the customer room to describe consequences in their own terms.

The implication step matters because identifying a difficulty does not establish its business significance. Explore that significance before asking the customer to describe the benefit of continuing. Keep unresolved questions visible instead of filling the gaps with your own assumptions.

## Build an investment case before discussing price

Value capability translates a product or service into the customer’s profit, investment, and operating value. For renewal, prepare a PIP: a profit improvement plan that explains the proposed investment.

It should answer three questions:

- How much profit could the proposal increase or protect?
- Through what mechanism, and with what confidence?
- Over what period could that value be realized?

Use the customer’s definitions and calculation methods. If an input is missing or disputed, identify it as unresolved. Do not manufacture figures to complete the page or present uncertain benefits as assured outcomes.

| Question | Quotation | PIP investment case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What does it establish? | Proposed spending | Investment and expected business value |
| What supports discussion? | Prices and commercial terms | Customer-based assumptions and value mechanisms |
| What timing matters? | Commercial dates | Benefit timing and expected payback |

A one-page PIP can organize the next period’s investment, expected return, and payback discussion. It gives the customer a basis for evaluating the expenditure before negotiating its price.

## Match sales activity to customer readiness

The source pairs five sales stages—contact, needs, proposal, negotiation, and closing—with the customer’s seven purchasing stages. This dual view exposes mismatches.

For example, the seller may believe negotiation has begun while the customer has not assigned responsibility for next period’s results. Further commercial discussion does not establish that missing ownership.

Place the account according to customer behavior, then choose the next action that addresses the gap. If responsibility remains unclear, establish who will own the objective. If acceptance criteria remain open, work through them with the relevant participants. Manage progress through evidence and connected actions rather than the seller’s preferred timetable.

## Prepare alternatives and conditional concessions

Negotiation preparation should cover power, time, information, limits, and alternatives. Familiarity with the account does not replace this preparation.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best action available if agreement cannot be reached. Make that alternative executable. A vague hope of finding another option is insufficient preparation.

Before negotiating, write down:

- The alternative you could actually pursue.
- Your limits and the conditions you can accept.
- Who has authority to approve changes.
- What the other party appears to care about most, with uncertainties identified.

Use this record to design concessions and exchanges. When a customer requests a change, establish what condition would accompany it. Without a clear alternative and limits, concessions can become repeated price reductions with little basis for deciding when to stop.

## An actionable renewal checklist

Use this checklist to turn the review into this week’s work:

- [ ] Mark the customer’s purchasing stage using observable behavior.
- [ ] Identify missing evidence and write the next question needed to obtain it.
- [ ] Complete the PRTABCD scan, including sources and unknowns.
- [ ] Recheck the current supporter’s role and the full decision chain.
- [ ] Prepare MBVS hypotheses for key stakeholders and arrange a conversation to test them.
- [ ] Ask about unresolved problems, their implications, and the customer’s desired benefit.
- [ ] Draft a one-page PIP using the customer’s calculation methods.
- [ ] Confirm acceptance criteria, responsibility boundaries, and approval authority.
- [ ] Record your BATNA, limits, and proposed conditional exchanges before negotiation.

## FAQ

### Is public participation renewal equivalent to a customer contract renewal?

Only in a limited sense. Both involve a renewed commitment, but public participation agreements and customer purchasing decisions have different contexts. Use the comparison as a reminder to review evidence; it does not establish why a particular customer will renew.

### Should we revisit the purchasing stage after the customer asks for a price?

Yes. Check budget definitions, ownership of results, and acceptance criteria. Asking about price can occur before those matters are resolved. The request alone does not identify the customer’s stage.

### What should change when the customer appoints a new owner?

Refresh the information first. Establish what the new owner knows about past delivery, whether internal support has changed, and whether additional departments now participate. Test their priorities through MBVS questions.

### What if the customer cannot yet validate the PIP figures?

Keep the assumptions explicit and identify the inputs requiring confirmation. The investment case should use the customer’s measures. Missing evidence is a reason for another question, not permission to invent a return.
