Technical capability does not automatically establish commercial viability. In a complex account, a solution must also survive the customer's operating conditions, satisfy different decision makers, and provide enough evidence to support a commitment. A production trial can connect those requirements, provided it is designed around a buying decision.

The supplied account of AGIBOT robots entering a semiconductor packaging and testing operation illustrates this transition. Its practical lesson is to manage progress through observable customer behavior. Installing equipment is a milestone; understanding what the customer has verified, accepted, and still needs to resolve is the sales task.

## What the reported trial establishes

The source attributes the underlying event to Eastmoney reporting dated November 23; the supplied material does not specify a year. It says an initial group of AGIBOT's Genie G2 robots joined an operating production line at JCET's automotive electronics factory in Shanghai Lingang. The robots completed a trial in a chip testing workshop, primarily handling tray loading and unloading.

That places the equipment inside a real production setting, where process complexity and operating variables make stability important. It offers a more substantive basis for evaluation than a presentation or an isolated demonstration.

The available account does not provide trial duration, measured reliability, financial returns, contract terms, or evidence of a wider rollout. Treat the event as evidence of operational evaluation, not proof of a completed commercial agreement or a demonstrated return on investment.

## Track the seller's activity and the buyer's decision separately

The source uses a dual-perspective funnel: five seller stages considered alongside seven customer purchasing stages. The purpose is to expose differences between what the seller is doing and what the customer is ready to decide.

From the seller's perspective, the trial can be interpreted as late solution development, approaching negotiation and a possible agreement. From the buyer's perspective, the source places it toward the latter part of stage six, solution and supplier selection, potentially nearing stage seven, commitment and contracting. These are analytical interpretations of public information. The customer's internal stage is not independently established.

For your own account, ask which purchasing question remains open. Is the customer still assessing value, comparing suppliers, assigning internal responsibility, or preparing to commit? A trial should address that specific uncertainty.

If the seller is discussing contract terms while the customer still needs value evidence, the process is misaligned. Return to the unresolved question and agree on what would answer it. More meetings alone do not establish progress.

## Give each buying role a reason to evaluate

A complex trial serves several audiences. The source distinguishes technical buyers, user buyers, and economic buyers. Their concerns overlap, but they require different evidence.

| Buying role | Main concern | Useful trial focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical buyer | Feasibility and operating stability | Performance under relevant cleanliness, precision, and production conditions |
| User buyer | Usability and operational burden | Loading workflows, ease of operation, and maintenance demands |
| Economic buyer | Potential returns and controllable risk | Evidence relevant to investment assumptions and implementation exposure |

Use these roles to organize conversations, not to assume that three individuals hold all authority. The source also highlights a decision chain that may span production, quality, and IT. Determine which functions actually participate in the account.

A technical acceptance alone does not resolve the user workflow or the investment case. Make those remaining questions visible.

## Design the trial around a decision

### Agree on success before equipment starts

Begin with the operating problem. For tray handling, the source suggests investigating the limitations of the existing manual process: where efficiency is constrained, what consequences follow, and which risks matter. Do not assume that automation is valuable merely because it is technically possible.

Translate those concerns into criteria that the customer can evaluate. Depending on the agreed scope, these might concern faults, handling efficiency, stable operation, ease of use, or maintenance burden. The source supplies no numerical targets; customer and supplier must define appropriate thresholds themselves.

Before starting, agree on:

- The process and operating conditions included in the trial.
- The question each participating buying role needs answered.
- The measures and acceptance thresholds that define success.
- Who will review the evidence and determine whether it is sufficient.
- The next decision the results are intended to support.

This turns a general request to test the product into a bounded evaluation. Without agreed criteria, the parties may finish the same trial with incompatible views of its significance.

Disclose limitations early. Explain which conditions the trial cannot cover and where further validation may be necessary. The source recommends candor about those limits as part of managing expectations and risk. A narrow successful trial should support a narrow conclusion.

## Make customer evidence the basis for progress

Separate supplier actions from customer conclusions. Delivering equipment, conducting a demonstration, and completing a trial describe activities. Customer progress requires evidence that a relevant uncertainty has been resolved.

In practical reviews, look for customer confirmation of success criteria, participation by the relevant functions, an agreed interpretation of results, and clarity about the next decision. These are ways to apply the source's emphasis on customer-side evidence; they are not reported facts about the AGIBOT project.

Also record what remains unresolved. A user may find the workflow acceptable while the economic buyer still needs to examine the investment assumptions. Alternatively, performance may appear promising while internal responsibility remains unclear.

Neither situation warrants treating the whole account as ready to sign. Match the next action to the remaining obstacle. If a critical role has not evaluated the result, arrange that evaluation. If responsibility is unclear, resolve ownership before pressing for commercial commitment.

## Build support through coordinated expertise

The source recommends finding a department or production line willing to host a pilot and developing an internal supporter. That supporter can help the evaluation proceed, but should not be assumed to represent every stakeholder or possess final purchasing authority.

Map who supports the proposal, who questions it, which functions influence selection, and what budget and timing constraints apply. Keep uncertainties explicit rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

Coordinate sales, technical support, and delivery expertise around those needs. Relationship strength in a demanding manufacturing account includes understanding process problems, communicating credibly with engineers, and responding to technical questions. Personal familiarity alone does not answer production concerns.

Give the customer a coherent explanation of the proposed scope, evidence, and limitations across those conversations.

## An actionable trial checklist

Use this checklist before launch and again before advancing the opportunity:

- [ ] State the customer's unresolved purchasing question.
- [ ] Identify the technical, user, and economic buying roles.
- [ ] Confirm the host department and internal supporter.
- [ ] Map decision authority, budget, and timing uncertainties.
- [ ] Agree on scope, operating conditions, and measurable success criteria.
- [ ] Assign responsibility for reviewing results.
- [ ] Document exclusions and untested conditions.
- [ ] Review findings with the relevant customer functions.
- [ ] Separate accepted conclusions from open questions.
- [ ] Agree on the next decision and any further validation required.

An unchecked item is a prompt for investigation. Its significance depends on whether it prevents the customer from making the intended decision.

## FAQ

### Does completing a trial mean the sale is nearly closed?

It may indicate movement toward a decision, but completion alone does not establish readiness to contract. In the supplied case, proximity to negotiation or commitment is an interpretation. Confirm the customer's remaining selection, ownership, and approval questions directly.

### What if the customer will not define success?

Return to the problem the trial is supposed to investigate. Ask which operating constraint matters, what its consequences are, and what evidence would make the proposed solution credible. Without that agreement, the trial's decision value remains uncertain.

### How should a disappointing trial be handled?

Review the cause with the customer. The source distinguishes technical mismatch from process adjustment problems and recommends joint analysis, revised expectations, and a phased improvement plan where appropriate. Do not relabel an unmet criterion as success. Agree on what must change before another evaluation is useful.

### When should negotiation enter the discussion?

Use the customer's readiness as the guide. The source warns against pushing price or contracts before internal responsibilities are settled. Understand alternatives as well: maintaining the current process or selecting another automation supplier may be options for the buyer. These are possibilities to investigate, not verified choices in this case.
