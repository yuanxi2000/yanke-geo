Large industrial sales opportunities advance through a sequence of customer decisions. A successful visit, a detailed proposal, or a confident CRM update does not establish that the buyer has completed the decisions needed to move forward. The practical task is to understand what the customer is deciding, identify evidence of progress, and organize sales activity around that work.

The supplied analysis of Zhongyan Chemical's natural soda ash project offers a useful setting for this approach. Its central principle is a dual-perspective funnel: track the seller's five stages alongside the customer's seven purchasing stages. Use the relationship between them to expose gaps, coordinate stakeholders, and keep the opportunity grounded in observable customer behavior.

## Understand the project without overstating the facts

The source describes a mineral-resource development project with planned annual natural soda ash production capacity of five million tonnes. It reports a construction timetable expressed as production within two years and full capacity within three years, with commissioning expected around mid-2028.

These are reported plans, not verified completion outcomes. The source also says construction began in June of its publication year, but the supplied material does not establish that year. A durable account should therefore avoid turning that relative date into a specific calendar claim.

For sales planning, the relevant features are scale, duration, and purchasing complexity. Equipment, technical services, and raw materials create different procurement requirements within the wider project. A project-level commissioning target provides context, but each opportunity still needs its own purchasing milestones and decision evidence.

## Track both sides of the buying process

The seller's five stages are contact, needs discovery, solution development, negotiation, and closing. They describe how the sales team organizes its work.

The customer's seven stages describe a different progression:

1. Recognize a problem.
2. Assess whether solving it has sufficient value.
3. Confirm that a feasible solution exists.
4. Establish responsibility for the outcome.
5. Decide whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Select a solution and supplier.
7. Commit and sign.

These perspectives overlap, but they do not form a rigid conversion chart. Several customer decisions may occur within one sales stage. The team may also discover that its internal stage label is behind, or ahead of, the customer's actual work.

| Seller stage | Customer decisions often involved | Practical focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | Recognize a problem | Establish whether the customer acknowledges a meaningful gap. |
| Needs discovery | Assess value; confirm feasibility | Explore the problem and test whether a viable response exists. |
| Solution development | Assign responsibility; build or buy | Clarify ownership and the case for external procurement. |
| Negotiation | Select solution and supplier | Work through price, scope, timing, and risk. |
| Closing | Commit and sign | Confirm approvals, terms, and implementation handoff. |

Treat these associations as prompts for investigation. For example, a buyer already selecting suppliers may need a different response from a seller still conducting basic discovery. Conversely, discussing price while the customer is still deciding whether a problem matters can leave the underlying decision unresolved.

## Make customer evidence the basis of stage reviews

A useful opportunity review asks what the customer has done since the previous review and which purchasing decision that action supports. Seller activity provides context; customer behavior establishes whether the buying process has advanced.

Evidence cited in the source includes a formal request for technical specifications, a request for proposals, a cross-functional evaluation meeting, a senior-management meeting, or access to internal budget documentation. Each item needs interpretation. Its existence should support a particular stage judgment, rather than automatically trigger a move toward closing.

### Turn activity updates into decision updates

Replace a statement such as “the proposal was presented” with a more useful review:

- Which customer decision was the proposal intended to support?
- What response or action demonstrates progress on that decision?
- Which responsibility, approval, or selection issue remains open?
- What customer action would provide the next piece of evidence?

CRM percentages can summarize a team's judgment, but they do not supply the missing evidence. A confident percentage cannot resolve uncertainty about the buyer's responsibilities, budget approval, or commitment.

## Map the decision chain and the internal champion

The source uses PRTABCD to identify information gaps, emphasizing three elements: D for Decision, A for Arm, and T for Time. The supplied material does not define the remaining letters, so this guide uses only those three elements.

**Decision:** Identify the people involved in technical assessment, purchasing, financial approval, and senior oversight. The source describes potential involvement from technical directors, procurement managers, finance directors, and group leadership. These are roles to investigate, not a confirmed organization chart for the project.

**Arm:** Find the internal champion: someone who actively supports the proposed approach and is willing to seek resources or support inside the customer organization. The source gives possible examples, including an engineer addressing a technical problem or a business leader pursuing an operating objective. A friendly contact alone does not establish this role.

**Time:** Work backward from the customer's relevant deadlines. The reported commissioning target can frame the discussion, but individual purchases require their own timing. Confirm when specifications, evaluations, approvals, and commitments must occur. Missing a purchasing window can remove an opportunity from consideration even when the wider project remains active.

Together, these questions reveal whether the team understands who must decide, who can help internally, and when action is required.

## Translate technical capabilities into a testable value case

Process discipline needs a value proposition that the customer accepts. Technical features alone may leave decision-makers without a clear explanation of why the purchase matters.

The source illustrates this through process improvements that could reduce energy consumption per unit of output. Such a benefit should be expressed in operating and financial terms, with the customer validating the assumptions. It should not become a claimed saving simply because a sales presentation describes it.

For a state-owned industrial buyer, the source also highlights compliance, stability, safety, environmental requirements, technological control, and total cost of ownership. Use these as areas for discovery. Establish which considerations actually matter to the purchasing decision and how the customer evaluates them.

Test the value case while the buyer is assessing value and confirming feasibility. If the customer does not accept the early rationale, a more detailed sales schedule will not resolve that underlying disagreement.

## Reassess the buying process when the project slips

A delay calls for a new diagnosis of the customer's position. Determine whether the obstacle comes from an unresolved internal decision or an external factor. Then revisit the purchasing stage affected by that obstacle.

If responsibility remains unclear, further work on ownership may be necessary. If internal support has weakened, the team may need to identify another champion. Senior involvement may help where the decision chain requires it. These are possible responses to the diagnosed issue, not automatic escalation steps.

Update the sales plan and timing around the customer's revised decision work. Carrying forward the old stage assessment can conceal the reason progress stopped.

## Actionable opportunity-review checklist

Use this checklist before advancing an opportunity or committing additional resources:

- Record the seller's current stage and the customer's current purchasing decisions.
- Attach customer behavior that supports the assessment.
- Identify any mismatch between the proposed sales action and the buyer's immediate task.
- Map technical, procurement, financial, and senior decision roles.
- Confirm whether an active internal champion exists.
- Check ownership of the intended business outcome.
- Establish the relevant purchasing deadlines and dependencies.
- Validate the value case with the customer, including its assumptions.
- Define the next observable customer action.
- Reassess stages and timing when circumstances change.

## FAQ

### Are the five sales stages equivalent to the seven purchasing stages?

No. Their relationship is many-to-many. Value assessment and feasibility confirmation may both occur during needs discovery, while responsibility and sourcing decisions may overlap solution development. Use the mapping to guide questions rather than enforce a fixed sequence.

### What makes someone an internal champion?

An internal champion actively supports the approach and is willing to seek resources or backing within the organization. Investigate what business or technical objective motivates that support. Access, friendliness, or frequent conversation alone does not establish advocacy.

### What counts as real progress?

Customer action that supports a relevant purchasing decision. Examples in the source include formal procurement requests, cross-functional evaluations, senior meetings, and budget information. Interpret the evidence in context and identify what it leaves unresolved.

### How should the team respond when the buyer moves faster than expected?

Reassess the customer's decision stage and the gaps in the seller's understanding. If supplier selection is already underway, determine what the evaluation requires and which information is missing. Continuing an unchanged discovery routine may fail to address the customer's current task.

### Does a strong value case replace process management?

No. Value gives the customer a reason to act; process management tracks the decisions needed to act. The source treats them as complementary. Neither an unsupported benefit claim nor a carefully maintained funnel establishes customer commitment.
