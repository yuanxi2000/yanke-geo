A customer's expansion announcement creates a reason to investigate, but it does not establish readiness to buy. For key-account sales teams, the useful question is which decision the customer is working through and what evidence would demonstrate progress. A proposal, technical discussion, or price negotiation should follow that decision task.

Anhui Jiguang's preparations for a second-phase TAC optical film project illustrate this discipline. The available information supports a sales planning hypothesis: the customer may be approaching solution and supplier selection. It does not confirm that stage. This guide explains how to test the hypothesis, identify decision gaps, develop a verifiable solution, and prepare for negotiation without outrunning the customer.

## Separate the reported event from the sales hypothesis

According to the supplied source's account of an Eastmoney report dated November 2, 2026, Anhui Jiguang entered into further agreements with the local development zone's administrative committee and other parties and began preparations for its second-phase project. The source describes the first-phase TAC optical film production line as having annual capacity of approximately 60 million square meters.

That first-phase figure must not become an assumed second-phase target. The supplied material provides no confirmed second-phase investment amount, capacity target, or implementation timetable. Its broader context is continued capacity expansion amid demand for domestic supply in the display panel industry chain.

For sales planning, the announcement suggests that the customer may have accepted the feasibility of a solution and may be moving toward comparing solutions and suppliers. These remain hypotheses until supported by customer behavior, such as defined evaluation criteria, a verification process, or active supplier comparisons.

## Use two funnels to match action to the buying task

A sales pipeline describes the seller's work. A purchasing process describes the customer's decisions. Tracking both helps expose situations where activity looks productive but fails to advance the customer's current task.

The five sales stages are contact, needs assessment, solution development, negotiation, and closing. The seven customer purchasing stages are:

1. Identify the problem.
2. Assess its value or significance.
3. Confirm that a viable solution exists.
4. Establish responsibility and accountability.
5. Decide whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Select a solution and supplier.
7. Commit and sign.

These perspectives should be assessed separately. A seller calling an opportunity a negotiation does not establish that the customer has completed supplier selection. Equally, continuing broad needs discovery may be poorly matched to a customer already evaluating specific solutions.

If evidence confirms that Anhui Jiguang is selecting solutions and suppliers, the corresponding sales work is solution development: jointly defining the solution approach, success criteria, and verification method. The project announcement alone cannot justify that stage assignment.

## Look for customer evidence before recording progress

Progress should depend on evidence that the customer has completed a decision task. Meeting counts, repeated visits, and seller confidence cannot substitute for that evidence.

| Customer evidence to seek | Purchasing task it helps assess | Appropriate sales action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Defined capacity goals and timing | Problem identification and value assessment | Clarify the objective and its significance |
| Named project owner and approvers | Responsibility and accountability | Map ownership and approval roles |
| Comparison of internal delivery and external purchasing | Build-or-buy decision | Establish which route is being considered |
| Supplier criteria and active verification | Solution and supplier selection | Align the proposal and validation method |
| Completed solution and supplier selection | Readiness for commitment | Confirm the basis for negotiation |

### Turn each assumption into a question

For every stage claim, identify the customer behavior that would support it. If the team believes supplier selection has started, ask what criteria the customer is using, how alternatives are being compared, and what verification is required.

When answers are incomplete, retain the uncertainty. An undefined evaluation process is a gap to resolve, not a reason to invent one. The next interaction should help the customer clarify that process or establish who owns it.

Likewise, distinguish a known project entity from a known decision chain. Knowing which organization is expanding does not reveal who sponsors the purchase, approves expenditure, evaluates suppliers, or supports a particular proposal.

## Map relationships through decision information

Relationship development should rest on professional competence, useful decision information, and credible actions. The source's PRTABCD scan provides seven prompts for finding information gaps:

- **P — Past:** Relevant history and earlier project information.
- **R — Requirements:** The needs the customer is trying to address.
- **T — Timing:** Important milestones and decision dates.
- **A — Advocates:** People inside the customer organization who support your involvement.
- **B — Budget:** Funding information and unresolved budget questions.
- **C — Competition:** Competing suppliers and alternative approaches.
- **D — Decision chain:** Participants, responsibilities, and approval roles.

For this project, the source particularly emphasizes advocates and the decision chain. Ask who supports your involvement and who participates in the decision, while recording unknown budget and timing information as unresolved.

If no advocate is identifiable, create an entry point through professional insight relevant to the customer's decision. Social contact alone does not establish either support or access to the approval process.

## Build a solution the customer can verify

Once selection activity is supported by evidence, move beyond a presentation of product specifications. Define how the proposed solution addresses the customer's task, what success means, and how the customer will verify it.

Value development connects the solution to investment, returns, and profit improvement in terms the customer can understand. For this case, the missing second-phase amount and capacity target limit what can responsibly be quantified.

Practical solution work should therefore:

- Identify the operating activity the proposed solution would affect.
- Establish which customer information is available and which inputs remain missing.
- Agree on relevant success criteria and a verification method.
- Quantify value only when supporting information becomes available.

The first-phase capacity figure provides context. It does not establish second-phase revenue, savings, investment returns, or profitability. Leave those calculations open until there is an adequate basis for them.

## Prepare for negotiation without starting it prematurely

Negotiation preparation covers power, time, information, and alternatives, with the aim of reaching a sustainable agreement. Preparation can begin while the customer is still making earlier decisions. Active negotiation should follow confirmation that solution and supplier selection is complete.

An early price question should prompt a stage check. Has the customer completed selection, or is it still comparing possible approaches? Without that distinction, a price discussion can run ahead of the decisions needed to support an agreement.

Prepare a BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Make the alternative executable, and clarify limits, authority, interests, potential concessions, and records. The source treats this as internal preparation: do not proactively disclose the BATNA before the customer reaches negotiation.

The immediate priority remains the customer's unfinished decision task. A prepared negotiating position does not make an opportunity ready for negotiation.

## Actionable opportunity checklist

Use this checklist before advancing the opportunity:

- [ ] Separate reported facts, customer-confirmed information, and working hypotheses.
- [ ] Confirm second-phase capacity goals and timing, or record them as unknown.
- [ ] Identify the project owner, approvers, decision participants, and any advocate.
- [ ] Check whether the customer has considered internal delivery versus external purchasing.
- [ ] Obtain evidence of evaluation criteria, supplier comparisons, and verification activity.
- [ ] Assess the customer purchasing stage and seller sales stage separately.
- [ ] Match the next action to the customer's current decision task.
- [ ] Define a verifiable solution and substantiate any quantified value.
- [ ] Prepare alternatives, authority limits, and concession conditions internally.
- [ ] Confirm selection is complete before entering negotiation.

## FAQ

### Does a second-phase announcement prove supplier selection has begun?

No. It supports a hypothesis worth investigating. Defined evaluation criteria, verification activity, and supplier comparisons provide stronger evidence of the customer's purchasing stage.

### What should a seller do when investment figures are unavailable?

Identify the information needed to explain business value and leave unsupported calculations open. Do not invent investment amounts, capacity targets, returns, or savings. Quantify when sufficient supporting information becomes available.

### What if the customer asks for a price early?

Check whether solution and supplier selection is complete. A price question alone does not demonstrate readiness for negotiation. Establish the decision context before treating the discussion as a negotiation.

### How can a seller progress without an internal advocate?

Use professional insight to establish a useful entry point, while continuing to investigate the decision chain. Do not assume that a friendly contact supports the proposal or influences approval.

### What counts as progress?

Customer behavior showing that a purchasing task has been completed. Examples include assigning responsibility, comparing delivery options, or establishing evaluation criteria. Visits and seller enthusiasm are insufficient substitutes.
