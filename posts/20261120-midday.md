Capacity growth creates a practical challenge for key-account sales: turning available supply into customer commitments that the organization can deliver. Product specifications and production capability matter, but they do not establish whether a customer has agreed on the problem, assigned responsibility, or chosen a purchasing route.

The supplied source explores this challenge through Eoptolink's expansion in Chengdu and Thailand and its discussion of 1.6T optical modules. Its useful management lesson extends beyond that setting: track the seller's work and the buyer's decisions separately, then connect them through observable customer behavior.

This guide explains how to use that approach in opportunity reviews, customer conversations, and delivery planning. The aim is to make the next sales action fit the decision the customer actually needs to make.

## Start with two maps of the same opportunity

A seller's pipeline describes work the sales organization is doing. A customer's purchasing process describes decisions the buying organization must resolve. Confusing these maps makes an opportunity appear further advanced than the evidence supports.

The seller's five stages are:

1. Contact.
2. Needs assessment.
3. Solution development.
4. Negotiation.
5. Closing.

The customer's seven purchasing stages are:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing the value of addressing it.
3. Establishing that a feasible solution exists.
4. Assigning responsibility for the initiative.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Making a commitment and signing.

These stages have a many-to-many relationship. A solution discussion can help establish feasibility, clarify ownership, or inform a build-versus-buy decision. Its purpose depends on what the customer has already resolved.

A dual-perspective funnel places the seven buyer stages against the five seller stages. Each position describes a combination of selling activity and buying progress. The resulting map helps teams identify mismatches; it does not prescribe an automatic sequence of meetings.

## Use stage alignment to choose useful actions

The following table summarizes possible connections. Treat them as working associations rather than mandatory stage pairings.

| Seller stage | Related customer tasks | Useful sales contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | Recognize a problem | Establish an entry point and discuss the gap between current and desired conditions. |
| Needs assessment | Assess value; establish feasibility | Explore consequences and test whether available solutions could help. |
| Solution development | Assign responsibility; consider build versus buy | Define success criteria and support internal resource ownership. |
| Negotiation | Select a solution and supplier | Discuss scope, timing, risks, and alternatives. |
| Closing | Commit and sign | Complete formal commitments and prepare the implementation handoff. |

### Diagnose a mismatch before advancing

Consider the source's hypothetical example of a large data-center customer evaluating 1.6T optical modules. A salesperson may be presenting technical specifications and production advantages while the customer is still deciding whether the problem warrants investment. More presentations do not resolve that uncertainty unless they address the value question.

Another mismatch arises when the customer has not assigned responsibility. Budget ownership and accountability for results may still be disputed internally. Moving directly into price negotiation leaves that decision unresolved and can contribute to a stalled opportunity.

At this point, help an internal supporter clarify the interests of technical, financial, and business stakeholders. Provide material that helps those stakeholders discuss ownership and success criteria. Commercial terms become more useful when the customer has a clearer basis for making a commitment.

Before each interaction, identify the unresolved buyer decision and choose an action that helps resolve it.

## Ground progress in customer behavior

Visits, presentations, and proposal revisions describe seller effort. They provide limited evidence of a customer's decision progress. A percentage entered in a CRM also needs a factual basis.

The source emphasizes observable customer actions, including:

- Issuing a formal request for proposal, or RFP.
- Arranging a technical review involving multiple departments.
- Bringing legal colleagues into an initial contract review.
- Comparing detailed specifications across suppliers.
- Asking about delivery schedules and contractual consequences of delay.

These actions help reveal what the buying organization is working through. A cross-functional technical review can support an assessment of feasibility. Detailed supplier comparisons and questions about delivery obligations can indicate selection activity.

Interpret each action in context. Record what happened and what it establishes. A scheduled meeting shows that a discussion is planned; the decisions made during that meeting provide further evidence. Similarly, a polished proposal does not establish purchasing progress by itself.

For each significant event, record its date, participants, decision content, and the buyer task it helps resolve. Keep unresolved questions visible alongside completed decisions. This creates a working opportunity map that can be updated as the customer acts.

In a pipeline review, ask: What has the customer done since the previous review, and how does that action support the proposed stage? Where evidence is missing, retain the uncertainty instead of replacing it with confidence.

## Respond to uncertainty with disciplined flexibility

Complex purchases can pause, revisit earlier decisions, or change direction. A customer may reconsider internal development, while an organizational change may reopen responsibility for the project.

Process discipline means using consistent stage definitions and requiring evidence for progress. Flexibility means changing the next question or action when the customer's situation changes. Both are needed.

If the customer remains uncertain about value, return to the consequences of the current problem. The source suggests structured questioning, including SPIN techniques, and revisiting the investment rationale. Any return-on-investment discussion should use the customer's situation and explicit assumptions; the supplied material provides no numerical model to reuse.

If the customer is weighing internal development against external purchasing, examine the costs, time risks, and technical constraints of each route. Help the internal supporter organize the case for a decision without treating a preference for buying as an established commitment.

A pause also deserves examination. The source describes apparent interruptions in which internal evaluation may continue. Check for customer behavior supporting that interpretation. Where the status remains unclear, use infrequent, substantive follow-up to verify it. Silence alone does not establish either rejection or continuing progress.

## Bring technical and delivery colleagues into the process

A single salesperson may not cover every decision in a complex account. Sales, presales, delivery, and relevant senior leaders need a shared understanding of the customer's position.

Presales colleagues can support technical validation when the customer is establishing feasibility. Delivery specialists can assess implementation risks before the final commitment. Senior involvement should serve the decision that requires it.

For each participant, define the customer task they support and the unresolved issue they are helping address. This gives collaboration a practical purpose.

Production expansion does not remove the need to review delivery obligations. Before closing, bring the relevant specialists into discussions of scope, timing, implementation risk, and handoff. The source's central concern is avoiding commitments that outrun either the customer's decisions or the seller's delivery assessment.

## Actionable opportunity-review checklist

Use this checklist when preparing a customer meeting or reviewing an active opportunity:

- [ ] Name the seller's current stage.
- [ ] Identify the customer's current purchasing task.
- [ ] Record the latest customer action supporting that assessment.
- [ ] Capture the date, participants, and decision content.
- [ ] Mark unresolved questions about value, feasibility, responsibility, or purchasing route.
- [ ] Check whether the proposed sales action addresses one of those questions.
- [ ] Select the colleague best placed to support that decision.
- [ ] Agree on a next step whose completion can be observed.
- [ ] Review delivery assumptions before making commitments.
- [ ] Update the opportunity map when customer behavior changes.

If the evidence does not support movement, keep the current stage under review and specify what remains unresolved.

## FAQ

### Does every seller stage match one buyer stage?

No. The relationship is many-to-many. Use the customer's unresolved decision and observable actions to determine which sales activity is appropriate.

### Why are frequent meetings insufficient evidence?

Meetings show activity, but their frequency does not establish that the customer has resolved a purchasing decision. Record resulting decisions and follow-through.

### What should happen when ownership is unclear?

Help the internal supporter clarify stakeholder interests, budget responsibility, and accountability for results. Those issues can constrain progress even when technical interest is strong.

### When does negotiation fit the customer's process?

Negotiation becomes relevant as the customer evaluates solutions and suppliers and addresses scope, timing, and risk. Ground that assessment in customer behavior rather than the seller's wish to secure terms.

### How do other sales capabilities support process management?

Value analysis explains why change matters. Relationship work connects stakeholders. Questioning reveals needs and obstacles. Negotiation addresses remaining differences. Process management helps determine when each contribution is useful.
