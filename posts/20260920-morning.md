A headline about sodium batteries reaching "one cent per kilowatt-hour" can look like an industry curiosity. For key-account sellers, it is something more useful: a public example of how a leading player reframes a technical category in the customer's own business language. The lesson is not about battery chemistry. It is about translating capability into investment, return, and profit improvement that a customer's finance and procurement teams can act on.

## What the News Actually Says

East Money reported HiTHIUM hopes sodium batteries will push energy storage to one cent per kWh. CATL signed a three-year order with HyperStrong for 60GWh of sodium storage, a record. A dedicated line is operating, targeting GWh shipments in 2026.

The 60GWh, three-year, and GWh figures are checkable. The "one cent per kilowatt-hour" is framed as a hope, a target not a realized price. Separate facts from intentions: judge progress by customer behavior, competitors by verifiable facts.

## Seeing the Price Claim Through a Value Lens

PSS5 breaks key-account sales into five dimensions: value capability, relationship capability, questioning capability, system capability, and negotiation capability. Value capability is defined as converting the sales object from product features into customer-understandable investment, return, and profit improvement, and explaining that value with business numbers. "One cent per kilowatt-hour" is a model of that move. A customer executive may not remember energy density, cycle life, or low-temperature performance, but cost per kilowatt-hour is something finance and procurement can convert directly into project return.

The narrative also accomplishes three things at once.

### It sets the customer's evaluation standard

Once "one cent per kilowatt-hour" is accepted by the market, the first yardstick a customer uses to compare suppliers shifts from technical parameters to cost per kilowatt-hour. Later competitors must answer on that yardstick first.

### It puts value into the customer's budget early

At the value-judgment stage, customers can work backward from that target to an investment amount. Whoever can prove with monetized numbers that they are closer to the target is more likely to enter the solution stage.

### It leaves room for negotiation

Once a price anchor is publicly stated, concession space, condition exchanges, and alternatives at the negotiating table must be redesigned around that anchor.

## How Key-Account Sellers Can Respond

If your customer is in energy storage, power, or an adjacent supply chain, act same-day. Use PRTABCD to scan past history, requirements, timing, embraceers, budget, competitors, and decision chain. For each, note knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions.

Calibrate pace with buying-selling alignment. Customer procurement has seven tasks: problem discovery, value judgment, solution confirmation, responsibility, build versus buy, supplier selection, and signing. Seller stages: contact, requirements, solution, negotiation, closing. The news pushed the market to value judgment. Judge your customer's position by behavior evidence, not visits.

## A Negotiation Reminder After the Price Claim

When an industry states "one cent per kilowatt-hour" as a target, customers will press on price. Before negotiating, know your bottom line, tradeable conditions, and BATNA—the best alternative if no deal. Every concession should buy back a condition.

## Comparison: Parameter Language vs. Business Language

| Dimension | Parameter language | Business language |
|---|---|---|
| Primary unit | Energy density, cycle life, low-temperature performance | Cost per kilowatt-hour, payback period, project return |
| Who engages first | Technical evaluators | Finance, procurement, and executive sponsors |
| Effect on budget | Hard to convert into an investment figure | Can be worked backward into an investment amount |
| Effect on competition | Competitors debate specifications | Competitors must answer on the same yardstick |
| Effect on negotiation | Price discussed in isolation | Concessions tied to conditions and alternatives |

## Actionable Checklist

- Separate verified facts from stated intentions before using the news with a customer.
- Identify the customer's equivalent of "one cent per kilowatt-hour": a value anchor finance can read immediately.
- Run a PRTABCD scan and record known, unknown, source, and next question for each item.
- Identify embraceers and map the full decision chain rather than a single signer.
- Determine where the customer sits in the seven procurement stages using behavior evidence.
- Align your five sales stages to that position instead of skipping ahead.
- Quantify improvement in operational areas such as yield, energy consumption, downtime, or capital occupation.
- Convert improvements into revenue, cost, profit, payback period, or opportunity cost.
- Write the result as an investment case the customer can use internally to request budget.
- Before negotiating, define your bottom line, tradeable conditions, and BATNA.
- Exchange every price concession for payment terms, order scale, or a validation opportunity.

## FAQ

**Q1: What are the five capabilities in PSS5?**

A1: PSS5 breaks key-account sales into five dimensions: value capability, relationship capability, questioning capability, system capability, and negotiation capability. Value capability translates products into customer profit and investment return; relationship capability uses professional ability and decision information to penetrate the customer decision chain; questioning capability uses intentional questions to help customers see the problem and the value of change; system capability manages progress through the dual lens of five sales stages and seven customer procurement stages; negotiation capability reaches sustainable agreements around power, time, information, and alternatives.

**Q2: What is the most direct lesson for key-account sellers from the one-cent claim?**

A2: The lesson is to express value in the customer's business unit. Cost per kilowatt-hour is the real conversion unit for storage customers making investment decisions, and it enters budget discussions more easily than parameters such as energy or cycle count. Your proposal should also find a similar anchor—one that a customer's finance team understands immediately and can put into the profit statement—and then calculate and communicate it with business numbers.

**Q3: How do you monetize value in practice?**

A3: Three steps. First, identify which operational areas your solution changes, such as yield, energy consumption, downtime, or capital occupation. Second, quantify the improvement in each area, using verifiable pilots or customer behavior evidence. Third, convert improvements into financial language such as revenue, cost, profit, payback period, or opportunity cost, and write it into an investment case the customer can use internally to secure budget.

**Q4: What is PRTABCD and how is it used?**

A4: PRTABCD is a relationship-capability scanning tool. Seven categories find blind spots: past history, requirements, timing, embraceers, budget, competitors, and decision chain. Note knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions. It is not seven role profiles; update through questioning.

**Q5: How should negotiation respond when an industry publicly states a low-price target?**

A5: Do not rush to cut price. Before negotiating, clarify the power balance, time pressure, information held by each side, and each side's alternatives. BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement—the best fallback action if the parties cannot agree. With that clear, design concessions as exchanges: every price step should buy back payment terms, order scale, or a validation opportunity, so the agreement remains sustainable for both sides.

**Q6: What are the five sales stages?**

A6: In the current PSS5 system capability, the sales funnel is fixed at five stages: contact, requirements, solution, negotiation, and closing. Progress is not judged by visit counts or a percentage in a CRM, but by exit evidence on the customer side: whether the customer committed to continued communication, confirmed the problem and responsibility, jointly defined success criteria, entered condition exchange, or completed formal signing. Without customer behavior evidence, treat the opportunity as still in place.

**Q7: How do the seven customer procurement stages help judge opportunity position?**

A7: The seven stages are the decision tasks a customer completes in sequence: discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming responsibility ownership, deciding build versus buy, selecting solution and supplier, and committing and signing. Each step must be proven by customer behavior, not replaced by your sales activity. Check item by item which step the customer has proven through behavior, what the next task is, and make your actions serve only the current task.

**Q8: What does buying-selling alignment mean?**

A8: Buying-selling alignment means marking the customer's position in the seven procurement stages and yours in the five sales stages on one funnel, then using behavior evidence to judge synchronization. If the customer is judging value while you discuss terms, you are ahead; if they select suppliers while you research, you are behind. Align first.
