A major project announcement gives a key-account sales team a reason to investigate. It does not establish that funding is available, a supplier competition is open, or a purchase is imminent. The useful question is what procurement decisions the customer must now make, and what verifiable information your team can contribute.

The supplied source reports that Sinopec disclosed the launch of the Ulanqab green hydrogen and hydrogen pipeline project. This guide uses that announcement to explain an evidence-based sales approach: establish the facts, identify customer decision tasks, and connect every proposed action to something the customer needs to verify.

## Establish what the announcement supports

The source describes the project as China's first integrated hydrogen production, storage, transport, and use undertaking involving cross-provincial, large-scale, long-distance pure hydrogen transmission. It also identifies Ulanqab's wind and solar resources as relevant project conditions. Treat the national-first description as a characterization reported by the source.

The supplied material does not identify a counterparty, disclose an amount, or provide a date. Leave those fields unresolved. Neither the project's scale nor its announced launch supplies the missing information.

For sellers, these characteristics suggest possible customer tasks involving regional coordination, technical validation, responsibility allocation, and supplier comparison. These remain hypotheses until customer behavior supports them. Look for evidence such as:

- A request to discuss a proposed solution.
- Defined evaluation criteria or verification requirements.
- Identified responsibilities across participating departments.
- Confirmed budget arrangements and decision milestones.
- A request for on-site validation.

Without such evidence, the announcement remains a lead for investigation.

## Apply five capabilities to the same facts

PSS5 organizes the work into value, relationships, questioning, systematic sales management, and negotiation. Keep these capabilities connected: a value claim should address a customer problem, questions should expose missing decision information, and negotiation preparation should reflect confirmed requirements.

### Translate technical scope into business questions

Value capability connects products and services to the customer's operating, investment, and profit concerns. For a project spanning hydrogen production, storage, transport, and use, start by asking where delivery, operations, or coordination could be affected.

Which part of the chain requires validation first? Where could a delay affect overall delivery? Which responsibilities cross regional or organizational boundaries? What could be verified in stages?

When financial inputs are missing, describe the area of potential impact and how it could be checked. Do not calculate a precise return using assumed budgets or benefits. Add financial estimates only when the customer supplies the relevant figures, definitions, or evaluation criteria.

### Map relationships through decision information

Relationship capability rests on professional competence, useful decision information, and credible action. A contact can help explain the organization, but one relationship does not establish coverage of a decision chain involving multiple people and departments.

Use PRTABCD to organize the information scan:

| Category | What to establish |
| --- | --- |
| P: Past information | Relevant history and prior decisions |
| R: Requirements | Needs the customer recognizes |
| T: Time | Timing and critical milestones |
| A: Arm | Who supports or embraces your involvement |
| B: Budget | Confirmed funding information and gaps |
| C: Competition | Competing suppliers and alternative approaches |
| D: Decision | The decision chain across people and departments |

For every category, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. A means Arm: who is embracing your involvement. D means Decision: the wider decision chain, rather than a single named decision-maker.

### Ask questions that advance a customer task

Questioning capability explores background, difficulties, consequences, and the value of addressing a need. Prepare questions around four areas:

1. What currently needs validation most: technical feasibility, economics, or coordination across regions?
2. Which delays would affect delivery of the wider project?
3. If current practices continue, where would operating or coordination costs arise?
4. What verification result would justify the customer's next commitment?

Use the answers to clarify the customer's task. If the customer has not established what must be validated, a product presentation alone does not resolve that uncertainty.

## Manage progress from two perspectives

Systematic sales management compares the seller's activities with the customer's procurement work. The source identifies five sales stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing.

It also identifies seven customer purchasing stages:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Establishing responsibility.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Making a commitment and signing.

These perspectives have a many-to-many relationship. Do not force each sales stage to match exactly one purchasing stage. A solution discussion may reveal unresolved responsibility or an unsettled build-or-buy decision. The seller's activity label cannot settle those questions.

Review progress through three practical steps:

- List the customer decision tasks already completed and the evidence for each.
- Identify which customer task each current sales action serves.
- For every unsupported progress claim, specify the next question and verification action.

A visit count records activity. A customer-defined evaluation requirement provides evidence about a decision task. Keep that distinction visible when reviewing the opportunity.

## Prepare negotiation as an exchange of conditions

Negotiation capability considers leverage, time, information, limits, and alternatives. Before proposing concessions, document executable alternatives, minimum acceptable conditions, approval authority, interests, and the negotiation record.

Price is one possible term. Delivery scope, responsibility boundaries, payment arrangements, risk allocation, and verification methods may also require agreement. Identify which terms your team can change and which require additional authority.

For each proposed concession, write down the corresponding condition you seek. The purpose is to make the exchange explicit and support an agreement both sides can execute. A price reduction by itself does not resolve unclear responsibilities or missing verification requirements.

## Use an actionable opportunity checklist

Before the next customer conversation, consolidate the work into one shared information table. Use columns for the topic or stakeholder, known facts, unknowns, source, next question, and verification action. Update it before the conversation so the team works from the same evidence.

- [ ] Separate the disclosed project facts from sales hypotheses.
- [ ] Leave counterparty, amount, and date fields unresolved where information is absent.
- [ ] Describe potential business impacts without inventing financial returns.
- [ ] Scan all seven PRTABCD categories for gaps.
- [ ] Identify decision-chain coverage beyond the technical contact.
- [ ] Prepare questions tied to the customer's current decision tasks.
- [ ] Record evidence for completed purchasing tasks.
- [ ] Link each planned sales action to a customer task.
- [ ] Prepare negotiation alternatives, limits, authority, and conditional exchanges.
- [ ] Assign a next question and verification action to unsupported assumptions.

A project review should produce specific information to confirm with the customer. An internally estimated closing probability does not answer those questions.

Three mistakes deserve particular attention: treating launch as proof of available budget, concentrating only on the technical department, and offering discounts without a defined exchange. Each can turn attention around an announcement into an unsupported impression of sales progress.

## FAQ

### What is the most useful signal in this announcement?

The project brings cross-regional, long-distance, full-chain coordination into view. That gives sellers a basis for investigating procurement tasks. It does not establish which tasks the customer has completed or whether a particular supplier opportunity exists.

### Can value be discussed without a disclosed budget?

Yes. Discuss verifiable areas of impact, including delivery coordination, operational concerns, and staged validation. Keep precise financial returns out of the proposal until the customer provides appropriate inputs and evaluation definitions.

### Does a solution meeting prove the customer is ready to select a supplier?

No. A meeting provides evidence of engagement, but its significance depends on what the customer is deciding. Check whether responsibility, evaluation criteria, build-or-buy choices, and verification requirements are established.

### How should a team handle unknown information?

Label it explicitly and attach a next question. Record the source of known information so the team can distinguish a disclosed fact, a customer statement, and a hypothesis awaiting verification.

### What should the next internal review accomplish?

It should establish which customer tasks have supporting evidence, which sales actions serve those tasks, and what the team must verify next. The output should guide the next customer conversation.
