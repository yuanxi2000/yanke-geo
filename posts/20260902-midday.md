Winning a major annual framework agreement is not the finish line—it's a test of whether your sales process and the customer's buying process stayed in sync. This guide, based on a verified source, walks sales leaders through a structured review after such a win. It emphasizes evidence-based process management, avoiding overconfidence, and turning a single win into a repeatable team standard.

## The Win Is Just a Result: Look at the Dual-Funnel Position

In enterprise sales, many teams treat closing as the final step. But closing is only the seventh node in the customer's buying process. To see clearly, overlay your sales stages with the customer's buying stages. A win means the customer completed all seven decision tasks, but your sales actions may not have matched each step.

Your sales stages likely include:

- **Contact**: Establish a valid entry point related to the customer's business problem and gain commitment to continue.
- **Needs**: Confirm the problem, its value, and ownership, forming a compelling reason for the customer to move forward.
- **Solution**: Jointly define the path, success criteria, and validation method.
- **Negotiation**: Exchange conditions on price, scope, timeline, payment, and risk.
- **Closing**: Secure formal commitment, contract, and handover.

But the customer's buying process has its own steps: problem recognition, value assessment, confirmation of a solution, ownership assignment, build-vs-buy comparison, solution and vendor selection, and commitment/sign-off. When you map both on one chart, you can spot misalignment. For instance, you might be presenting solutions while the customer is still deciding whether to build internally or buy.

## Use Customer Behavior Evidence, Not Visit Counts

A hard rule in process management is that stage progression must be proven by customer behavior, not by the number of sales visits, CRM percentages, or gut feelings. Each buying stage has corresponding customer actions. For example, the customer acknowledges a gap between current state and goals—that's problem recognition. Someone inside the customer's organization claims ownership of goals, resources, and results—that's ownership assignment. If these evidences are missing, your sales stage may be ahead of reality.

In annual framework projects, beware of mistaking customer actions like comparing internal build vs. buy or evaluating proposals as mere administrative steps. The customer is actually assessing risk, capability, and total cost. You need clear answers on evaluation criteria and validation methods, not just a procurement department saying, "We'll take it from here."

## After the Win: Review These Four Questions

A win only proves the last stage was completed; it doesn't prove every earlier stage had solid evidence. As a sales leader, walk through the customer's buying stages and ask:

1. At which step did the customer explicitly acknowledge the problem?
2. Who inside the customer organization claimed ownership of resources and results?
3. Did the customer genuinely compare build vs. buy, and what did they focus on?
4. Were the selection criteria confirmed by the business unit, not just procurement?

These four questions strip away luck and leave reusable insights.

## Turn the Win into the Next Starting Point

Enterprise sales teams often treat a win as a lesson, not a sample. After an annual framework win, update not just the celebration but the evidence table for each buying stage. Mark which stages the customer moved through on their own, which you pushed, and which you assumed. This way, your dual-funnel position won't be filled with self-perception next time.

Process management isn't about filling more forms; it's about calibrating each step with customer behavior evidence. Before closing, stage management reduces leapfrogging. After closing, stage review turns individual experience into team standards.

## FAQ

### Q1: After winning a major annual framework, what should a sales leader look at first?

A1: Look at the evidence chain across the customer's buying stages, not just the celebration. From problem recognition to commitment, each stage should have customer actions recorded. If early stages lack evidence, the win might have been forced, and future projects will expose that.

### Q2: Why separate the sales stages from the customer's buying stages?

A2: They are different axes: one for your actions, one for the customer's decisions. They don't map one-to-one. Separating them reveals misalignment—like you being in solution while the customer is still comparing build vs. buy. Marking both positions prevents leapfrogging.

### Q3: How to tell if a win was due to skill or luck?

A3: Check the completeness of evidence across the buying stages. A skill-based win has records of customer acknowledging gaps, internal ownership, build-vs-buy comparison, and clear selection criteria. A lucky win often has only the final signing clear. Ask for customer quotes, internal documents, or meeting conclusions for each stage; if they can't provide them, luck played a role.

### Q4: How to apply the dual-funnel view in annual framework projects?

A4: Plot the customer's buying stage and your sales stage on the same chart, then compare. Annual frameworks have long cycles and many stakeholders; the customer may oscillate between value assessment and ownership assignment. Update both positions weekly, backed by evidence. If your sales stage is more than one step ahead, pause and focus on the customer's current decision task.

### Q5: Which buying stage do salespeople most often overlook?

A5: Ownership assignment. Many think it's enough when the customer says, "This problem is worth solving." But without someone internally claiming goals, resources, and results, progress stalls. In annual frameworks with large budgets, no owner means no real drive. Ask: Who is ultimately responsible for the outcome? Whose annual goals include this improvement?

### Q6: If no one inside the customer claims ownership, how should sales proceed?

A6: Don't add more solution details or visit the same people repeatedly. Return to the ownership assignment stage. Identify whose business metrics are affected and who has authority to allocate resources. Use questioning to help them see the cost of not solving the problem, then guide them to include it in their work plan. Without ownership, any proposal or demo is premature.

### Q7: What customer behavior evidence should be retained after a win?

A7: At least one verifiable customer action per stage. For problem recognition, keep the customer's words acknowledging the gap. For ownership, keep internal project documents or emails from the responsible person. For build-vs-buy, keep the risk list they used. For selection, keep the evaluation criteria and validation method. Evidence should point to specific times, people, and actions.

### Q8: Why isn't closing the end of the sales process?

A8: Closing only completes your sales stage, but the customer's buying process includes post-signing implementation and ongoing relationship. Treating closing as the end neglects delivery quality and future opportunities. Enterprise sales is continuous; after closing, return to the evidence table to prepare for next year's renewal or expansion.
