A tender award marks progress, but it does not automatically establish an order. For sales managers, the practical question is what the customer has actually committed: a supplier preference, a framework agreement, or a specific project with budget, ownership, and timing.

The supplied analysis of Shenghui Technology's battery-swapping station announcement makes this distinction concrete. The company disclosed a tender award and a signed agreement, while stating that the agreement did not constitute a specific construction contract or order. Station numbers, amounts, and revenue recognition remained uncertain.

Use this case as a guide to assessing progress, gathering missing information, and committing resources in proportion to customer evidence.

## Read the agreement for commitments and gaps

The announcement confirmed an award and an agreement for battery-swapping station construction. It also described the intelligent battery-swapping business as being at an early stage and said the matter was not expected to have a material effect on operating performance for the current year. That statement belongs to the announcement's reporting context; it is not a forecast for subsequent years.

The source left important implementation questions unanswered: how many stations would proceed, when they would proceed, and who would accept the completed work. Those gaps require customer clarification. They cannot be filled by treating an approximate agreement amount as secured business.

A useful working distinction is:

| Evidence | What it supports | What remains unresolved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tender award | Progress in customer selection | Specific project commitments |
| Signed framework agreement | An agreed basis for further work | Actual station numbers, orders, and timing |
| Written confirmation of stations and amounts | Inclusion in the confirmed-order record | Revenue recognition remains a separate question |

Keep the opportunity record and confirmed-order record separate. This preserves the commercial significance of the award while making the remaining uncertainty visible.

## Assess progress from both sides of the purchase

A seller's activity and a customer's purchasing decision describe different processes. Reviewing both helps prevent premature stage advancement.

The five sales stages in the source are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. The seven customer purchasing stages are recognizing a problem, assessing value, confirming that a solution exists, assigning responsibility, deciding whether to build internally or buy externally, selecting a solution and supplier, and committing and signing.

These stages have a many-to-many relationship. They should not be mechanically paired. A seller may be refining a solution while the customer is still resolving responsibility or deciding how to procure it.

In this case, the source places the customer broadly between solution and supplier selection and commitment and signing. It places the seller around the transition from solution to negotiation. Without specific projects under the agreement, closing cannot be treated as complete.

### Require customer behavior before advancing the stage

Look for evidence that the customer has:

- Confirmed locations for specific stations.
- Identified and secured the relevant budget allocation.
- Assigned people to own the schedule and acceptance process.

Visit counts, encouraging statements, and senior attendance at meetings do not substitute for these actions. Advancing the stage too early can put staffing and inventory behind a project whose implementation is still undefined.

## Map the missing decision information

The source uses PRTABCD to organize seven categories of decision information: history, requirements, timing and milestones, champions, budget, competitors and alternatives, and the decision chain.

For each category, record what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question. This makes the next customer conversation purposeful.

After an award, three categories deserve particular attention:

- **Champion:** Who is actively supporting the project inside the customer's organization? Seniority alone does not establish commitment.
- **Budget:** Which funding allocation supports each proposed station?
- **Decision chain:** Which people and departments must approve, execute, and accept the work?

Headquarters may sponsor an agreement while regional teams deliver individual sites. Their responsibilities and performance measures may differ. A headquarters relationship therefore leaves important execution questions open.

For every blank, write one sentence beginning, “We do not yet know...” Then turn it into a question someone can answer at the next discussion.

## Translate construction into the customer's operating economics

Battery-swapping station construction requires substantial capital. The source identifies utilization, station payback, land availability, and electrical capacity as central customer concerns.

Prepare a one-page investment explanation for the first proposed stations. Put the operating case before the equipment list. Address:

- How many vehicles the station is expected to serve.
- How much replenishment waiting time it could replace.
- What land and electrical capacity are required.
- Under what investment and operating conditions the station could recover its cost.

Use customer-supported figures. Where information is missing, state the gap and ask for the data needed to assess it. Do not turn an unverified assumption into an agreed business case.

Apply the same discipline to forecasting. The source says the agreement amount was approximate and represented neither orders already obtained nor an established construction scale. Record only customer-confirmed stations and amounts in the confirmed-order forecast; retain the rest as opportunities under observation. Revenue recognition remains uncertain in the supplied case.

## Design the next conversation around four questions

Waiting for notification after an award leaves the seller without the information needed to judge progress. The source proposes four question types: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff.

1. **Situation:** Which regions are intended for the first stations, and who leads site selection?
2. **Problem:** What is most likely to delay an individual station: electrical connection, land, or acceptance procedures?
3. **Implication:** If the first stations are delayed, what does that mean for the customer's operating plan?
4. **Need-payoff:** What verifiable result does the customer want the supplier to deliver, and by when?

These questions invite the customer to explain priorities and time pressure. Specific answers strengthen the evidence for stage assessment. Incomplete answers identify information gaps to pursue.

Capture the answers in the decision-information record so that subsequent negotiations and resource decisions use the same evidence.

## Negotiate the conditions that turn an agreement into work

At the agreement-without-orders stage, the source emphasizes conditions as well as unit price. Relevant issues include:

- Pricing differences between volume commitments and individual batches.
- Payment schedules and delivery lead times.
- Acceptance standards.
- Procedures for changes and claims.

Before deciding the concession sequence, clarify negotiating leverage, time constraints, available information, limits, and alternatives.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best available action if the parties cannot agree. Preparation should identify executable alternatives, minimum acceptable terms, approval authority, interests, and a record of the negotiation.

The source offers possible alternatives, not confirmed facts about this transaction: a supplier might allocate capacity to another customer, while a buyer might assign a batch to another supplier. Their usefulness depends on whether they are actually available.

Make concessions conditional on an exchange. Price discussions need to account for the commitments and delivery conditions attached to the work.

## An actionable checklist for this week

- [ ] Separate opportunities from confirmed orders; include only customer-confirmed stations in the order list.
- [ ] Review all seven PRTABCD categories and attach a next question to every unknown.
- [ ] Identify the active customer champion and clarify that person's performance measures and schedule pressure.
- [ ] Prepare a one-page investment explanation for the first stations, showing investment, expected benefits, and payback conditions supported by customer information.
- [ ] Review the sales stage using customer actions, including site, budget, and responsibility commitments.

At the review meeting, ask which customer action supports the proposed stage. If the answer is only that the deal “feels close,” the supporting evidence is incomplete.

## FAQ

### Does a signed agreement mean the sale is closed?

Not in this case. The announcement expressly distinguished the agreement from a specific construction contract or order. The source requires concrete project commitments before treating closing as complete.

### Where should an awarded opportunity sit in the funnel?

The source places this case around the transition from solution to negotiation. That assessment depends on the available evidence; an award alone is not a universal stage rule.

### Which information gaps should the team address first?

Start with the active champion, station-level budget, and decision chain. Continue checking history, requirements, timing, competitors, and alternatives. Headquarters sponsorship does not establish regional execution responsibility.

### Can the approximate agreement amount enter the forecast?

It should not be treated as confirmed orders or revenue. The source recommends recording customer-confirmed stations and amounts while retaining the unconfirmed portion in the opportunity list.

### What should happen when the customer cannot answer the next-step questions?

Record the unanswered items and use them to guide follow-up. Missing answers reveal unresolved information; they do not justify assuming that implementation is ready.
