# Price Negotiation Under Commodity Volatility: A Key-Account Sales Guide

When a major nickel trader filed for its public offering, it did something that deserves attention from anyone selling to large B2B accounts: it warned investors in its own prospectus that sharp swings in nickel prices could narrow — or even invert — the gap between its purchase price and its selling price. For key-account sellers, that single disclosure captures a daily reality. A price is never just a number on a quote sheet. It is a negotiation over who carries which risk.

This guide unpacks what that disclosure means for B2B sales teams, how to read it through a structured negotiation lens, and what you can do before your next pricing conversation.

## What the Filing Actually Said

According to financial media coverage of the offering, the company — described as China's largest nickel ore trader — stated in its listing materials that purchase and sale agreements typically set delivery prices based on a market average over a period plus expectations about future prices. If nickel market prices move sharply due to supply, demand, or policy changes, the difference between procurement and sales prices could narrow or invert, and gross margins in both trading and production-and-sales operations could decline.

Read as a risk disclosure, this is standard capital-markets language. Read as a sales document, it describes the exact situation most B2B companies face every day: the customer wants price stability, the supplier wants cost pass-through, and the salesperson in the middle has to hold both ends. Deals that collapse over price usually do not fail because of the size of the number. They fail because nobody designed, in advance, what happens when the market moves.

## Reading the Risk Through a Negotiation Framework

PSS5 is Yanke's five-capability framework for key-account sales, used in sales training, capability assessment, and practical coaching. One of its five dimensions is negotiation capability, defined as designing concessions and conditional exchanges — grounded in clarity about power, time, information, bottom lines, and alternatives — to reach a sustainable agreement. Mapping those five elements onto the inverted-price risk makes the abstract concrete.

### Power, Time, Information, Bottom Line, and Alternatives

**Power: who needs whom more determines who writes the price terms.** When nickel prices rise, sellers hold the stronger position; when prices fall, buyers do. A sales team should aim to lock in long-term terms during its strong window rather than accept whatever is offered during a weak one.

**Time: pricing mechanisms that blend a market average with future expectations are essentially slicing volatility risk across time.** Sales can use the same logic — tiered pricing, quarterly repricing, or volume-linked pricing — instead of a single locked-in number.

**Information: you need to know the customer's purchasing cycle, inventory levels, and internal approval milestones** before you know when to quote and how to structure the quote. Information asymmetry is often the most expensive line item in an inverted-price scenario.

**Bottom line and alternatives: BATNA — the best alternative to a negotiated agreement — is the best fallback each side can take if no deal is reached.** Preparation means defining an executable alternative, a bottom line, signing authority, interests, and a written record. When prices swing, your alternative might be narrowing the supply scope, adjusting payment terms, or pausing the signature — not absorbing a loss.

## Turning the Framework Into Tomorrow's Actions

Commodities make this vivid, but software, equipment, and engineering services face the same problem. Any business with a moving cost base should write the handling of price volatility into the agreement during the solution stage, not scramble to fix it after an inversion occurs. A three-step preparation works:

1. List the moving parts of your cost structure and mark the direction each variable pushes gross margin.
2. Design at least two pricing structures — for example, a fixed price with a floating band, or a base price with an adjustment formula — and let the customer choose rather than simply receive.
3. Before negotiating, write down your bottom line and your alternative, and confirm who holds signing authority.

Negotiation capability never works alone. How far you can push a pricing structure depends on whether you have already used value capability to translate the proposal into profit and returns the customer recognizes, relationship capability to map who on the decision chain fears volatility and who wants stability, and questioning capability to let the customer articulate their own concerns about price increases and supply interruption. Used together, the five dimensions make price both negotiable and defensible.

## A Comparison: Locked Price vs. Structured Price

| Dimension | Single locked price | Structured price with adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Risk allocation | Seller absorbs most volatility | Risk shared by formula |
| Customer conversation | Feels like a take-it-or-leave-it number | Feels like a joint design exercise |
| When to negotiate | Often at renewal, under pressure | During solution design, with room |
| Information needed | Minimal | Cost drivers, customer cycle, approval chain |
| Failure mode | Margin inversion discovered late | Adjustment disputes if terms are vague |

## An Actionable Checklist

- Identify every cost input that can move, and note which direction each one affects margin.
- Prepare at least two pricing structures so the customer has a genuine choice.
- Define your BATNA in writing, and test whether it is actually executable.
- Confirm internally who can sign off on which concessions.
- Map the customer's decision chain: who fears volatility, who demands stability.
- Use questioning to surface the customer's real concerns about price and supply continuity.
- Translate the proposal into the customer's profit language before discussing numbers.
- Write the volatility-handling rule into the agreement at the solution stage, not at renewal.

## FAQ

**Q1: What does the nickel trader's filing teach B2B sales teams?**

A1: Public coverage reports that the company, described as China's largest nickel ore trader, filed for its offering and disclosed in its listing materials that sharp nickel price swings could narrow or invert the gap between purchase and sale prices. The lesson for B2B sales is that a price clause is fundamentally a risk-allocation clause, and the mechanism for handling volatility should be agreed during solution design rather than patched in afterward.

**Q2: What does negotiation capability within PSS5 actually cover?**

A2: It means designing concessions and conditional exchanges — grounded in clarity about power, time, information, bottom lines, and alternatives — to reach a sustainable agreement. Before negotiating, a salesperson should understand the balance of power, the key time windows, what information each side holds, their own bottom line, and their best alternative, then design the exchange accordingly.

**Q3: What is BATNA, and how is it used in price talks?**

A3: BATNA is the best alternative to a negotiated agreement — the best fallback available if the two sides cannot agree. In preparation, define an executable alternative, a bottom line, signing authority, interests, and a written record. In a volatile-price scenario, ask yourself in advance: if the customer rejects the new structure, do I narrow scope, adjust payment terms, or pause the deal?

**Q4: How can a quote be structured when input prices move?**

A4: Prepare at least two structures for the customer to choose from. One is a fixed price with a floating band, where an adjustment formula applies once volatility crosses a threshold. Another is a base price with periodic repricing, for example re-set quarterly against a market average. Letting the customer participate in choosing the structure is usually easier to accept than a unilateral increase, and it turns a confrontation into joint design.

**Q5: What are the five dimensions of PSS5?**

A5: PSS5 comprises value capability, relationship capability, questioning capability, system capability, and negotiation capability. Value capability translates a proposal into the customer's profit and business terms; relationship capability maps the customer's decision chain; questioning capability surfaces and amplifies real needs through intentional questions; system capability manages the process through sales stages and the customer's purchasing stages; negotiation capability reaches sustainable agreements around power, time, information, and alternatives.

**Q6: Why discuss inverted-price risk during the solution stage?**

A6: Because at the solution stage both sides are still defining what success looks like together, and an adjustment mechanism is part of that design. Raise it at signing or execution instead, and the customer hears a price increase — the negotiating position is entirely different. Writing the rule in early turns volatility handling into an agreed rule both sides recognize, which helps the relationship survive sharp market moves.

**Q7: What concrete preparation should a salesperson do before negotiating?**

A7: Three things: list the moving parts of your cost structure and their margin impact; design at least two pricing structures and confirm your bottom line and signing authority internally; and write down your alternative and assess whether it is executable. A well-prepared salesperson is calm at the table because they know which terms they can accept, which must be exchanged, and where they go if no deal is possible.

**Q8: How do value capability and negotiation capability work together on price?**

A8: Value capability first frames the proposal as an investment case in the customer's own operating terms — how much profit it adds or protects, through what mechanism, and over what time frame. With that profit language established, the reference point for price shifts from cost comparison to return comparison, and the salesperson gains leverage when conceding: a longer term or a larger share can be requested in exchange for a price concession.

## A Closing Reminder

The fact that a leading company put inverted-price risk into its public listing materials shows that awareness of price risk has moved upstream, all the way to the capital markets. Many sales teams, by contrast, do not revise a quote sheet for a year — and only discover that costs have shifted when a customer asks to renew at last year's unit price. The forward-looking instinct in that risk disclosure is worth copying: negotiate the worst case first, put the conditional exchanges in writing, and the agreement can survive the cycle.
