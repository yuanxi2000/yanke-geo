## Introduction

Winning a large contract is not a single event but a process where the seller's five stages align with the buyer's seven decision tasks. Many sales teams focus only on their own actions, missing the buyer's journey. This guide provides a systematic approach to align both perspectives, ensuring that every step is backed by observable customer behavior. By using a dual-view funnel, you can know exactly what to do next and avoid costly missteps.

## The Dual-View Funnel: Sales Stages and Customer Decision Tasks

### Sales Five Stages

The sales process is fixed into five stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. These stages represent the seller's progression from initial outreach to final commitment.

### Customer Seven Decision Tasks

On the buyer's side, there are seven decision tasks: problem identification, value assessment, solution confirmation, responsibility assignment, build-or-buy decision, solution and vendor selection, and commitment and signing. These tasks are not linear but can overlap and repeat.

### The Multi-to-Multi Relationship

The two axes are not one-to-one but multi-to-multi. For example, a customer might still be in the value assessment stage while the salesperson has already submitted a proposal. This mismatch makes the sales action ineffective. Understanding this relationship helps you avoid pushing too early or too late.

## Using Customer Behavior Evidence to Gauge Progress

Systematic selling requires that progress be measured by customer behavior, not by visit counts, CRM percentages, or salesperson intuition. For instance, to know if the customer acknowledges a gap between current state and goals, look for evidence that they raise this issue in internal meetings, not just that you think it's a problem.

### Key Behavioral Indicators

- The customer proactively schedules meetings with the user department, not just procurement.
- The customer requests calculation logic and is willing to discuss metric definitions.
- A clear owner emerges internally, with goals written into meeting minutes.
- Budget discussions are placed on the next meeting agenda.
- The customer starts comparing internal build vs. external purchase rather than directly asking for a quote.

## Aligning Sales and Buying: Avoid Overstepping

Buy-sell alignment means your sales actions serve the customer's current decision task. When the customer is in value assessment, help them translate the problem into profit language. When they are in responsibility assignment, assist them in finding an internal champion. Overstepping often leads to proposals being shelved.

### Common Mistake

A frequent error is rushing to solution and pricing when the customer shows interest. However, the customer might still be in solution confirmation or unresolved on the build-or-buy decision. If you quote too early, the customer will only use price to delay.

## Handling False Interruptions with the Three-Pronged Approach

Large projects often face interruptions like "leadership needs more time," "budget is next year," or "we'll discuss internally." The three-pronged approach is not about pressuring the customer but identifying whether the interruption is real or false and securing the next genuine progress. For example, if the customer says they need to study further, ask what specific issues they will study, who needs to be involved, and when they will provide an answer.

Each genuine progress you secure moves the sales process forward and provides evidence for the customer's decision tasks.

## From Winning to Signing: Treat Negotiation as a Stage, Not an Event

Negotiation is not a one-time event before closing but the fourth stage of the sales process. During negotiation, both parties exchange conditions on scope, timeline, payment, and risk. Only when the previous three stages are done well does negotiation avoid becoming pure price haggling.

Signing is the formal commitment in the closing stage, but before that, the customer must complete budget, terms, responsibility, and risk confirmation. If these are not done, the signing can easily flip at the last step.

### Prepare a Negotiation Checklist

For large contracts, write down a negotiation checklist: which conditions can be conceded, which must be held, and what alternatives exist. This preparation should be on paper, not improvised.

## Practical Implementation: Start Building a Dual-View File Today

For each key account, create a 7x5 coordinate file, marking the customer's seven decision tasks and your five sales stages. Each position must be supported by customer behavior evidence. If there is no evidence, mark it as unknown and design the next question to fill the gap.

### Steps to Implement

1. **List your key accounts** and create a table with rows for the seven customer tasks and columns for the five sales stages.
2. **For each cell**, write down the current status based on observed customer actions. If unknown, leave blank.
3. **Identify gaps** where the sales stage is ahead of the customer task, and adjust your actions to align.
4. **Schedule regular reviews** to update the file with new evidence.

## Common Misconceptions

- Treating the sales five stages as a linear process that must be followed step by step.
- Treating the customer seven tasks as psychological activities guessed by intuition.
- Using sales actions as a substitute for customer behavior evidence.
- Equating winning the bid with signing, ignoring risk confirmation before signing.

These misconceptions distort process management. The real determinant of winning large contracts is not the final kick but whether the customer has completed each decision task and whether that completion is recorded with evidence.

## FAQ

### Q1: Why can't we just look at the winning result in large account sales?

A1: The winning result is the endpoint of the sales process but does not reflect which decision task the customer truly completed. Looking only at results hides stage misalignment, such as the customer still in value assessment while the salesperson thinks negotiation can begin. Using the dual-view funnel helps you identify ineffective actions and missing evidence, allowing you to improve even after a loss.

### Q2: What is the relationship between the sales five stages and the customer seven decision tasks?

A2: The sales five stages are the seller's progression axis, including contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. The customer seven tasks are the buyer's decision axis, from problem identification to commitment and signing. The two axes are multi-to-multi, not one-to-one. For example, the customer might be in solution confirmation while the salesperson is in contact or needs. The key is to judge whether the positions match, avoiding overstepping.

### Q3: How to determine if a customer has entered the "value assessment" stage?

A3: Look for customer behavior, not just verbal interest. Signals include the customer asking about ROI, requesting calculation logic, linking the problem to business numbers, or someone internally raising "is this worth doing?" If the customer only responds politely without these actions, they are not yet in that stage.

### Q4: What is buy-sell alignment and why is it important?

A4: Buy-sell alignment means your sales actions serve the customer's current decision task, aligning the seller's stage with the buyer's stage. If the customer is in responsibility assignment and you rush to present a solution, they will feel you don't understand their internal situation. Conversely, if the customer is in solution selection and you are still discussing needs, you miss the competitive window. Alignment ensures every interaction moves the customer's decision forward.

### Q5: How should a salesperson respond when the customer says "we need to study further"?

A5: First, distinguish between a real and false interruption. Ask three questions: What specific issues will be studied? Who needs to be involved? When will we get an answer? If the customer cannot answer, it might be a false interruption, and you need to go back to earlier stages to fill evidence. If they answer specifically, turn this study into the next genuine progress and schedule a follow-up, rather than waiting passively.

### Q6: Why can't negotiation be prepared only at the end?

A6: Negotiation is the fourth stage of the sales process, and preparation starts from the previous three stages. If needs, solution, and responsibility are not confirmed, negotiation becomes pure price haggling. Preparing a negotiation checklist in advance, clarifying which conditions can be conceded, which must be held, and what alternatives exist, allows you to exchange conditions for a sustainable agreement instead of improvising concessions.

### Q7: What is customer behavior evidence and why is it more reliable than visit records?

A7: Customer behavior evidence is observable actions the customer takes on decision tasks, such as putting budget on the meeting agenda, arranging user department participation in evaluation, or assigning an internal owner. Visit records only prove what the salesperson did, not what the customer advanced. Using customer behavior evidence to judge stages helps you know if your actions truly influence the decision chain, avoiding self-deception.

### Q8: How to manage multiple large account projects with the dual-view funnel?

A8: For each key account, create a 7x5 coordinate file, marking the customer's seven tasks and your five stages, with supporting evidence. Mark unknown positions and design next questions to fill them. Review regularly, prioritize resources to projects with sufficient evidence and alignment, and use the three-pronged approach to identify interruption causes for long-stalled projects.
