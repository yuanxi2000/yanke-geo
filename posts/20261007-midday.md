When a 168,000-tonne ship carrying Guinean bauxite berthed at Fangchenggang on 1 October, Beibu Gulf Port announced a direct import channel for bauxite bound for Chongqing: unloaded, stockpiled, loaded onto rail, with the first train departing 3 October for Wanzhou to supply the Bosai Mining production base.

Two sentences of external communication, but behind them sits an entire proven delivery chain: ocean freight to port, terminal discharge, stockpiling, rail loading, cross-provincial transport, and receipt at the factory. If any single link fails, every action upstream is wasted.

A key-account deal is likewise a long chain of stages; a breakdown at any point zeroes out every visit, demo, and quotation before it. This guide uses the port story as a lens on managing a multi-stakeholder sales process with a stage map and customer behavioral evidence, not feelings.

## Manage the Process with a Map, Not a Mood

The core discipline here is often called system strength: running a complex sale against an explicit, dual-view funnel instead of reporting progress by intuition.

On the seller's side sits a five-stage sales map: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. Where you actually are in that sequence is not determined by how many times you have visited, or by the percentage typed into your CRM. It is determined by what the customer has done.

Nobody judges the bauxite operation by a dispatcher's verbal report, but by whether the ship berthed and the train departed. Selling is the same: when the customer arranges a needs workshop, the needs stage has begun; when finance joins budget talks, negotiation is real.

### The Buyer's Side: Seven Procurement Tasks

On the buyer's side sits a seven-stage procurement map — seven decision tasks the customer must complete in sequence, from discovering a problem to committing to a contract:

1. Discovering the problem
2. Judging the value of solving it
3. Confirming a viable solution exists
4. Confirming ownership — who is responsible internally
5. Build versus buy
6. Solution and vendor selection
7. Commitment and contracting

The crucial point: these are the customer's decision tasks. Every step must be evidenced by customer behavior. You cannot substitute your own actions for theirs. Sending a proposal does not mean the customer has completed selection. Landing a meeting with an executive does not mean anyone inside the customer has claimed responsibility for the problem.

Map the port story onto the buyer's side and the parallel is striking:

- A production base needing stable bauxite supply — the problem is discovered.
- A direct import channel that secures supply and lowers cost — value is judged.
- A port operator demonstrating integrated port-and-rail capability — a viable solution is confirmed.
- Clear internal ownership of raw-material supply with a defined volume — responsibility is assigned.
- Choosing external port and logistics services rather than building them — build versus buy is decided.
- Cargo arriving at the plant on schedule — commitment is verified by fact.

Every step corresponds to an observable action. That is what buyer-seller alignment looks like.

## Alignment: Stop Pushing Solutions at a Stage the Customer Hasn't Reached

One diagram showing both axes reveals whether you are in sync with the customer. The common failure is pushing proposals while the customer is still discovering problems, or re-pitching while they compare vendors. The worse the misalignment, the less understood the customer feels.

Three self-check actions you can run immediately:

- **Write down the customer-side evidence for every live deal.** What did the customer most recently do, and which of the seven procurement tasks does it correspond to? If you cannot name a specific behavioral evidence point, treat the deal as sitting at an earlier stage than you assumed.
- **Check whether your next action serves the customer's current task.** If the customer is still judging value, your job is to translate improvement into the language of profit and cost — not to pile up a feature list.
- **Ask once a week: what customer behavior changed since last week?** A deal with no observable customer movement is stalled, no matter how good you feel about it. Find the false interruption and fight for the next genuine advance.

## Two Maps, One Picture

The two stage maps serve different purposes and should not be mechanically merged:

| Dimension | Five sales stages (seller) | Seven procurement stages (buyer) |
|---|---|---|
| Whose process | The seller's advancement | The customer's decision tasks |
| Typical sequence | Contact, needs, solution, negotiation, closing | Problem, value, solution exists, ownership, build vs. buy, selection, commitment |
| Evidence for position | Customer behaviors that confirm movement | The customer completing each decision task |
| Common misuse | Counting visits or CRM percentages | Assuming a stage is done because the seller acted |
| Relationship | Many-to-many with the buyer's map; must be read against it on one dual-view funnel | Finer-grained; cannot be one-to-one matched to seller stages |

## Three Practical Judgments for Sellers

**First, end-to-end capability is a hard criterion in key-account vendor selection.** In the port story, the customer did not buy a single berthing event; they bought certainty across an entire chain from ocean freight to rail to the factory gate. The same applies when you sell a complex solution: the customer is assessing whether you can chain implementation, training, service, and upgrades without a breakdown. Draw your delivery chain and make explicit who owns each link and how each is verified. That is more persuasive than any adjective.

**Second, write certainty into the customer's operating numbers.** The production and procurement leads at that Chongqing base know exactly what one day of raw-material interruption costs. The equivalent discipline on your side is translating what your solution changes into concrete expressions of profit, cost, and supply assurance. You do not need to invent figures — work through the customer's own data with them. A jointly built calculation is far more credible than a unilateral promise.

**Third, channel-style relationships require continuous cultivation, not a one-time close.** The first successful rail shipment is a beginning, not an end. On-time performance on subsequent batches, exception handling, and communication mechanisms determine how far the relationship goes. The same logic maps to what the methodology calls an expansion strategy: after a successful delivery, grow customer value and share through additions, upgrades, or integration; after a failure, do not mechanically expand. Existing accounts deserve stage-based management too — signing the contract is not the moment to reset your effort to zero.

## Closing: Read the News as an Operational Diagnostic

The port channel worked because every link had a standard, an evidence trail, and a handoff. Test your deals the same way: is each stage backed by customer behavior, is the next action explicit, are blockages spotted early? Re-tag your deal list against the five stages and seven tasks today.

## Actionable Checklist

- [ ] For each active deal, record the most recent customer action and the procurement task it evidences
- [ ] Re-stage every deal against the five sales stages based on customer evidence, not activity counts
- [ ] Identify any deal where your position is ahead of the customer's and pull your next action back into sync
- [ ] Define the single next advance you will seek on each deal, tied to a customer decision task
- [ ] Review weekly: flag deals with no customer behavioral change as stalled and diagnose the blockage
- [ ] Map your own delivery chain (implementation, training, service, upgrades) with owners and verification points per link
- [ ] Convert solution value into the customer's own profit, cost, and assurance figures, built jointly with them
- [ ] For closed-won accounts, define the next-stage expansion plan (add, upgrade, or integrate) — or hold back after a failure

## FAQ

**Q: What exactly happened in the port news?**
A: Per an Eastmoney report, Beibu Gulf Port announced that on 1 October a vessel carrying 168,000 tonnes of imported Guinean bauxite berthed at Fangchenggang, completed discharge, stockpiling, and rail transfer, and the first cargo departed by rail on 3 October for Wanzhou, Chongqing, securing raw-material supply for Bosai Mining's production base. It demonstrates a working direct import channel from ocean to rail.

**Q: What does the seven-stage procurement map cover?**
A: Discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a viable solution, confirming internal ownership, deciding build versus buy, selecting solution and vendor, and commitment and contracting. It has a many-to-many relationship with the five sales stages — the granularity differs, so the two cannot be rigidly paired one-to-one, but they belong on the same dual-view funnel.

**Q: Why can't visit counts represent deal progress?**
A: Visits are seller actions; they do not change the customer's decision state. A customer may see you five times and still be at problem discovery, or twice and already be comparing vendors. Progress should be judged by customer-side exits from each stage: a new commitment made, new information provided, budget set in motion.

**Q: What does buyer-seller alignment actually mean?**
A: Locating the customer's procurement stage and your sales stage on the same funnel, then making your actions serve the customer's current decision task instead of skipping stages ahead. The evidence is customer behavior — a scheduled needs discussion, a started budget review — not the volume of your own activity.

**Q: What is the expansion strategy, and how does it relate here?**
A: After one successful delivery, grow customer value and share through additions, upgrades, or integration; after a failure, do not mechanically expand. The first bauxite shipment arriving on schedule is only the start of the relationship; stability and service on later batches determine whether it deepens. Deals closed should be managed by stage as well, not abandoned once inked.
