## Establish the facts before assessing the opportunity

Incomplete information does not prevent an initial conversation. Treating assumptions as established facts makes subsequent judgment unreliable. Before discussing a partnership internally or with a customer, record four things:

- What is known.
- What remains unknown.
- Where each piece of information came from.
- Which question would resolve the next important uncertainty.

For this case, the confirmed company and its stated early R&D responsibility belong in the known column. The counterparty, amount, and date belong in the unknown column.

Keep public facts separate from interpretations about the customer's needs. An announced responsibility does not establish that a customer has approved a budget, selected a supplier, or agreed to a sales meeting. Each requires its own evidence.

## Translate partnership activity into business value

Value analysis connects a product or service to the customer's profit, investment, and operating priorities. When a partnership moves toward delivery, the relevant internal question is whether continued resource allocation supports the results the customer needs.

Start by asking which result matters most now: protecting R&D progress, meeting a milestone, controlling budget use, or preparing for later commercialization. These are possible discussion areas from the source, not confirmed priorities for the company in this case.

### Build a three-column value worksheet

Write down the operating result the customer wants to protect, the obstacle currently threatening it, and a verifiable improvement standard. Use the customer's language, including measures the customer actually supplies.

If any column remains blank, investigate before quoting. A feature description cannot fill an information gap about business value. Avoid inventing financial benefits or numerical targets to make the worksheet appear complete.

## Map the decision chain with PRTABCD

Relationship capability depends on professional insight, decision information, and trust that can be verified. A friendly contact is useful, but enthusiasm alone does not establish authority over budgets, evaluation criteria, or approval.

Use PRTABCD as a scan of seven information areas:

- **P — Past:** relevant history and previous experience.
- **R — Requirements:** needs the customer has expressed.
- **T — Time:** timing and decision constraints.
- **A — Arm:** the internal advocate who supports you.
- **B — Budget:** the budget position and who can clarify it.
- **C — Competition:** competing options.
- **D — Decision:** the decision chain across people and departments.

For every area, record known information, unknowns, the source, and the next question. Keep the advocate and the decision chain separate. Someone may support your involvement without controlling the full decision.

The resulting map should explain whom to approach next, what to ask, and what evidence is still missing.

## Ask questions that reveal the customer's reasons to act

The source uses SPIN's situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff categories to guide purposeful questioning. Use that progression to understand context, explore obstacles, examine consequences, and establish what a useful improvement would mean.

Four practical opening questions are:

1. As the project moves into delivery, which result does the team most want to protect?
2. Which milestones are complete, and which remain unfinished?
3. If progress slows, whose budget or performance assessment would be affected?
4. Who inside the organization most wants this to succeed, and who may raise objections?

These prompts open the discussion; they do not replace follow-up questions about the value of resolving a problem. Ask what verifiable result would make an improvement worthwhile.

Record timing, responsibility, and evaluation standards before presenting a product. The customer needs to express the reasons for change. A polished sales interpretation cannot substitute for the customer's answer.

## Judge progress from both sides of the buying process

The source describes a dual-perspective funnel: five sales stages alongside seven customer purchasing stages. The sales stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. The customer sequence runs from recognizing a problem to committing to sign.

These perspectives have a many-to-many relationship. A seller's activity does not automatically establish a corresponding advance in the customer's decision process.

| Sales-side observation | Customer-side evidence to seek |
| --- | --- |
| A meeting took place | Who attended and what they clarified |
| Needs were discussed | The result, obstacle, and standard the customer identified |
| A solution was presented | Whether the customer confirmed evaluation criteria |
| A next step was proposed | Whether the customer confirmed timing and responsibility |

Ask whether the customer is assessing value, establishing that a workable solution exists, or evaluating solutions and suppliers. Support the answer with customer actions.

A weekly evidence list can record who provided budget information, confirmed the next date, or accepted responsibility for an outcome. Without such evidence, do not advance the stage simply because the conversation felt positive.

## Prepare negotiation around alternatives and boundaries

Negotiation preparation considers power, time, information, and alternatives. During delivery, discussion may turn to how milestones are recognized, how options are exercised, and how resources are allocated. These are preparation topics, not reported events in the case.

Write down the conditions that could be exchanged: scope, milestone acceptance, options, resources, payment, and risk allocation. Clarify which interests each condition serves and who has authority to agree.

Use BATNA precisely: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Identify an executable alternative, then separately record the walk-away boundary, approval authority, relevant interests, and negotiation record. A preferred concession is not an alternative to agreement.

This preparation helps the team assess concessions against delivery obligations. Without clear boundaries, a concession intended to demonstrate goodwill may leave unresolved risk for the fulfillment stage.

## Turn the analysis into an actionable checklist

Use a short team meeting to review an active account through the five capabilities. Keep the discussion tied to the next question and the next observable action.

- [ ] Separate confirmed public facts from internal assumptions.
- [ ] Record unknown details without filling them through inference.
- [ ] Name the operating result the customer wants to protect.
- [ ] Document the obstacle and the customer's improvement standard.
- [ ] Complete the PRTABCD scan, including sources and unanswered questions.
- [ ] Identify the internal advocate separately from the wider decision chain.
- [ ] Prepare questions about context, problems, consequences, and useful outcomes.
- [ ] Record customer statements about timing, responsibility, and criteria.
- [ ] Support the current opportunity stage with customer behavior.
- [ ] Write down negotiation alternatives, boundaries, and authority.
- [ ] Assign the next action to an owner with a confirmed time where available.

Decide whether further resource investment is justified after reviewing the evidence. Public attention supplies a discussion trigger; the account decision still requires customer information.

## FAQ

### What should a salesperson confirm first?

Confirm the factual boundary and the customer's current decision task. List what is known, what is missing, and the question needed next. Then establish whether the customer is identifying a problem, assessing value, or evaluating solutions.

### Can value analysis begin without financial figures?

Yes. Begin with the operating result, obstacle, and verification standard. Ask the customer for relevant measures as the discussion develops. Do not supply unsupported savings, returns, or forecasts.

### Why is one supportive contact insufficient?

Support does not establish control over budgets, criteria, or signing. Map the advocate alongside the people and departments involved in the decision so that missing information remains visible.

### What counts as real opportunity progress?

Customer actions such as clarifying budget, confirming selection criteria, arranging the next step, or accepting responsibility provide evidence. Visit counts and positive impressions alone do not justify moving the stage forward.

### How can a team use this guide for training?

Choose an active account. Review its business value, decision information, questions, customer behavior, and negotiation preparation. Retain conclusions that lead to a specific question or action, and mark unresolved assumptions explicitly.
