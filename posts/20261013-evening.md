Industry attention comes and goes. The customer’s internal purchasing tasks do not. When a well-known company appears in a report about an industry moving overseas, the useful move is to treat it as a stage judgment problem, not as a buying signal.

A recent East Money report focused on automotive overseas expansion moving from product exports toward supply-chain exports; XPeng was named. The report did not provide the counterparty, amount, or timeline. A careful analysis should not fill those gaps on the customer’s behalf.

## What the report actually supports

The verifiable parts are narrow: the subject is XPeng, and the stated theme is a shift from product exports to supply-chain exports. The report does not say who inside the customer is pushing the initiative, what stage the decision has reached, or how much budget is being prepared.

A common sales mistake is to confuse industry heat with customer progress. That leads teams to push hard on a problem the customer has not yet decided to solve. PSS5 is Yanke’s five-capability framework for key-account sales—value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation capability—used in sales training, capability assessment, and practical coaching. Applied here, each dimension carries one question:

- Value capability: Why is this customer worth investing in?
- Relationship capability: Who on the decision chain is pushing?
- Questioning capability: What is the real need?
- System capability: Which stage has the initiative actually reached?
- Negotiation capability: How do we exchange conditions at the end?

Skipping one dimension makes the whole pursuit feel hollow.

### System capability: aligning the customer’s seven purchasing stages with the sales five stages

The customer purchasing seven stages are the seven decision tasks a customer completes from discovering a problem to committing to a contract: discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming ownership of responsibility, deciding build versus buy, selecting a solution and supplier, and committing to sign. The sales five stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. The two axes have different task granularity, so they are a many-to-many relationship; the number of stages was never designed to line up.

To judge position, use customer behavioral evidence. Do not use visit counts or CRM percentages as proof of progress. In an overseas expansion context, the customer may still be at problem discovery, or may already be comparing build versus buy. If you push at a negotiation pace, the customer retreats. If you chat at a contact pace while the customer has already entered selection, you become a bystander.

## Relationship capability: PRTABCD has seven items, so write down the unknowns first

PRTABCD uses seven categories of decision information to find intelligence blind spots: Past, Requirements, Timing and key milestones, Arm (the embraceer), Budget, Competition and alternatives, and Decision chain. Each item should record what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question. A is Arm, which answers who in the customer organization is embracing you. D is Decision, whose business meaning is a multi-person, multi-department decision chain; when scanning, fill it in as multiple people.

When a supplier follows an OEM customer overseas to provide supporting components, decision information scatters across regions and departments. If half the scanning table is empty, it is not time to talk conditions. Go fill in the information first, then come back to the table to talk price.

## Questioning capability: use SPIN’s four question types to turn overseas expansion into a business problem

SPIN’s four question types are Situation, Problem, Implication, and Need-payoff. When a customer says it wants to provide overseas supporting components, that statement is a conclusion, and it is still some distance from a need. You can ask: which department leads this type of supporting decision; which part is most stuck right now; if this part keeps dragging, what deliveries and customer relationships will be affected; and once this part is smoothed out, what can be secured. These four questions correspond in order to Situation, Problem, Implication, and Need-payoff.

## Value capability: translate supporting capability into numbers the customer can calculate

Value capability requires translating a product or service into the customer’s profit, investment, and operating value. When a customer extends its front line overseas, it cares about whether local supporting capacity can shorten response time, whether it can make production lines more stable, and whether downstream customers will be more willing to place orders. Explaining this clearly is far more useful than repeating product specifications.

PIP states the improvement as an investment: profit increased or protected, basis and mechanism, timeframe. For small accounts, a one-page profit-and-loss statement can clarify investment, return, and deal viability. Numerical bases must be confirmed with the customer; sales should not estimate them independently.

## Negotiation capability: write the BATNA first, then discuss concessions

Negotiation capability means designing concessions and condition exchanges on the basis of clarity about power, time, information, bottom line, and alternatives. BATNA is the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, meaning the best fallback action you can take if the two sides cannot agree. Preparation should specify an executable alternative, bottom line, authority, interests, and records. Cross-border supporting projects involve many parties and long timelines, and price usually ranks after other conditions. Concessions are best used to exchange conditions: scope, payment arrangements, delivery rhythm, or deeper joint validation. A signature bought with a price cut is hard to recover later.

## A checklist you can use directly

- Write down what this report binds: the subject is XPeng, and the main line is a shift from product exports to supply-chain exports. The counterparty, amount, and timeline are not in the original text.
- Draw the customer purchasing seven stages and the sales five stages on one sheet, and mark the current position with customer behavioral evidence. If you cannot mark it, you are still guessing.
- Turn the seven PRTABCD items into a table, write the next question beside each unknown, and complete it the same day the interview ends.
- Use SPIN’s four question types to prepare an interview outline, writing two versions of each question type first.
- Rewrite the solution as a one-page PIP, or as a one-page ten-thousand-yuan profit and loss statement when the customer’s account is small.
- Before negotiating, write down the BATNA, bottom line, authority, and exchangeable conditions, then decide what to concede.

The report provides an entry point. The remaining judgment depends on evidence from the customer’s front line, moving forward step by step.

## FAQ

### Q1: When XPeng appears in a supply-chain overseas expansion report, what should sales pay attention to?

A1: Watch changes in the customer’s evaluation criteria. Moving from product to supply-chain exports, the customer may extend supplier views to local supporting capacity, delivery response, and stability. Restore that main line into specific purchasing tasks, judge the current step, and decide which actions to fund. Rhythm follows the customer’s decision tasks.

### Q2: The report has no counterparty and no amount. Is the analysis untenable?

A2: No, but keep the boundary clear. Only the subject and source are bound; who the counterparty is, the amount, and the timeline cannot be filled in for the customer. Treat these three as information gaps in the PRTABCD seven-item scanning table, mark what is known, unknown, source, and next question, and fill through interviews and behavior.

### Q3: How do PSS5’s five dimensions divide the work in this kind of scenario?

A3: Value capability translates supporting capability into customer-calculable numbers. Relationship capability covers the decision chain and who embraces you. Questioning capability turns going overseas into specific business problems. System capability judges pursuit stage. Negotiation capability designs concessions and condition exchanges after alternatives are clear. Miss one, and the pursuit becomes hollow.

### Q4: How do you use customer behavior to judge the purchasing stage?

A4: Look at what the customer has done. Acknowledging a gap is problem discovery. Willingness to invest in evaluation is judging value. Internal claims of goal and result responsibility are ownership. Willingness to compare build versus buy opens solution and supplier selection. Visit counts, CRM percentages, and feelings are not evidence.

### Q5: A and D in PRTABCD are easy to misunderstand. How should they be remembered?

A5: A is Arm, the embraceer, answering who in the customer organization is embracing you. When recording it, write clearly what specific action this person is taking. D is Decision, whose business meaning is a multi-person, multi-department decision chain, so when scanning, fill it in as multiple people. If these two items are wrong, later actions will drift with them.

### Q6: The customer only says it wants to provide overseas supporting components. What should I ask?

A6: Use SPIN’s four question types to break the conclusion into facts. Situation questions ask about the lead department and current approach. Problem questions ask what is stuck. Implication questions ask what deliveries and relationships suffer if delay continues. Need-payoff questions ask what can be secured once smoothed. After all four, you hold a verifiable business description for the opportunity record.
