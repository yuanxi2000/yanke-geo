Order growth and rising material prices can coexist without creating a ready market for an efficiency solution. A PCB manufacturer may be adding capacity while still evaluating a new process. It may recognize cost pressure without having approved a budget to address it. For a key-account salesperson, these distinctions determine what to investigate, what to propose, and when to negotiate.

This guide applies the five PSS5 capabilities: value, relationships, questioning, systematic opportunity management, and negotiation. Each becomes a practical check on whether an opportunity has progressed.

## Value: turn efficiency into an operating calculation

An efficiency proposal needs a customer-verifiable economic basis. Translate process improvements into possible changes in material cost, yield, energy consumption, delivery stability, and rework risk. For a manufacturer serving several production scales, changeover speed may also deserve investigation. These are hypotheses to test, not benefits already demonstrated at Xunjiexing.

Ask which cost is absorbing the profit contribution from growing orders. Then connect the proposed improvement to investment, operating benefit, and profit. Could it reduce rework, avoid a stoppage, or support additional orders? The customer must supply the relevant operating context before those possibilities become a credible business case.

### Build a five-item efficiency worksheet

Bring a worksheet covering material cost, yield, energy consumption, delivery stability, and rework risk. For each item, record:

- The customer's current concern and available baseline.
- The proposed improvement and the assumptions behind it.
- The data or trial needed to evaluate the change.
- The person responsible for supplying or validating evidence.

Ask the customer to select the most pressing item. Agree on a trial run or a defined set of production data to examine it. If the economics remain unclear, resolve that gap before treating a quotation as the next milestone.

## Questioning: uncover the constraint behind the request

Organize discovery around background, difficulty, impact, and the value of change. Start with the customer's immediate decision task and develop questions from the answers.

Useful opening questions include:

- Which product categories account for the order growth?
- Which materials and process steps face the greatest price pressure?
- Where does the existing production flow lose efficiency?
- Is the additional line intended to close a capacity gap, expand process capability, or address both?

Once the constraint is clearer, investigate its consequences. If material prices continue rising, which cost becomes hardest to control? If process introduction takes longer, which orders could become difficult to serve? These questions explore exposure; they do not establish that losses have occurred.

Then ask what the customer would commit to evaluating: a particular product, line, data set, or trial window. Willingness to share data, appoint a process owner, or arrange a trial provides evidence that discovery is supporting a decision. A detailed conversation alone does not.

## Relationships: map support and decision gaps

Relationship capability combines professional credibility, decision information, and dependable action. Use PRTABCD to organize seven areas of account knowledge:

- P: Past history.
- R: Requirements.
- T: Timing.
- A: Arm, identifying who actively supports you.
- B: Budget.
- C: Competition.
- D: Decision, covering the decision chain across people and departments.

An enthusiastic contact may provide valuable support while leaving budget authority or process ownership unresolved. Record what each person cares about: delivery, material cost, process validation, or investment return. Add an explicit entry for what remains unknown.

Maintain this information separately for Shenzhen, Xinfeng, and Zhuhai. A finding at one production base does not establish conditions at another. For every gap, record the information source, the next question, and an observable action that could resolve it.

Assess relationship progress through access to decision information and validation activity. Visit frequency and social contact do not establish purchasing readiness.

## Systematic opportunity management: separate the workstreams

PSS5 uses two perspectives on progress: five sales stages and seven customer purchasing stages. The supplied material does not enumerate the five sales stages, so retain your established labels without inventing a sequence here. The seven purchasing stages are:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value.
3. Establishing that a solution exists.
4. Confirming responsibility.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Committing and signing.

Assess capacity construction and new-process introduction separately using both perspectives. Capacity work could be approaching supplier selection while process work still needs evidence of feasibility or agreement on responsibility. This is an illustration of possible divergence, not a claim about Xunjiexing's actual purchasing position.

Look for a trial window, a named process owner, validation requirements incorporated into procurement, or a budget discussion. Record what each action establishes and what remains unresolved. A trial window supports validation activity; it does not establish budget approval.

If the customer is still determining whether a workable solution exists, help resolve that question. Treating the opportunity as quotation-ready would outrun the available evidence.

## Negotiation: exchange executable conditions

Material pressure may prompt price demands or delayed purchasing. Prepare by clarifying relative bargaining strength, timing, information, limits, authority, and alternatives. Then connect concessions to conditions the parties can actually carry out.

If the customer requests a reduction before validation, seek agreement on the validation scope, trial window, and decision participants as part of the next-step arrangement. If it requests longer payment terms, explore an exchange involving clearer purchasing volume or faster acceptance. These are possible negotiation structures, not reported customer agreements.

Other variables include phased scope, delivery schedules, validation responsibilities, and risk allocation. Specify who will do what and when.

If using BATNA, apply its contractual meaning: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, meaning the best available action if agreement cannot be reached. Document executable alternatives, minimum acceptable terms, authority, interests, and the negotiation record. A sustainable agreement requires workable commitments beyond the signature.

## FAQ

### What should a salesperson establish first?

Clarify whether the immediate decision concerns capacity construction, process introduction, or both. Keep separate records when their tasks and evidence differ.

### Does order growth establish demand for an efficiency solution?

It supports investigation. It does not establish willingness to pay, an approved budget, or readiness to purchase.

### What makes an efficiency claim usable?

A customer-relevant measure, operating data, and an agreed way to evaluate the proposed change. Product specifications alone do not establish an economic return.

### What counts as opportunity progress?

Customer actions tied to a decision task, such as providing data, naming a process owner, arranging a trial, or discussing budget. Interpret each action within its actual scope.

### What cannot be concluded from this source?

It does not establish new-process volume production, purchasing commitments, or the missing counterparty, amount, and date. Recommendations in this guide describe possible next steps, not actions the customer has already taken.
