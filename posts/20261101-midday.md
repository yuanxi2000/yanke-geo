Industrial development can reveal sales opportunities, but it does not establish that a customer is ready to buy. A cluster may connect several organizations while leaving each with its own priorities, purchasing process, and decision timetable. The practical task is to turn that context into evidence about individual buying projects.

The supplied source describes COMAC, Aviation Industry Corporation of China, and Aero Engine Corporation of China establishing a presence in Nanchang. It quotes COMAC chairman He Dongfeng on their contribution to a chain spanning aircraft manufacturing, components, and propulsion support. For key-account sellers, the useful question is how those connections affect customer decisions.

This guide applies the source's five-capability approach to that question. Its examples are working hypotheses to investigate, not confirmed purchasing opportunities.

## Start with five complementary capabilities

A complex account requires more than access to a contact or a persuasive proposal. The source organizes professional key-account selling around five capabilities that work together.

| Capability | Practical focus | Working output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Value | Translate the offer into profit, investment, and operating value | An investment explanation |
| Relationships | Understand and cover the customer decision chain | A decision map |
| Questioning | Discover needs through purposeful questions | A sequence of SPIN questions |
| Systems | Track buyer decisions alongside seller activity | A dual-perspective funnel |
| Negotiation | Prepare alternatives and conditional exchanges | A negotiation brief |

Use these capabilities together. A strong contact cannot substitute for an understood decision process, and a detailed proposal cannot establish value that the customer has not recognized.

## Track buying decisions separately from selling activity

The source's systems capability uses two perspectives. The seller's five stages are contact, needs, proposal, negotiation, and close. The customer's seven purchasing stages are:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Confirming responsibility and ownership.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Committing and signing.

These are many-to-many relationships. Do not force each seller stage into one corresponding buyer stage. Instead, ask what decision the customer is making and what work the seller must complete to support it.

In an industrial cluster, a component manufacturer might already be selecting solutions while a propulsion-related business is still assessing value. Their connection to the same ecosystem does not establish a shared purchasing stage.

### Diagnose alignment with observable evidence

If the customer has confirmed responsibility but the seller remains at initial contact, seller activity may be behind the buying process. If the seller is developing a proposal before the customer has confirmed that a solution exists, the seller may be advancing prematurely.

Record the customer behavior supporting each assessment. Visit counts and seller confidence do not establish purchasing progress. Where evidence is missing, retain the uncertainty.

Maintain a separate dual-perspective funnel for each project. One organization may have several purchasing initiatives with different owners and decision tasks.

## Map the decision chain across organizations

Relationship capability means understanding who participates in a decision and building professional, verifiable trust. It should not be reduced to entertainment, personal familiarity, or the number of contacts collected.

The Nanchang scenario involves organizations with independent procurement processes. A supportive person in one company may help, while relevant decisions extend into another organization. Investigate that possibility without assuming that every connected company controls the purchase.

Use PRTABCD to scan seven areas:

- Past information: What relevant history is known?
- Requirements: What does the customer need?
- Timing: What timetable matters?
- Arm: Who inside the customer organization embraces your involvement?
- Budget: What is known about available funding?
- Competitors and alternatives: What other options are under consideration?
- Decision: Which people and departments participate in deciding?

In this tool, A is fixed as Arm and D as Decision. An Arm is a supporter; that support alone does not establish decision authority.

For example, a supporter could work for a component manufacturer while the decision chain reaches the aircraft manufacturer. The practical response is to identify the other roles and understand their involvement. Recording only the supportive contact leaves the account picture incomplete.

## Use questions to test the operating problem

Industry news gives context for inquiry. It does not tell you which problem a particular customer has or how seriously that customer views it.

The source uses SPIN: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions. Build a purposeful sequence rather than asking disconnected discovery questions. For a possible component-delivery issue, that sequence could be:

- Situation: Where does your operation sit in the aircraft supply chain?
- Problem: Where, if anywhere, do delivery schedules become difficult to meet?
- Implication: If those delays occur, how could they affect downstream aircraft delivery?
- Need-payoff: What operating value would more reliable delivery create?

These questions are illustrative. They do not establish that a customer has delivery problems or that delays have affected aircraft production.

Let the answers determine the next question. If the customer does not identify a difficulty, do not proceed as though the problem were proven. If a difficulty exists, explore its consequences so the customer can assess the value of changing it.

## Turn the offer into an investment explanation

Value capability translates products and services into customer profit, investment, and operating value. In a connected industrial setting, customers may consider longer-term coordination as well as an individual purchase price. Treat that emphasis as something to verify.

The source recommends PIP, a profit improvement plan. Write it as an investment explanation addressing three questions:

1. How much profit could the proposal create or protect?
2. Through what mechanism, and with what confidence?
3. Over what period could that value be realized?

Use customer-supported operating figures where available. Distinguish established inputs from assumptions, and leave unsupported amounts unresolved. The supplied material provides no basis for inserting financial results.

For a delivery-related proposal, explain the connection between the proposed change and the operating consequence the customer has identified. That makes the value argument reviewable without claiming a guaranteed outcome.

## Prepare negotiation before pressure arrives

The source suggests that connected buyers may have stronger bargaining power. That is a possibility to assess, not an established fact about every organization in a cluster.

Review power, time, information, limits, and alternatives before negotiation. A delivery deadline could affect timing pressure; relevant coordination data could affect the information balance. Verify those circumstances rather than treating them as automatic advantages.

Prepare a BATNA: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Identify what you can actually do if agreement is not reached, alongside your limits, authority, interests, and negotiation record.

Keep an alternative outside the agreement distinct from changes to the proposed deal. Adjusting scope or payment terms is ordinarily a negotiating option; it does not by itself explain what happens if talks fail.

When the buyer requests a lower price, revisit the supported value case and prepare conditional exchanges. Any concession should have a clear purpose and corresponding condition. The objective is an agreement both sides can sustain.

## Actionable account checklist

Before advancing an opportunity, review the following:

- [ ] Define the specific purchasing project being assessed.
- [ ] Complete the PRTABCD scan and mark information gaps.
- [ ] Identify the Arm and the other decision-chain participants.
- [ ] Check whether relevant decisions cross organizational boundaries.
- [ ] Record the customer's purchasing stage and supporting behavior.
- [ ] Record the seller's stage separately.
- [ ] Identify whether the two perspectives are aligned or mismatched.
- [ ] Prepare SPIN questions for the unresolved customer decision.
- [ ] Draft a PIP with mechanisms, timing, and explicit uncertainty.
- [ ] Define an executable BATNA, limits, and negotiation authority.
- [ ] Prepare conditional exchanges before making concessions.

Use the review to choose the next action that serves the customer's current decision task. Revisit it as customer behavior changes.

## FAQ

### Does an industrial cluster represent one sales opportunity?

It may contain several opportunities, but shared geography or supply-chain connections do not establish a single buying process. Assess each purchasing project independently.

### Is a supportive contact enough to qualify the relationship?

No. An Arm identifies support inside the customer organization. Decision-chain coverage requires understanding the other people and departments involved.

### Must the five sales stages match the seven purchasing stages?

No. Their relationship is many-to-many. Track both perspectives and use customer behavior to assess alignment.

### When should a seller avoid advancing a proposal?

When it would move ahead of the customer's unresolved decision tasks. The source's example is proposing before the customer has confirmed that a solution exists.

### What if the financial value is still unknown?

Keep it unresolved. Use questions to clarify the operating mechanism and relevant figures rather than filling the gap with an invented return.
