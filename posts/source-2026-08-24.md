# From Strategic Agreement to Joint Action: A GitHub Pages Field Guide for Large-Account Sales

Strategic agreements between major enterprises—like the publicly announced partnership between Haier Group and Lenovo Group in August 2026—signal more than relationship milestones. They represent five distinct, parallel opportunity streams: data center and compute infrastructure; intelligent manufacturing and industrial internet; smart home and AIoT ecosystems; global supply chain resource alignment; and industry standards and co-innovation. Yet signing a document does not launch projects. It launches the work of translation: turning high-level directions into jointly owned, evidence-based actions with clear ownership, measurable outcomes, and repeatable rhythm.

This guide distills practical discipline from observable patterns in large-account collaboration. It is written for sales professionals, account leads, and cross-functional engagement teams who must move beyond ceremony into execution—without inventing steps, overpromising outcomes, or relying on informal goodwill alone.

## Why Strategic Agreements Are Not Projects

A strategic agreement is not a project. It is a *set of five independent opportunity domains*, each with its own:

- Business stakeholders (often non-overlapping across domains),
- Budget sources and approval paths,
- Technical constraints and integration dependencies,
- Success criteria and risk profiles,
- And timelines—some near-term, others multi-year.

Treating all five as one initiative invites misalignment. Prioritizing only the most visible domain—or deferring action until “the right moment”—lets momentum decay. The real test begins after the press release: Can both sides name, by next week, *one* concrete scenario where shared effort will produce verifiable evidence?

Without that discipline, strategy remains abstract. With it, even small validations build credibility, surface hidden blockers, and reinforce accountability.

## Translating Five Directions Into Joint Action

Each of the five cooperation areas reflects a distinct business challenge—not a technical capability list. Effective translation starts by reframing each direction as a question about current operations, not future possibilities.

### Data Center and Compute Infrastructure

Avoid leading with hardware specs or cloud capacity. Instead, ask: Which business growth driver is currently constrained by infrastructure? Where are latency, cost volatility, or scaling delays impacting time-to-market or service reliability? Who owns the P&L impact of those constraints—and what baseline metrics reflect them today?

The goal is not to propose infrastructure. It is to co-identify a bottleneck that both parties can measure, monitor, and improve together.

### Intelligent Manufacturing and Industrial Internet

Do not begin with platform features. Begin with one operational pain point: quality yield variance, unplanned equipment downtime, energy consumption per unit, production schedule adherence, or on-time delivery rate. For that single point, define:

- Current performance (with documented source),
- Target improvement (quantified and time-bound),
- Required data inputs and system access,
- Minimum viable validation period (e.g., four weeks of live monitoring),
- And who confirms success.

Public references to “compute + platform + scenario” underscore a simple truth: value emerges only when capability meets context.

### Smart Home and AIoT Ecosystems

Ecosystem work fails when responsibility is diffuse. Before proposing integrations or APIs, clarify boundaries: Who owns the end-user journey? Which team validates interoperability? Where do data residency, consent, and error-handling responsibilities land? What happens if a joint feature degrades user retention or increases support load?

Shared ownership requires explicit handoffs—not shared aspirations.

### Global Supply Chain Resource Alignment

“Resource sharing” is too vague. Translate it into mutual metrics: forecast accuracy over 13 weeks, average inventory cover days, supplier lead-time variability, order-to-delivery cycle time, or mean time to resolve supply exceptions. Both sides must agree on how to collect, verify, and interpret each metric—before committing resources.

Without jointly owned indicators, coordination becomes ad hoc, not structural.

### Industry Standards and Co-Innovation

These efforts involve longer horizons and broader stakeholder groups. To avoid indefinite discussion, establish governance early: Who initiates new workstreams? What budget and expert time is committed per initiative? How are interim outputs reviewed and approved? What defines “completed contribution” versus “ongoing participation”? Where do intellectual property rights and usage rights begin and end?

Strategic scope demands operational specificity.

## The Five Levers for Sustainable Progress

Five interlocking disciplines convert direction into motion. None works in isolation.

**Relationship leverage** means identifying, naming, and confirming commitment from four roles per domain: the business owner (P&L or outcome accountability), the technical owner (system access and integration authority), the resource owner (budget or headcount control), and the internal advocate (senior sponsor who escalates roadblocks). High-level sponsorship opens doors; mid-level ownership moves work forward.

**Questioning leverage** means replacing solution talk with diagnostic inquiry. Ask: What has changed recently to make this urgent? What would deteriorate if no action were taken? What evidence would convince you this is working—or failing? Let the customer articulate the problem before offering the answer.

**Value leverage** means defining value as a testable hypothesis—not a feature list. Example: “Integrating predictive maintenance alerts into shift supervisor dashboards will reduce unplanned downtime by ≥15% over 90 days, measured against Q2 2026 baseline.” Baseline, target, measurement method, and timeframe must be agreed *before* design begins.

**Negotiation leverage** extends beyond pricing. Clarify trade-offs early: data access scope, change control process, escalation path for unresolved issues, exit conditions, and decision rights for scope adjustments. Ambiguity here creates friction later.

**System leverage** is the simplest but most critical: maintain one living document—the Joint Action Table. Update it after every joint meeting. It need not be complex. Its power lies in consistency and shared visibility.

## Joint Action Table: Minimum Viable Structure

| Field | Purpose | Example |
|-------|---------|---------|
| Cooperation Area | Which of the five directions? | Intelligent manufacturing |
| Specific Scenario | Narrowest testable activity | Predictive alert integration for CNC Line 3 |
| Business Problem | Customer-defined pain, with evidence | 22% unplanned downtime in May; root cause logs show bearing failure pattern |
| Joint Owners | Names and titles, internal to each organization | Haier Plant Ops Lead + Lenovo Edge Analytics Lead |
| Current Evidence | Verified baseline data source | CMMS log export, May 1–31, 2026 |
| Testable Hypothesis | If X changes, Y improves by Z within T | Alert integration reduces bearing-related downtime by ≥18% in 60 days |
| Value Metric | How success is measured and by whom | Downtime minutes tracked in CMMS; validated weekly by Haier reliability team |
| Next Milestone | First irreversible action | Haier grants API access to Line 3 sensor feed by Aug 30 |

This table is not a contract annex. It is a coordination tool—updated, referenced, and held accountable in every follow-up.

## Six Questions to Answer in the First Joint Meeting

Within 30 days of agreement signing, the first structured joint session must produce answers to these six questions:

- Which of the five areas offers the strongest near-term signal of mutual benefit—and why?
- What specific, observable business problem does it address? Where is the evidence?
- Who, on each side, holds formal accountability for results—and who controls necessary resources?
- What is the smallest scenario we will jointly validate first? What defines completion?
- Which metric will prove progress—and whose data source establishes the baseline?
- What is the next irreversible action the customer will take—and by when?

If any answer is “we’ll decide later,” the meeting was premature. If all six are captured in the Joint Action Table and scheduled for review in 14 days, momentum has begun.

## FAQ

**What is the first step after signing a strategic agreement?**  
Select one cooperation area. Convene core owners from both sides. Draft a Joint Action Table for a single, bounded scenario—including problem statement, baseline evidence, testable hypothesis, and next irreversible action. Do not wait for perfection. Publish and review.

**Why do strong executive relationships sometimes fail to produce results?**  
Executive alignment authorizes direction—but execution depends on middle managers, engineers, procurement, and operations. Without named owners, shared metrics, and recurring joint review, strategy stays at the principle level.

**Should all five directions advance simultaneously?**  
No. Prioritize by urgency, measurability, resource availability, and decision simplicity. Run one scenario to validation, learn, then replicate the method—not the solution—to other areas.

**What fields must a Joint Action Table include?**  
Cooperation area, specific scenario, business problem, joint owners, current evidence, testable hypothesis, value metric, next milestone, and date of last update. Every field must have an owner and be updated after each joint interaction.

**How is value quantified meaningfully?**  
Anchor metrics in existing operational KPIs: cycle time, defect rate, inventory turns, forecast accuracy, uptime, or user task completion rate. Baseline and target must come from the customer’s systems—not estimates.

**How do you know strategy has become a real project?**  
When the customer takes an irreversible action: assigns a named owner, grants system access, allocates budget or FTE time, agrees to share baseline data, or commits a milestone to their official calendar. Verbal alignment is necessary—but insufficient.
