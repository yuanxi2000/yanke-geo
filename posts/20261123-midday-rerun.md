Large procurement opportunities demand more than a strong relationship or an attractive price. A supplier must understand which decisions the customer has already made, which remain unresolved, and what evidence would justify the next commitment. Activity alone does not establish progress.

The supplied source illustrates this challenge through China Mobile's stated second batch of centralized procurement for next-generation AI supernode equipment for 2026–2027: 52,160 accelerator cards, with six to seven suppliers planned for selection. These figures describe the procurement discussed in the source, not a verified award outcome.

The durable lesson is process alignment. PSS5's process-management capability uses a dual-perspective funnel to connect the seller's work with the buyer's decision process. This guide explains how to apply that approach without confusing customer friendliness, internal sales activity, or negotiation pressure with genuine advancement.

## Start with the customer's decision task

A conventional sales funnel describes the seller's work: contact, needs discovery, solution development, negotiation, and closing. That view becomes more useful when paired with the customer's purchasing decisions.

The source identifies seven buyer stages:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value or importance.
3. Confirming that a workable solution exists.
4. Establishing internal ownership and responsibility.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Making a commitment and signing.

These stages provide a diagnostic structure, not a rigid timetable. Their relationship with sales stages is many-to-many. One sales activity may support several customer decisions, while one customer decision may require several kinds of supplier support.

Before choosing an action, identify the decision the buyer is trying to complete. A technically detailed proposal may help during supplier selection but miss the point when the customer is still debating whether the problem deserves investment. Likewise, introductory visits may add little when a tender already requires a compliant solution and credible delivery plan.

## Map sales activity to procurement progress

Use the following comparison as a working map. Confirm the fit through customer behavior rather than treating the table as an automatic stage assignment.

| Seller stage | Buyer decisions it may support | Practical focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | Recognize the problem; assess value | Gain access to information and establish trust |
| Needs discovery | Assess value; confirm feasibility | Clarify the problem and request supporting data |
| Solution development | Assign ownership; build or buy; select suppliers | Address configuration, compatibility, and delivery |
| Negotiation | Complete selection; prepare commitment | Resolve terms, price, risk allocation, and compliance |
| Closing | Commit and sign | Establish the agreement and implementation handoff |

A tender announcement may indicate that the customer has already recognized the problem and assessed its value. It does not prove that every internal question is settled. The seller should establish what remains open before deciding where to concentrate effort.

In the supernode example, useful solution work could include a configuration aligned with the tender specifications, compatibility test documentation, and a clear delivery path. The supplier must substantiate its ability to deliver at the relevant scale; a broad statement of ambition does not answer that question.

### Diagnose a mismatch before adding activity

When an opportunity stalls, compare your current sales stage with the buyer's observable work. If you are discussing price while the buyer is still establishing feasibility, commercial concessions may leave the central uncertainty untouched.

If the buyer is evaluating suppliers while your team is still seeking basic introductions, the immediate gap may be technical or delivery evidence. Choose an action that helps complete the unresolved decision, then look for the customer's response.

## Replace impressions with behavioral evidence

A positive meeting is useful context, but it is insufficient evidence for advancing a forecast. The source emphasizes concrete customer actions such as:

- Inviting the supplier's technical team to a proof-of-concept test.
- Including the supplier on a shortlist for commercial clarification.
- Circulating the proposal internally for review by other departments.
- Arranging a meeting involving senior decision-makers.

Each action needs interpretation. A test invitation supports the conclusion that technical evaluation is occurring; it does not establish that purchasing approval or contract commitment has followed. Record the action and the decision it appears to support.

A practical opportunity record can capture the current buyer decision, observed behavior, unresolved question, and next customer action. Keep observations separate from interpretations. “The customer circulated our proposal for departmental review” is an observation. “We are likely to win” remains an interpretation.

If interactions repeatedly produce praise without substantive follow-through, reassess the opportunity's progress and your approach. More supplier activity does not necessarily resolve the customer's obstacle.

## Establish ownership across the buying organization

Internal responsibility deserves explicit attention. A network department, an IT department, and a business unit may evaluate the same infrastructure through different priorities. The source suggests that network teams may emphasize stability and operational convenience, while business teams may focus more on computing-resource scheduling efficiency. These are possibilities to investigate, not assumptions about every department.

Use questions to establish:

- Which department is driving the expansion and why.
- Who owns the problem and its consequences.
- Which functions must review the proposal.
- Who can support the initiative internally.
- Which concerns remain unresolved across departments.

An internal supporter matters because proposals must move through the customer's organization. However, identifying a supportive contact does not replace understanding responsibility, compliance requirements, or the wider decision chain.

Respond to the priorities you uncover in the solution itself. Technical material, delivery arrangements, and commercial discussions should address the concerns of the people evaluating them.

## Compete without making price the default response

A procurement planning several selected suppliers creates a competitive setting that requires attention to other offers as well as your own. The source recommends monitoring changes in competitor pricing, service standards, and technical positioning.

Use that information to assess your proposal's relevance and your negotiating alternatives. BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. In this setting, it includes considering other regional or industry opportunities if the current procurement does not produce an acceptable agreement. An alternative should be assessed realistically rather than assumed to exist.

When requirements or evaluation priorities change, revisit the proposal before changing price. If an increased weighting on a technical criterion exposes a weakness, a discount alone may not address it.

Sudden customer silence or tougher conditions also require diagnosis. The source discusses apparent interruptions that may reflect negotiation tactics, but it also names possible underlying causes such as budget constraints, internal disagreements, or interest in a competing offer. Do not assume a bluff. Check the decision stage, missing stakeholders, and unresolved concerns before conceding or withdrawing.

## Coordinate the team around decision requirements

Large opportunities involve technical, commercial, delivery, and legal questions that one account manager may struggle to cover alone. The source recommends coordination led by an experienced account manager, with support from presales engineers, delivery specialists, and senior leaders where appropriate.

Assign work according to the buyer's current decision. Presales specialists can support feasibility and compatibility evaluation. Delivery specialists can clarify implementation arrangements. Commercial and legal contributors can address terms, responsibilities, and risk allocation.

Keep communications and documents consistent across these roles. The purpose is to give the customer coherent answers and maintain continuity throughout a lengthy procurement process.

## Actionable opportunity-review checklist

Use this checklist before changing the forecast or committing substantial additional effort:

- [ ] Name the buyer decision currently being completed.
- [ ] Record the customer behavior supporting that assessment.
- [ ] Check whether your sales activity matches that decision.
- [ ] Identify the internal owner, supporter, and reviewing functions.
- [ ] List unresolved technical, delivery, commercial, or compliance questions.
- [ ] Assign each question to an appropriate team member.
- [ ] Define the next observable customer action.
- [ ] Review competitive changes and realistic alternatives.
- [ ] Investigate interruptions before changing terms.
- [ ] Confirm agreement and implementation handoff before treating the opportunity as closed.

## FAQ

### How does this differ from a standard sales funnel?

It adds the buyer's seven decision stages to the seller's five activity stages. Progress depends on evidence of customer decisions, rather than the seller's activity labels alone.

### Must the stages match one-to-one?

No. Needs discovery may span value assessment and feasibility confirmation. Solution development may support ownership, the build-or-buy decision, and supplier selection.

### What counts as meaningful evidence?

Concrete actions such as a test invitation, shortlist inclusion, or internal proposal review. Interpret each action narrowly; none automatically proves that the entire purchase is approved.

### Should a stalled opportunity receive a lower price?

First investigate the obstacle. Unclear ownership, technical concerns, or compliance questions require responses that address those issues. Price may be relevant, but it is not a universal remedy.

### Can smaller suppliers use this approach?

Yes. The source presents the decision logic as applicable beyond very large procurements. Smaller teams can use it to focus limited resources on opportunities showing substantive customer progress.
