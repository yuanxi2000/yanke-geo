A capital-intensive expansion announcement gives a sales team useful context, but it does not reveal the customer's investment case. The practical task is to connect money, capacity, timing, and risk in an operating picture the customer can check. Product specifications and delivery promises become more useful when their business implications are explicit.

The supplied source uses Shanghai Silicon Industry's continued silicon-wafer capacity construction to explore this task. Its central lesson is a working discipline: separate reported facts from sales hypotheses, identify the customer's decision task, and seek evidence before claiming progress. This guide turns that discipline into meeting preparation, opportunity review, and negotiation practice.

## Establish what the source actually supports

The supplied material describes East Money coverage of semiconductor wafers in the context of AI demand and renewed attention to the industry's foundational role. It reports that Shanghai Silicon Industry continues to advance silicon-wafer capacity construction.

The event information identifies the company and public source. It does not supply a counterparty, investment amount, or event date. Those fields must remain unknown. The material also does not establish customer orders, internal project decisions, realized savings, or financial returns.

Keep three categories separate in account notes:

- **Reported facts:** statements directly supported by the supplied material.
- **Sales hypotheses:** possible business implications that require customer confirmation.
- **Unknowns:** missing information with a named verification question.

An industry interpretation can frame a conversation. It cannot establish an individual customer's priorities or prove that an investment has produced results.

## Translate capacity into an investment discussion

Value capability means expressing an offering through the customer's profit, investment, and operating concerns. For a capacity expansion, start with variables the customer can examine:

- Which customer requirements would additional capacity serve?
- How could the production ramp affect unit costs?
- Could more stable quality reduce rework and scrap?
- Which orders would support cash recovery?

These are questions, not findings about the company in the report. Their purpose is to reveal the assumptions behind an investment decision.

### Build a value hypothesis sheet

Use three columns: customer objective, operating activity affected by the proposed solution, and evidence awaiting confirmation. A possible entry might connect a quality objective to production stability, with customer data needed to assess any effect on rework or scrap. Leave the evidence cell blank when the information is unavailable.

Do not fill gaps with invented percentages or projected returns. A blank identifies the next question. The practical test is whether the customer can explain the investment's merits using its own operating figures and assumptions. A persuasive presentation alone does not satisfy that test.

## Map relationships through decision information

Relationship capability combines professional competence, decision information, and credible action. Its focus is understanding and covering the decision chain.

Use PRTABCD to organize seven information areas:

- **P — Past:** relevant history and previous experience.
- **R — Requirements:** the customer's needs.
- **T — Timing:** deadlines and key milestones.
- **A — Arm, or embracer:** who inside the customer organization actively supports working with you.
- **B — Budget:** what is known about available funding.
- **C — Competition:** competing suppliers and alternative approaches.
- **D — Decision chain:** the people and departments involved in the decision.

For every area, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. Keep inference visibly separate from confirmation.

A and D require particular care. A supportive contact may welcome discussion without representing the wider decision chain. Record that support accurately, then investigate who else participates and who is responsible for the intended result. Update the map after each meeting with new evidence and unresolved gaps.

## Ask questions that let customers explain value

Questioning capability uses purposeful questions to uncover and develop real needs. SPIN provides four categories: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff.

For an expansion discussion, the sequence could be:

1. **Situation:** How does current capacity utilization align with customer qualification timing?
2. **Problem:** Which quality fluctuations are most concerning during the production ramp?
3. **Implication:** How would those fluctuations affect delivery, cost, and customer trust?
4. **Need-payoff:** If improvement could be demonstrated with verifiable evidence, which next step would become worthwhile?

Use the customer's answer to choose the next question. The sequence is a preparation aid, not a script that overrides what the customer says.

Introduce public coverage as background: the report mentions continued wafer capacity construction; what does that mean for the customer's current project schedule? Then give the customer room to respond. Do not present the report's industry interpretation as a conclusion the customer must accept.

## Judge progress from both sides of the purchase

Process capability uses two views of the opportunity. The sales view has five stages: contact, needs, proposal, negotiation, and close.

The purchasing view contains seven tasks: recognizing a problem, judging value, confirming that a solution exists, establishing responsibility, deciding whether to build internally or buy externally, selecting a solution and supplier, and making a commitment and signing.

These views have a many-to-many relationship. A proposal does not prove the customer has completed its value assessment. Repeated meetings do not establish that responsibility has been assigned.

| Sales activity | Customer evidence to seek |
| --- | --- |
| Needs discussion | Willingness to provide relevant operating data |
| Proposal development | Requests for explicit validation criteria |
| Opportunity review | Participation from relevant departments |

These signals help assess the customer's current task; none should be treated as an automatic stage transition.

Before choosing the next sales action, ask which purchasing task the customer is actually addressing. If value remains unresolved, seek the information needed to evaluate it. If responsibility remains unclear, clarify the decision chain. Let customer behavior determine whether the opportunity has advanced.

## Prepare alternatives before negotiating terms

Negotiation capability considers power, time, information, and alternatives when forming a sustainable agreement. Preparation begins with BATNA: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, meaning the best available action if agreement cannot be reached.

Write down:

- An alternative that can actually be executed.
- Limits beyond which agreement is unacceptable.
- Who has authority to approve commitments.
- The interests involved and the record supporting the discussion.

For a capital-intensive project, possible negotiating issues include delivery timing, payment terms, risk allocation, and subsequent service. These are preparation topics, not confirmed terms of the reported expansion.

Build an exchange list showing what the customer requests, what you could concede, and what verifiable commitment you would receive. Greater specificity makes the exchange easier to assess and helps avoid repeated one-way concessions. A further price reduction should not become the only option considered.

## Checklist for the next customer meeting

Prepare three working documents: a value hypothesis sheet, a PRTABCD map, and a short question list. Use this checklist to make them actionable:

- [ ] Separate reported facts, hypotheses, and unknowns.
- [ ] Connect each proposed benefit to an operating activity and evidence needed.
- [ ] Leave unavailable figures blank.
- [ ] Mark all seven PRTABCD areas with sources and next questions.
- [ ] Distinguish an internal embracer from the complete decision chain.
- [ ] Prepare three initial questions using the four SPIN categories as a coverage check.
- [ ] Identify the customer's current purchasing task provisionally.
- [ ] Define the customer behavior that would support a claim of progress.
- [ ] If negotiation is approaching, document BATNA and possible condition exchanges.

After the meeting, update the documents with what the customer actually said or did. Record the next verification step rather than converting enthusiasm into an assumed commitment.

## FAQ

### Why begin with value?

Capital-intensive expansion raises questions about investment, returns, cash flow, and risk. Value capability helps translate a proposed solution into terms the customer can examine. Relationship, questioning, process, and negotiation capabilities then provide additional checks.

### Can preparation proceed without an amount or date?

Yes. The source supports preparing hypotheses, mapping missing information, and designing questions. It does not support estimating the missing event fields or claiming knowledge of the company's internal plans.

### Does an enthusiastic contact indicate progress?

It indicates support from that contact. Wider progress requires evidence relevant to the customer's decision task, such as sharing data, arranging cross-department discussions, or requesting validation criteria. Interpret each behavior in context.

### How should potential benefits be described?

Use conditional language and identify the evidence required. A possible reduction in rework remains a hypothesis until the customer confirms the relevant assumptions and information. Do not rewrite an intended benefit as an achieved outcome.
