A framework agreement creates a useful starting point for a complex sale. It does not, by itself, establish that the buyer has approved funding, assigned responsibility, selected a supplier, or committed to a final contract. Those decisions need their own evidence.

For sales teams managing large accounts, the practical task is to connect seller activity with buyer progress. A productive meeting matters when it helps the customer complete a decision. A proposal matters when the customer has reached the point where evaluating a solution is the next necessary step.

This guide explains how to map both sides of that process, recognize substantive progress, and choose actions that address the customer's current obstacle.

## What a framework agreement actually tells you

The supplied source describes an investment cooperation framework agreement between Lenovo Group and the Tianjin Port Free Trade Zone for a next-generation intelligent computing research, development, and manufacturing center. According to Lenovo, as reported in that source, the project was expected to be completed and enter production by the end of 2027.

That is a stated expectation, not evidence that construction, investment approvals, or delivery milestones have been completed. The source does not establish the project's current status.

The case illustrates a useful sales question: what decisions remain between an announced agreement and implementation? A framework may indicate initial agreement on needs or the direction of a solution. It cannot establish, without further evidence, how far the customer has progressed through its internal purchasing decisions.

Treat the announced timetable as a planning constraint. Work backward to identify the approvals, responsibilities, and technical decisions needed to support it.

## Map seller activity against buyer decisions

The source uses two complementary sequences. The seller's five stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. The buyer's seven stages are identifying the problem, assessing value, confirming that a solution exists, assigning responsibility, deciding whether to build internally or buy externally, selecting the solution and supplier, and commitment and signing.

These sequences provide two views of the same opportunity. They do not imply a fixed one-to-one correspondence.

| Review point | Seller's perspective | Buyer's perspective | Practical implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initial agreement | Needs may be understood | Value and feasibility may be accepted | Check what remains unresolved |
| Solution development | The team is refining its proposal | Ownership or build-versus-buy may remain open | Resolve the buyer's decision obstacle |
| Supplier evaluation | The team seeks selection | The customer compares solutions and suppliers | Look for formal evaluation activity |
| Commitment | The team expects to close | The customer must commit and sign | Require evidence beyond positive discussion |

A common mismatch occurs when the seller presents detailed solutions while the buyer is still deciding which department owns the project. More proposal detail may leave that obstacle untouched.

### Identify the next buyer decision

At each review, write down the customer's current decision, the evidence supporting that assessment, and the next decision it must complete. Then select a sales action that helps it move forward.

If ownership remains unresolved, the immediate task is to clarify accountability. If supplier selection has begun, the task shifts toward evaluation requirements and solution differentiation.

## Use customer behavior as evidence

Visit counts, cordial conversations, and CRM percentages describe activity or internal expectations. They do not establish that the customer has advanced its purchasing process.

The source identifies four useful categories of evidence for large infrastructure or industrial investment projects:

- **Budget documentation:** Has the customer created a specific capital expenditure budget application? Distinguish an application from an approved allocation; record what the document actually demonstrates.
- **Written technical requirements:** Have the parties documented technical specifications and delivery standards? Check whether those requirements are agreed and stable enough to guide evaluation.
- **Senior decision-maker support:** Has a relevant executive or senior public-sector decision-maker formally supported the project? Record the scope of that support without treating endorsement as a substitute for approval.
- **Selection-related behavior:** Has the customer invited the team into an internal review or reduced substantive engagement with competing suppliers? Such actions may indicate deeper evaluation, but should be interpreted alongside other evidence.

These signals answer different questions. A specification does not prove funding, and executive support does not prove that procurement responsibilities are settled.

Maintain a short evidence record for each unresolved decision: what the customer did, which decision it supports, and what remains unconfirmed. This makes opportunity reviews more precise without requiring an invented probability of closing.

## Build a practical path from agreement to implementation

### Clarify the decision chain and timetable

The source recommends PRTABCD as an account intelligence tool covering past history, requirements, time, champions, budget, competitors, and the decision chain. In complex projects, it places particular emphasis on time and decision authority.

Use that scan to establish:

- Who can approve the budget.
- Who owns the implementation target.
- Which departments must participate in the decision.
- Which approvals must precede the next milestone.
- Where the team still lacks reliable information.

For a project with a stated production date, identifying the responsible owner is as important as repeating the deadline. A timetable becomes actionable when the decisions needed to support it have clear responsibility.

### Help the customer resolve its current obstacle

When responsibility is unclear, the source suggests using an ROI model or an explanation of policy compliance to help the prospective lead department address its concerns. These materials should support a specific internal decision.

Keep assumptions visible. An ROI model is a decision aid, not proof of an outcome. Likewise, supplying a compliance explanation does not establish that the relevant approval has been granted.

Once ownership is settled, help the customer examine whether to build internally or purchase externally. Avoid assuming that willingness to discuss a solution means this choice is already complete.

### Define milestones with observable completion criteria

Break a long implementation path into smaller, verifiable steps. The source gives deployment and acceptance of initial pilot equipment as an example of a possible milestone. It does not report that such a pilot occurred in the Lenovo project.

For an appropriate milestone, agree on its scope, responsible parties, completion evidence, and the decision it enables next. Before negotiation becomes the main focus, establish a shared definition of success and delivery acceptance. This gives both parties a concrete reference when discussing scope and commitments.

### Reassess alternatives as the opportunity develops

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best available course of action if agreement cannot be reached.

The source emphasizes that alternatives can change during a long sales process. Revisit the customer's options and your own position as requirements, competitors, and purchasing choices become clearer. Explain the distinctive value of your solution in relation to the customer's agreed needs. An early expression of preference should not be treated as permanent supplier selection.

## An actionable opportunity-review checklist

Use this checklist before advancing the opportunity internally or committing to the next major sales action:

- [ ] Record the seller's current stage.
- [ ] Record the buyer's current decision stage separately.
- [ ] Attach customer behavior evidence supporting each assessment.
- [ ] Separate confirmed facts from interpretations and open questions.
- [ ] Identify the budget authority and implementation owner.
- [ ] Check whether ownership and build-versus-buy decisions remain unresolved.
- [ ] Confirm the status of technical requirements and acceptance standards.
- [ ] Define the next customer milestone and its completion evidence.
- [ ] Choose an action that addresses the current decision obstacle.
- [ ] Reassess alternatives before relying on an earlier negotiating position.

If several answers remain unknown, use the next conversation to close those information gaps. Repeating a general follow-up may leave the underlying decision unchanged.

## FAQ

### Does a signed framework agreement mean the sale is nearly closed?

Not necessarily. It may establish initial intent while budget, ownership, technical requirements, or supplier selection remain open. Judge its significance by the decisions and commitments it actually records.

### How can a team recognize apparent progress that has stalled?

Look for smooth communication without corresponding movement in the customer's internal process. Recheck the buyer's current stage, identify the unresolved decision, and use targeted questions to understand the obstacle.

### Must every opportunity produce identical evidence?

The source offers evidence categories for complex projects, rather than a universal document checklist. Use them to identify what demonstrates progress in the opportunity being reviewed. Avoid treating one signal as proof that every purchasing decision is complete.

### When should negotiation become the main focus?

The source recommends first aligning on the solution, success standards, and purchasing progress. If responsibility or requirements remain unresolved, commercial negotiation alone may not address what is preventing commitment.

### What should a manager ask during a pipeline review?

Ask what the customer has done, which decision that behavior demonstrates, and what must happen next. Compare those answers with the seller's recorded stage. Where they differ, adjust the next action to the buyer's actual position.
