A public signal that Volkswagen may pause follow-on investment in its India electric-vehicle project can tempt a sales team to reach for a verdict. The better question is: which commercial conditions are still open, and what evidence would close them? The source offers two signals only: follow-on investment may pause, and a clearer commercial proposal is awaited. It does not confirm termination, price pressure, urgency, amount, or event date. Treat the pause as a condition-closure problem, not proof that the deal is dead.

## Set the Fact Boundary First

Start by separating three columns: known facts, bound fields, and unknowns. Known facts come from the public signal. Bound fields include the counterparty, Volkswagen, and the wait for a clearer commercial proposal. Unknowns include the amount, date, negotiation history, decision timing, and the customer's internal alternatives. For each unknown, write a next question rather than a conclusion. A pause may reflect missing value evidence, an unresolved approval path, a budget question, a competing alternative, or sequencing; the public information does not say which. That is why the first task is to protect the fact boundary. If the team writes assumptions into the fact column, later questions chase a false premise.

## Why Negotiation Power Is the Entry Point

PSS5 treats key-account sales through five capabilities: value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation. The pause points most directly to negotiation power because conditions are not closed. But negotiation power should not stand alone. Value explains the return, relationship maps the decision chain, questioning fills information gaps, and system shows where the customer is in its buying process. The five capabilities check one another around the same opportunity; they are not separate stories used to make the pipeline look healthier.

## Prepare Five Negotiation Conditions

Negotiation power begins before anyone sits at the table. Five conditions must be visible: power, time, information, bottom line, and alternatives. Put them on one page so a pause becomes a set of preparation tasks, not an emotional judgment.

| Condition | What to establish | Preparation task |
|---|---|---|
| Power | Resources, authority, and constraints each side can exchange | List exchangeable assets and limits; do not treat size or reputation as the only lever |
| Time | Which milestones affect each side and who feels urgency | Mark decision, project, and communication milestones; separate fact from assumption |
| Information | Confirmed conditions, open items, and hypotheses | Keep confirmed, unconfirmed, and to-verify records separate |
| Bottom line | Minimum acceptable terms and non-negotiables | Clarify what can move and what cannot, marking inferred items |
| Alternatives | Best fallback if no agreement is reached | Identify executable options, authority, and cost; do not invent the customer's choices |

The common mistake is converting an unknown into a known. Waiting for a clearer commercial proposal cannot become the customer is rejecting us, delaying on purpose, or demanding a lower price. Each judgment needs a source and a next question. Unsupported judgments belong in a hypothesis column, not the fact column.

## Use PRTABCD to Find Information Gaps

PRTABCD scans seven decision-information areas: P for past and history, R for need, T for time and key milestones, A for the Arm or embracer, B for budget, C for competitors and alternatives, and D for the decision chain. A answers who inside the customer organization is embracing the project; D describes a multi-person, multi-department chain. PRTABCD is not a personality profile; it is an evidence table of knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions.

For this pause, split clearer commercial proposal into R, B, C, and D. Which operating goals does the need serve? Are budget and approval conditions clear? What alternatives exist? Who owns the result in the decision chain? P, T, and A should not be blank either: prior project experience, key dates, and internal supporters matter. Write all seven areas before the next conversation.

## Translate the Proposal Into Business Language

Value power translates the proposal into inputs, returns, risks, and operational improvement in language the customer can verify. Product features alone rarely move a paused investment. Build a value check list: which business process should improve, who verifies it, who approves the input, who carries the risk, and what opportunity cost remains if the project waits. Every number must come from customer confirmation or public evidence. The source gives no amount, so the amount stays blank; do not invent a payback period, margin, or market size. Value power gives negotiation power something to exchange beyond price.

## Cover the Decision Chain and Ask Better Questions

Relationship power means understanding and covering the customer's decision chain, not entertainment. For this pause, identify who embraces the project, who controls budget, who is accountable for the result, and who may propose an alternative. For each key person, MBVS can generate hypotheses about motivation, benchmark, vision, and solution. Treat all four as hypotheses to validate, not as customer statements.

Questioning power can follow SPIN: ask about background and current state, ask about difficulties where conditions remain unclear, ask about the operational impact of a pause, and ask what outcomes the customer would fund. Keep questions anchored to bound facts. Do not build a question on an undisclosed amount, unknown date, or assumed attitude. After each round, write answers back into PRTABCD and the evidence table.

## Judge Progress With a Dual Funnel

System power uses a dual-view funnel: five sales stages and seven customer buying stages. Sales stages are contact, need, solution, negotiation, and closing. Customer stages run from problem discovery, value judgment, confirmation that a solution exists, responsibility ownership, build-or-buy, solution and supplier selection, to commitment and signing. The two sides are many-to-many, not one-to-one. Judge position only by customer behavior evidence, such as continued discussion of commercial conditions, a new communication round, named approval responsibility, or an agreed verification method. Visits, verbal warmth, and seller optimism are not progress. If the customer is waiting for a clearer proposal, the project may sit near value judgment or solution confirmation; the next action is to strengthen value proof and fill information gaps, not rush the signature.

## Design Alternatives and Concessions

BATNA is the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Prepare an executable alternative, bottom line, authority, interests, and records. A seller cannot invent the customer's alternatives, but can ask questions that help clarify optional actions, time costs, and approval boundaries. Concessions follow an exchange principle: every adjustment in price, scope, time, payment, or risk must return a verifiable condition. Write can-give and cannot-give items first, then the confirmation action needed from the other side. Unconditional concessions drain the relationship; conditional concessions can support a durable agreement.

## Action Checklist

### Five-Step Next-Action Sequence

- Separate public facts, bound fields, and unknowns into three columns.
- Complete PRTABCD with knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions.
- Build a value check list; leave unverified numbers blank.
- Use the dual funnel to name the customer's current decision task.
- Design exchangeable conditions and BATNA questions before the next conversation.
- Keep assumptions out of the fact column and update the evidence table after each interaction.

## FAQ

Q1: Why use negotiation power instead of declaring the deal dead or alive?
A1: The public information gives only two signals: a possible pause in follow-on investment and a wait for a clearer commercial proposal. Negotiation power requires power, time, information, bottom line, and alternatives to be clarified before a pause is treated as an ending. The team can organize confirmed conditions, open conditions, and exchangeable resources before choosing whether to add value proof, adjust timing, or prepare alternatives.

Q2: What five preparations does the negotiation-power lens require?
A2: Power is what each side can exchange. Time is who is more affected by milestones. Information is what is confirmed and what is blank. Bottom line is the minimum acceptable terms and non-negotiables. Alternatives are what each side can do if no agreement is reached. Put all five on one table so the pause becomes verifiable next actions.

Q3: How is PRTABCD used here?
A3: Use it as an evidence table. P covers past and history, R the need, T time and milestones, A the embracer, B budget, C competitors and alternatives, and D the decision chain. Split the clearer commercial proposal into need, budget, alternatives, and decision-chain questions. Write knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions for all seven areas.

Q4: Why not read the wait for a clearer proposal as price pressure?
A4: That phrase says only that commercial conditions are not closed. It does not prove price pressure, delay, or termination. Treating an unknown as known makes later questions follow a false premise. Instead, list price, scope, budget, approval, and alternatives as separate items to confirm, then update judgment with customer-confirmed facts.
