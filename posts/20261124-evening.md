A successful production trial gives a sales team something valuable: evidence that a proposed solution can operate in the customer's environment. It does not establish that the customer has approved a purchase, assigned accountability, or resolved competing ways to solve the problem.

For complex key-account opportunities, the practical challenge is to keep the seller's activities aligned with the buyer's decisions. A dual-perspective funnel makes that alignment explicit. It records where the sales team believes the opportunity stands, identifies the decision the customer is actually working through, and requires evidence before either position advances.

The robot deployment described in the supplied source offers a useful case for applying this discipline. Its lesson extends beyond robotics: technical progress and commercial readiness need separate examination.

## What a production trial establishes

The supplied account reports that an initial batch of G2 robots from Zhiyuan Innovation was integrated into a live chip-testing production line at JCET's Lingang automotive electronics facility. The robots completed trial operation, primarily handling tray loading and unloading.

The account interprets this as progress beyond an early proof of concept toward adaptation in a real production environment. That is a meaningful technical milestone. It gives the customer and supplier a basis for discussing operating requirements, trial results, and the scope of a possible purchase.

However, the reported milestone does not establish a volume order. The source does not provide a completed procurement decision or evidence that every internal approval has been secured. Its discussion of the customer's purchasing position is therefore a diagnosis to investigate, rather than a confirmed account of internal deliberations.

Sales teams should preserve this distinction in both account plans and forecasts. Record the trial as technical evidence, then identify the commercial decisions that remain unresolved.

## Map the seller and buyer separately

The dual-perspective funnel combines five seller stages with seven customer purchasing stages. The seller stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. The supplied source discusses selected buyer tasks, including recognizing a problem, assessing value, deciding whether to build internally or buy externally, selecting a solution and supplier, and confirming accountability. It does not enumerate the complete seven-stage sequence.

In the robot case, the source places the seller around the later solution stage or the beginning of negotiation. A solution has operated in the customer's environment, so discussions may begin to address price, scope, and payment terms.

The buyer may still be comparing internal development with external procurement, evaluating suppliers, or resolving budget questions. Those possibilities require confirmation. A particularly important question is whether an internal owner has accepted responsibility for the objective, resources, and results.

| Observed signal | What it supports | What still needs confirmation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trial operation on a live line | Progress in production-environment adaptation | Purchasing readiness and approved scope |
| Formal supplier onboarding | Evidence of procurement activity | Remaining evaluation and approval tasks |
| Cross-functional technical review | Active organizational evaluation | Decision criteria and unresolved objections |
| Customer inquiry or tender invitation | A basis for advancing commercial discussions | Budget, accountability, and agreement terms |

These signals help locate the opportunity. None should be stretched into proof of a decision it does not document.

## Choose the next action from the buyer's task

Alignment means that each sales action helps the customer complete its current decision. It does not mean that the seller and buyer must use identical stage names.

When the customer is still assessing value, detailed commercial bargaining can arrive too early. The source warns that premature negotiation may expose pricing flexibility before the customer has settled why it should invest. Conversely, repeated technical demonstrations can delay progress when the buyer is ready to finalize a commitment.

### Turn the next unresolved decision into a working session

Start by asking what the customer needs to establish about tray handling. Which efficiency measures matter internally? What trial evidence will support the customer's value assessment? Who needs to accept that assessment? The source recommends uncovering specific, measurable objectives; it supplies no numerical targets, so the account team must obtain them from the customer.

Next, help the customer examine internal development and external purchasing on a comparable basis. The source calls for a clear financial comparison that considers time costs and operating or maintenance risks. Use customer-supported assumptions and distinguish measured inputs from estimates. The trial alone cannot demonstrate that purchasing externally is financially preferable.

Then confirm accountability. Identify who owns the intended result, who can commit resources, and whether that responsibility is acknowledged within the customer organization. An interested technical contact does not, by itself, answer these questions.

Once the relevant decisions are sufficiently supported, move commercial discussions forward. Clarify the proposed pilot scope, price, payment terms, and any intention to expand. Treat an expansion intention as an intention until the customer takes the corresponding purchasing steps.

This sequence gives the sales team a practical way to advance the opportunity while respecting the customer's actual work.

## Build an evidence chain for stage progression

Pipeline reviews become unreliable when activity substitutes for customer progress. Visits, demonstrations, and encouraging conversations may be useful, but they do not establish that the buyer has completed a decision.

The source proposes a stronger evidence chain for the robot opportunity: a formal trial report, an acceptance document signed by the customer's project owner, and internal meeting records concerning subsequent purchasing batches. These are recommended evidence items, not documents confirmed to exist in the reported case.

For each item, ask what it proves and what it leaves open. A trial report can document operating results. Acceptance can establish that the responsible customer representative has acknowledged an agreed milestone. Meeting records can reveal whether follow-on purchasing has entered an internal decision process. None should automatically be recorded as a signed purchase.

Stage exit criteria turn this reasoning into a management practice. The source gives two concrete examples:

- Before moving from needs to solution, obtain written customer confirmation of the impact of the problem.
- Before moving from solution to negotiation, look for a customer-issued inquiry or tender invitation.

Apply criteria consistently and retain the supporting records. If evidence is missing, revisit the stage assignment or flag the opportunity as at risk. The purpose is to direct attention toward the decision that still needs work.

## An actionable account-review checklist

Use this checklist before advancing an opportunity or presenting its status in a pipeline review:

- [ ] State the seller stage and cite the evidence supporting it.
- [ ] Identify the buyer's current decision task separately.
- [ ] Distinguish confirmed customer actions from working hypotheses.
- [ ] Obtain the customer's measures for assessing trial value.
- [ ] Document unresolved internal-build versus external-buy questions.
- [ ] Confirm ownership of objectives, resources, and results.
- [ ] Check for formal procurement signals, such as onboarding, evaluation, or an inquiry.
- [ ] Link each proposed stage change to its required evidence.
- [ ] Assign a next action that helps complete the buyer's current decision.
- [ ] Reclassify or flag opportunities whose recorded stage lacks support.

The checklist is most useful when it produces a specific next step: obtain written confirmation, resolve a cost assumption, identify an accountable owner, or clarify the procurement process.

## FAQ

### Does a successful trial justify moving directly to negotiation?

It can support a discussion about commercial readiness, but the stage change needs customer evidence. Check whether the buyer has resolved value, accountability, and purchasing-process questions. The source identifies a customer inquiry or tender invitation as an example of evidence for entering negotiation.

### How can a team recognize genuine supplier selection?

Look for customer actions such as a formal request for proposals, a cross-functional evaluation group, supplier onboarding, or requests for detailed technical comparisons. These are stronger indicators than verbal enthusiasm. Confirm what each action means within that customer's process.

### What should happen when the seller is ahead of the buyer?

Redirect the next action toward the unfinished buyer task. If the customer is comparing internal development with purchasing, help establish the comparison. Repeating a proposal or pressing for commercial concessions does not resolve that decision.

### How should unsupported pipeline progress be handled?

Review the opportunity against its stage exit criteria. Where the required customer evidence is absent, return it to a supported stage or mark it as at risk. Keep the missing evidence visible so the team knows what to investigate.

### Where does PRTABCD fit?

The source describes PRTABCD primarily as a way to scan decision information within relationship management. It can also expose gaps relevant to stage assessment. Unclear budget or timing, for example, leaves the team without information needed to judge negotiation readiness.
