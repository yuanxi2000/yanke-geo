A public strategy announcement can give a key-account salesperson a useful starting point. It does not establish a buying requirement. The work begins by separating what the announcement actually says from what still needs checking.

The supplied source examines a CEO's public commitment to remain invested in the Chinese and Thai markets and advance investment in both directions. That statement contains no procurement requirement, budget, implementation timetable, or decision-maker list. Its practical value lies in directing further investigation.

This guide explains how to turn that kind of signal into a disciplined account conversation: record the gaps, identify the people involved, investigate the business problem, build an investment case, and judge progress through customer actions.

## Start with an intelligence record

Before drafting a proposal, organize the available information using PRTABCD. For every field, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question.

| Field | What the statement establishes | What still needs checking |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P: Past | No verified operating history | Previous investments, facilities, and channel changes |
| R: Requirements | A broad strategic direction | The specific business problem |
| T: Time | No implementation dates | Milestones and their reasons |
| A: Arm | No identified internal champion | Who will support a concrete initiative |
| B: Budget | No disclosed funding | Amount, allocation, and approval process |
| C: Competition | No supplier information | Suppliers serving the relevant business activity |
| D: Decision | No confirmed decision chain | Participants, responsibilities, and approvals |

The gaps are useful. They show where a salesperson would otherwise be guessing. A reference to investment does not reveal whether the underlying concern involves capacity, supply chains, distribution, or brand development. Keep those possibilities as questions.

Check historical claims against public information rather than memory. Ask the customer to confirm requirements, timing, and funding. Only verified information should support proposal claims.

## Find a champion and map the decision chain

Relationship building here means earning trust through professional insight, useful decision information, and actions the customer can verify. Senior attention to a strategic topic does not establish that anyone is advancing your particular project.

In PRTABCD, A stands for Arm: the internal champion willing to support your work. D stands for Decision: the chain of people and departments participating in a decision. An enthusiastic contact and an organizational decision are different kinds of evidence.

Begin with a short verification email asking who leads the initiative internally. Then map relevant departments and record whom you know, who knows you, and who could introduce you to another participant.

For each important contact, use MBVS to write four provisional hypotheses:

- Motivation: What might make this issue matter to them?
- Benchmark: What standard might they compare performance against?
- Vision: What future condition might they want?
- Solution: What approach might they consider useful?

Job titles cannot validate these hypotheses. Ask questions, revise your notes, and record observable changes: sharing information, introducing colleagues, or spending time on a defined problem.

## Turn strategic language into business questions

A commitment to two markets remains too broad for a useful solution discussion. SPIN provides four question types for investigating what the statement might mean operationally.

### Follow the customer's reasoning

Move through situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions in sequence. Adapt the wording to the conversation:

1. Situation: How are responsibilities and processes divided across the two markets today?
2. Problem: When the markets move at different speeds, where does coordination become difficult?
3. Implication: What does that difficulty mean for delivery timing or customer experience?
4. Need-payoff: If that step worked more smoothly, what coordination costs might fall, or what additional orders might become possible?

These questions explore possibilities; they do not establish that delays, unnecessary costs, or missed orders exist. Let the customer describe the issue and its consequences.

Do not move straight to returns before the customer recognizes a problem. That makes the discussion sound like a pitch built around an assumed need. Ask a small number of questions, capture the answers accurately, and agree on the next verification step.

## Build an investment case the customer can explain

Once a business problem is verified, translate proposed capabilities into operating and financial value. A feature list alone gives an operating manager little basis for requesting funds.

The source calls this a PIP, or profit improvement proposal. It should answer three questions:

- How much profit could the change add or protect?
- Through what mechanism, and with what basis for confidence?
- Over what customer-confirmed period could the change occur?

Start by listing the operating activities the proposal would affect. Identify whether each connects to revenue or cost. For every proposed improvement, record the supporting customer data and the basis for the estimate. Translate the result into revenue, cost, profit, or payback terms familiar to the customer's finance team.

The source distinguishes Box 1, which allocates and approves funding, from Box 2, the operating managers who use it. The salesperson's task is to help Box 2 present an operating-value case that Box 1 can assess.

Ask the business owner to review the draft before using it in a funding discussion. Leave unsupported figures and unconfirmed timing open for verification.

## Judge progress from both sides of the purchase

A seller's process and a customer's purchasing process describe different activities. Keep both visible when assessing an opportunity.

The five seller stages are contact, needs, proposal, negotiation, and close. The seven customer stages are:

1. Discovering a problem.
2. Assessing its value.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Confirming responsibility.
5. Choosing between building internally and buying externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Making a commitment and signing.

These sequences have a many-to-many relationship. Sending a proposal does not establish that the customer has assigned responsibility or decided to buy externally.

Look for customer behavior that supports the current position: acknowledging a gap, assigning an owner, comparing internal development with external purchase, or providing evaluation criteria.

In the source example, the CEO's statement establishes strategic attention. It does not establish a confirmed solution, an accountable project owner, or readiness to contract. Record the supporting behavior beside any stage assessment. Meeting counts and seller confidence cannot substitute for that evidence.

## Prepare exchanges before negotiating price

Negotiation preparation covers power, time, information, and alternatives. Start by writing down your executable alternative and your limits. Ask about the customer's deadlines and why those dates matter; a public investment commitment does not establish urgency by itself.

Prepare three items beyond price that could be exchanged: scope, payment terms, and service response. Broader scope, later payment, or earlier delivery commitments should each have a corresponding condition. Depending on the discussion, that might involve a longer cooperation period, clearer acceptance criteria, or an internal introduction.

Write each concession as a conditional exchange and document the agreement. The purpose is to make the terms explicit rather than treating a discount as sufficient reason to sign.

## FAQ

### Why follow an announcement without procurement details?

It provides direction for investigation. A strategic commitment can help identify relevant departments and questions before a formal project emerges. It remains a lead to investigate, with purchasing intent unconfirmed.

### Does a supportive senior contact count as a champion?

Support needs behavioral evidence. Look for someone willing to invest time in a specific issue and speak for the proposed approach. Even then, map the wider decision chain separately.

### What should happen when the customer cannot provide financial data?

Keep the financial inputs unresolved. Identify the operating activities involved and ask who can validate the necessary figures. Do not replace missing customer data with invented returns.

### Can negotiation planning begin before a proposal is ready?

You can clarify your alternatives, limits, and possible exchange items. Customer deadlines, coordination costs, and other potential sources of negotiating information still require verification.

### What is the most useful first action?

Record the statement, identify the information gaps, and arrange a verification conversation. Afterward, note what the customer actually did and use that evidence to choose the next step.
