A public roadmap can move a deal without anyone signing. When a supplier announces a next-generation part with a rough launch window, buyers recalculate budget, refresh cycles, and alternatives. The useful question for key-account sellers is not how to promote the announcement but where the account sits in its purchase decision and what changed.

The scenario below is grounded in a real announcement pattern: a memory manufacturer signaling that a new-generation DDR5 RDIMM is expected before year-end. The specifics of that roadmap belong to the vendor; the sales implications belong to whoever is carrying the account.

## The Buyer's Arithmetic Changes First

When a buyer hears "new product expected by year-end," three thoughts tend to surface:

- Will buying now leave us behind quickly?
- Can we wait until year-end without missing our own business window?
- What are we actually buying — bandwidth, capacity, or a lower cost per rack?

These are value judgments, not specs. The customer's operating numbers answer them, not datasheets. Architecture names and module standards are seller language; the operating layer asks how many units fit per rack and which budget line pays.

The seller first asks those questions clearly and listens. Technology depth becomes a topic only when tied to the buyer's economics. Confirm structure, workload, and budgets in conversation; unverified benefits get overturned.

## Turning "Expected by Year-End" into Verifiable Information

"Expected by year-end" is vague, hitting T (time and milestones) and C (competitors and alternatives). The buyer's procurement rhythm, internal milestones, and alternatives must be recorded: known, unknown, source, next question.

- **P (past/historical information):** How did this customer buy similar products before, and what legacy issues remain? How much of that do we actually know?
- **R (requirement):** Is the current problem capacity, bandwidth, or total cost? Do we have the customer's original wording, not our paraphrase?
- **T (time and key milestones):** What does year-end mean to them — a budget node, a go-live node, or a supplier evaluation node?
- **A (Arm, the embracer):** Who inside the account is pulling for us, and what are they willing to spend to move this forward?
- **B (budget):** Which cost center pays, and do this year and next year sit in different budget cycles?
- **C (competitors and alternatives):** Do their alternatives include the incumbent, an internal option, or simply waiting?
- **D (decision chain):** This is a multi-person, multi-department chain. Who on it is still uncovered?

Writing T and D down as explicit open items is often the difference between a forecast and a wish.

### The two-funnel view: locate the buyer before you pitch

The customer runs seven stages: problem, value, solution exists, ownership, build-versus-buy, selection, commitment. The seller runs five: contact, requirement, solution, negotiation, close. These map many-to-many, not one-to-one. Position is inferred from behavioral evidence.

If the buyer is still confirming a solution exists and the seller discusses price, that is skipping ahead. If the buyer is in selection and the seller presents technical depth, that is lagging. A roadmap can push accounts back into fresh comparison. Put both timelines on one page and let the customer do the math.

## Waiting Is an Alternative

When the supplier names a date, the buyer gains leverage: *I can wait.* Waiting fits BATNA. Avoid the buyer saying "revisit at year-end" while the seller scrambles. Prepare the alternative, walk-away line, authority, interests, and record in advance. If the deal slips, what does the customer lose?

Negotiation centers on power, time, information, and alternatives. The vendor's year-end milestone is the buyer's reason to wait and the seller's countdown. Whoever computes the gap between schedules more clearly holds the initiative.

A compact comparison can help clarify what each side is actually weighing:

| Dimension | What the buyer sees in the announcement | What the seller must do |
|---|---|---|
| Time | A reason to delay commitment | Build a reverse timeline from the milestone |
| Value | Uncertainty about being outdated | Translate specs into operating numbers |
| Alternatives | Waiting becomes a real option | Define BATNA, limits, authority, record |
| Decision chain | Internal re-evaluation | Map and cover uncovered stakeholders |
| Information | Public roadmap, private concerns | Replace guesses with verified T and D |

## Write Technical Depth as a Profit Plan

A Profit Improvement Proposal (PIP) frames the solution as an investment case: profit added or protected, mechanism, confidence, and time frame. Unlike a quotation, the buyer can carry it into an internal budget fight.

Help the operating manager build the numbers, then let that manager persuade capital allocation and approval. Technical accumulation enters the checklist only when translated into numbers the operating layer can verify.

## Relationship and Questioning: An Announcement Is Not Progress

A technology announcement does not mean the relationship advanced. Relationship capability is built through professional ability, decision information, and credible action — covering the decision chain, not entertainment. Progress shows in behavioral evidence: someone claimed evaluation, an alignment was scheduled, a next step was committed.

Questioning capability, organized around the four SPIN categories — situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff — can follow this order for this event:

- What business does the current storage support?
- Is the current constraint capacity, power, or cost?
- If the constraint persists, which business line is affected?
- If a better approach existed, when would they want to start evaluating?

After those four, both the buyer's position and the next action tend to surface.

The boundary to hold: motivation, benchmark, vision, and solution ideas about a key person are hypotheses to be verified, not facts. Job titles or personal inferences should never be written into a proposal as customer fact.

## Actionable Checklist

1. Pull evaluating accounts and mark purchase stages.
2. Fill missing T and D, write the next question into the visit plan.
3. Replace parameter-heavy materials with a PIP draft opening with verifiable numbers.
4. For "we'll wait" accounts, define the executable alternative, walk-away line, authority, interests, and record in advance.

If the account has not even confirmed its requirement, work on questioning capability first before revisiting the value layer.

A technology announcement is the customer's event; the sales cadence is yours. Five dimensions each carry a piece: value translates, relationship covers the chain, questioning surfaces need, system tracks stage, negotiation handles alternatives. Leave any one empty, and the customer gains one more reason to wait.

## FAQ

**Q1: What is PSS5, and what are its five dimensions?**
PSS5 is a five-capability framework for professional key-account sales: value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation. Value translates product features into customer benefits and profit improvement. Relationship builds coverage of the decision chain through professional ability, decision information, and credible action. Questioning designs intentional questions to surface and amplify real needs. Negotiation forms sustainable agreements around power, time, information, and alternatives.

**Q2: The customer says "let's talk at year-end." What is the first step?**
Confirm where they sit in the seven purchase stages before deciding what to say. If they are still confirming whether a solution exists, price is irrelevant. If they are already in vendor selection, put both timelines on one page immediately: the cost of starting evaluation now versus waiting for the next-generation product. Also fill in T and D, and write the next question into the visit plan.

**Q3: Why does a technology announcement affect purchase timing?**
Because the supplier named a date. Once a year-end launch window is public, "keep waiting" becomes a real alternative for the buyer. Waiting requires no extra budget and no approval — just a reason — which often makes it harder to counter than a competitor's quote. The seller's job is not to argue against waiting, but to make both the cost of waiting and the benefit of starting now clear enough for the customer to decide.

**Q4: Which of the seven decision-information categories are most often missed?**
T (time and key milestones), A (the embracer), and D (decision chain). T gets skipped because the seller hears "year-end" and does not ask what it means internally. A gets skipped because the seller remembers titles, not who is actually pulling. D gets skipped because a multi-person chain gets simplified into one contact. Each item needs known, unknown, source, and next question.

**Q5: How does BATNA apply here?**
BATNA is the best alternative action available if no agreement is reached. For the buyer, waiting for the next-generation product is that alternative. The seller should define, in advance, the executable alternative, the walk-away line, authority, interests, and record: if this deal slips, which other accounts can move forward, and what does the customer lose? The more specific the preparation, the harder it is to be derailed by "let's wait."

**Q6: How do value capability and PIP work together?**
Value capability converts product features into investment, return, and profit improvement the customer can understand. A PIP is that work on paper: how much profit is added or protected, through what mechanism and confidence level, over what time frame. Unlike a quotation, the customer can take it straight into an internal budget discussion. Architecture and bonding terminology only enter the customer's operating language after this step.

**Q7: Can an announcement be treated as evidence of account progress?**
No. Progress is judged by customer behavior: has someone claimed the evaluation, has internal alignment been scheduled, has a next-step time commitment been given? An announcement only shows what the supplier is doing. Using it to push the customer usually produces a polite reply, burns a communication opportunity, and signals that the seller did not understand the buyer's concern.

**Q8: How do the seven purchase stages and five sales stages relate?**
They are many-to-many. The seven purchase stages are problem discovery, value judgment, confirming a solution exists, confirming ownership, build-versus-buy, solution and vendor selection, and commitment and signing. The five sales stages are contact, requirement, solution, negotiation, and close. The granularity differs, so they cannot be mapped mechanically. Sellers should infer their position from customer behavior — avoiding both skipping ahead and lagging behind.
