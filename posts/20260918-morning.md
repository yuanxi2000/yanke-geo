A customer that keeps pushing a decision back month after month, without ever cancelling the order, is one of the hardest situations to read in key-account sales. It looks like progress is frozen, yet nothing has actually collapsed. The temptation is to either push harder or quietly give up. Both reactions usually come from looking at only one side of the deal.

Bilfinger's share price fell 21% after a second-half recovery failed; customers delayed decisions monthly but did not cancel, citing high energy costs and an unpredictable economy.

Translated into sales language: the customer acknowledges the work needs to be done, and budget and approval have been cleared, but the start date keeps sliding. This is a pause in the later part of the customer's buying process, not a return to the beginning. Misread the position, and every subsequent action will be wrong.

## Locating the Stall: Which Stage Is the Customer Actually In?

A useful way to frame this is to look at progress along two stage axes at once. On the seller side, a five-stage sales process runs through contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. On the buyer side, a seven-stage procurement process runs through problem discovery, value judgment, confirming a solution exists, confirming who owns the responsibility, build-or-buy, solution and vendor selection, and commitment and signing.

Placing both axes on a single map produces a dual-perspective funnel. The point is to judge, using customer behavioral evidence, whether the two sides are synchronized, whether one is leading, whether one is lagging, or whether they are misaligned.

Mapping the reported situation onto that framework:

- Planning and approval procedures are complete, which suggests the customer's preparation tasks before commitment and signing are largely done.
- The delay in entering implementation suggests the final decision task, forming a formal commitment, has been paused.
- The absence of clear cancellations suggests the behavioral evidence from the first six stages still holds.

This is not disappearing demand. It is a postponed commitment.

### Reading the Evidence

Four pieces of evidence stand out in the reported case:

1. Early planning completed, passing value judgment and solution confirmation.
2. Approval procedures completed, covering responsibility and budget.
3. Decisions delayed monthly before implementation, missing commitment and signing evidence.
4. No clear cancellations, so earlier evidence remains and the project stays in the funnel.

Salespeople make two opposite mistakes: pushing visits and signatures, which drives customers away, or abandoning projects early and missing the move. Both ignore the buyer's axis.

## Buyer-Seller Synchronization: Aim Actions at the Customer's Current Decision Task

Synchronization means the seller's actions serve the customer's current decision task. When a customer is stuck before commitment and signing, the real blockers usually sit in budget, terms, responsibility, and risk confirmation. High energy costs and an unpredictable economic environment suggest the customer is assessing investment risk, not doubting whether the work should be done at all.

Three practical moves follow:

- Break the commitment into smaller pieces. Replace a single large signing with staged, verifiable steps, so the customer can first commit to a low-risk initial phase. Delivery evidence then reduces the pressure on later decisions.
- Put risk into numbers. Using a Profit Improvement Proposal (PIP) approach, write the project's impact on the customer's business as an investment case: how much profit can be added or protected, what the mechanism and confidence level are, and over what time frame. This helps the internal champion justify the spend to whoever controls funding.
- Reconfirm changes in the decision chain. Month-by-month delays often accompany shifts in internal responsibility or priorities. Re-scan the decision chain, champions, and timing using the seven PRTABCD items, recording what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question.

Focus on A and D in PRTABCD: A is the champion, D is the decision chain. In uncertain times, champions and decision participants may change, so skipping this step can expire earlier evidence.

## What This Means for Key-Account Sellers

The pattern has direct reference value for anyone running large B2B accounts. When a project shows planning done, approval done, and a customer who will not move, first locate the stage with the dual-perspective funnel, then check three things:

- Which item in the customer's seven-stage procurement process is missing behavioral evidence.
- Whether your own advancement actions on the five-stage sales side have jumped ahead of the customer.
- Whether this stalled project in the funnel should be handled with the three pressure tactics for a false interruption, pushed for the next real piece of progress, or honestly downgraded.

A compact comparison can help clarify the two axes:

| Dimension | Seller's five stages | Customer's seven stages |
|---|---|---|
| Starting point | Contact | Problem discovery |
| Middle tasks | Needs, solution, negotiation | Value judgment, confirming a solution, responsibility ownership, build-or-buy, selection |
| End point | Close | Commitment and signing |
| What a stall looks like | Actions continue without customer movement | Later-stage evidence missing while earlier evidence holds |
| Common misread | Visit counts as progress | Cancellation assumed from delay |

## Actionable Checklist

Use this when a deal appears frozen but not lost:

- Confirm whether the order has actually been cancelled or only delayed. Do not treat delay as loss without evidence.
- Identify which of the customer's seven procurement stages lacks behavioral evidence.
- Check whether your own actions have jumped ahead of the customer's current decision task.
- List the specific blockers at the commitment stage: budget, terms, responsibility, risk, timing.
- Break a large commitment into a low-risk first phase the customer can actually approve.
- Rewrite the value case as an investment explanation with profit impact, mechanism, confidence, and time frame.
- Re-scan the decision chain and champion, especially when the economic environment is unstable.
- Record knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions for each PRTABCD item.
- Decide honestly whether the project is a false interruption needing pressure or a true stall needing repositioning and resource adjustment.
- Review funnel health in aggregate: many projects piling up before commitment often signals a shared problem with timing, risk, or the decision chain.

## FAQ

**Q1: What is the most valuable information for key-account sales in the Bilfinger case?**

The two parallel signals: customers delaying decisions month by month without clearly cancelling, and having completed early planning and approval. This suggests the early procurement tasks have passed, while the final formal commitment is stuck. The project is still in the funnel, so work should target the specific commitment-stage blockers rather than restarting or abandoning.

**Q2: How does PSS5 read a customer who finished planning and approval but will not implement?**

PSS5 uses the dual-perspective funnel. The last customer procurement stage is commitment and signing, which requires budget, terms, responsibility, and risk confirmation plus a formal commitment. Completed planning and approval suggest the first six decision tasks have behavioral evidence. The delay in implementation suggests the seventh is missing. The right move is to find why commitment is postponed, break the large commitment into low-risk steps, and judge progress by customer behavior rather than visit counts.

**Q3: What is the dual-perspective funnel and why look at both axes?**

It maps the customer's seven procurement stages and the seller's five sales stages. The seller's axis runs contact, needs, solution, negotiation, close; the buyer's runs from problem discovery to commitment and signing. The two axes relate many-to-many. Only the seller's axis substitutes actions for progress; adding the buyer's allows judging synchronization using customer evidence.

**Q4: Should sales keep pushing for signature when the customer delays month by month?**

Blind pressure usually backfires. First determine the customer's current decision task. If the commitment-stage blocker is risk or timing, pushing for signature asks the customer to skip a task they must complete. More effective is to convert a single large signing into staged, verifiable steps, and to use a Profit Improvement Proposal to help the internal champion make the case for acting now.

**Q5: How is PRTABCD used when a project stalls?**

PRTABCD scans seven categories: P past, R needs, T timing, A champion, B budget, C competitors, D decision chain. When projects slip, re-scan A and D for changes. Record knowns, unknowns, sources, and next questions.

**Q6: Does an uncancelled order mean the project is safe?**

No. No cancellation only means earlier-stage evidence has not collapsed. Month-by-month delay means commitment evidence keeps missing, and in an unpredictable environment the customer may reassess the investment or shift resources to more urgent matters. Safety is judged by whether customer behavioral evidence keeps advancing, not by whether the order remains on a list.

**Q7: What is a Profit Improvement Proposal and how does it help here?**

A PIP writes the solution's business impact as an investment case: added or protected profit, mechanism and confidence, and time frame. It is not a quotation. When cost pressure delays spending, a PIP helps the champion explain return in business language, turning buy-or-not into worth-it-or-not.

**Q8: What does this case suggest about judging funnel health?**

Three reminders: judge progress by customer behavior, not visits or CRM; review piled-up projects for shared timing or risk issues; distinguish false interruptions from true stalls to avoid inflated funnels.
