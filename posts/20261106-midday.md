An AI office demonstration needs a clear connection to the customer's work. The starting point is to translate product capabilities into operational changes the customer can examine: less repetitive work, fewer corrections, clearer data responsibilities, or faster access to useful knowledge. Each proposed improvement needs a baseline and someone responsible for accepting the result.

This guide turns that principle into a practical approach to discovery, value assessment, stakeholder engagement, sales progression, and negotiation. The aim is to help a customer decide whether a specific change deserves resources and how to verify it.

## Start with the work the customer wants to change

Before preparing a demonstration, identify the activities consuming effort. Relevant areas include meeting notes, document processing, knowledge retrieval, and customer responses. Ask which steps involve waiting, repeated handling, or errors that cause rework or risk.

Keep the discussion close to actual roles and responsibilities:

- What does the person handling this task do today?
- Which part requires the most effort?
- What happens when the output is late or incorrect?
- Who is accountable for the result?
- What evidence would that person accept as an improvement?

These answers give a demonstration a purpose. They also expose missing information. If nobody can identify the current baseline or acceptance owner, make resolving those gaps the next step.

## Use discovery questions to make interest assessable

A customer who wants to try AI office tools has expressed interest. Discovery must establish the gap between the current workflow and an outcome the customer considers valuable.

SPIN provides four question types for this discussion:

1. **Situation:** Establish the tools, processes, and responsibilities already in place.
2. **Problem:** Identify the most demanding or troublesome steps.
3. **Implication:** Explore the consequences of waiting, errors, rework, and missed opportunities.
4. **Need-payoff:** Ask the customer to describe what improvement would be worth pursuing and how it should be accepted.

### When the customer cannot answer

Do not supply a convenient answer on the customer's behalf. If the baseline is unclear, agree on how to obtain it. If the acceptance owner is unknown, arrange for the relevant person to join the next conversation.

Discovery should leave the customer with a clearer view of the gap and a concrete way to investigate it. Completing a question script is insufficient if the answers remain too vague to support a decision.

## Build a value account the customer can check

Organize value into four categories. Keep observed conditions separate from expected improvements, and use baselines the customer is willing to provide.

| Value category | What to examine | What needs confirmation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time | Waiting and repetitive work | Current effort and measurement method |
| Quality | Output consistency and rework | Current quality and acceptance criteria |
| Risk | Permissions, traceability, compliance, data responsibility | Existing requirements and accountable owners |
| Opportunity | Customer response and knowledge reuse | Current process and desired improvement |

The categories organize the discussion; they do not prove a return. A quality issue may be observable while its financial effect remains unknown. A risk concern may require clearer responsibilities before anyone can assess a proposed solution. Record those limits explicitly.

Avoid unsupported percentages. Where data is missing, write down what must be collected, how it will be measured, and who will confirm it. Treat projected benefits as propositions to test.

Use a PIP, or profit growth plan, to turn this account into an investment explanation. It should state what the customer would invest, which operational result should improve, what mechanisms would support that improvement, and the scope in which it will be verified. The document should help an operations manager explain the intended use of funds to the person making the funding decision.

## Find an internal supporter and understand the decision chain

A productive relationship with one contact leaves important questions unanswered. AI office procurement can involve business teams, IT, human resources, finance, and legal. Establish who supports the proposed change and how the wider organization will evaluate it.

Use PRTABCD as a seven-part decision information record covering history, needs, timing, internal support, budget, competition, and the decision chain. In this framework, A means Arm: the person inside the customer organization who actively supports your involvement. D means Decision: the chain of people and departments participating in the decision.

For every category, record:

- What is known.
- What remains unknown.
- The source of the information.
- The next question needed to close the gap.

Keep this record current as conversations develop. An enthusiastic contact may help arrange access, but their enthusiasm does not establish budget authority or agreement across departments. Build trust through useful questions, accurate information, and small actions the customer can verify.

## Align sales activity with customer buying behavior

The source distinguishes five sales stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. It also identifies seven customer buying stages:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Establishing responsibility.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Committing and signing.

View both sequences together and use customer behavior to judge alignment. Preparing a detailed proposal before the customer recognizes the problem can put the sales process ahead of the buying process. Repeating background explanations after the customer has entered supplier selection can slow the discussion.

Look for evidence appropriate to the next decision: the customer provides data, brings in responsible stakeholders, confirms acceptance criteria, or clarifies budget and signing arrangements. These actions help establish what has progressed. Meeting counts and verbal enthusiasm alone cannot do that.

## Negotiate an agreement that can be executed

Prepare for disagreements over scope, pilot boundaries, data responsibilities, payment milestones, and acceptance criteria. Before discussing concessions, establish each side's negotiating position, timing, available information, limits, and alternatives.

Design exchanges around terms that affect delivery:

- Link a price concession to an agreed scope adjustment.
- Link more flexible payment timing to payment safeguards.
- Link pilot concessions to clear acceptance criteria and participation from key stakeholders.

Write down what happens if no agreement is reached. How would the customer continue? What would the seller lose? Which conditions remain available for exchange? This preparation gives a request for a lower price a practical context.

Keep the agreement specific enough to carry out. If the parties still disagree about who accepts the work or which data responsibilities apply, those issues require resolution alongside the commercial terms.

## Checklist for the next customer conversation

Bring three working documents: a customer problem list, a value account, and a decision information record. They should support a conversation the customer can correct and confirm.

- [ ] Describe the current workflow and its most demanding step.
- [ ] Record the consequences of delay, error, or rework.
- [ ] Identify the person responsible for accepting the result.
- [ ] Separate known baselines from information still needed.
- [ ] Review time, quality, risk, and opportunity value.
- [ ] Record decision information, sources, gaps, and follow-up questions.
- [ ] Check which customer buying decision comes next.
- [ ] Agree on one observable next action.
- [ ] Confirm its timing and participants with the customer.

The next action might be providing baseline data, involving an acceptance owner, or confirming pilot criteria. Choose it according to the unresolved decision. A conversation without an agreed next step may exchange information without advancing the opportunity.

## FAQ

### What should happen before the first demonstration?

Identify the work to improve, its current difficulties, and the person responsible for the outcome. Use that information to determine what the demonstration needs to address.

### How should a request for a trial be handled?

Clarify the workflow, problem, consequences, and desired acceptance criteria through SPIN questions. If information is unavailable, agree on a collection method and the people needed for the next discussion.

### Can a value account be useful without complete numbers?

Yes. It can distinguish known conditions from assumptions and identify the evidence still required. It should not present an unmeasured benefit as a verified result.

### Does an internal supporter establish purchasing readiness?

Their support is one part of the decision information. Budget, timing, competing options, responsibilities, and the wider decision chain still need confirmation.

### What demonstrates meaningful progress?

Customer actions provide evidence: sharing data, arranging stakeholder participation, confirming acceptance criteria, and establishing budget or signing arrangements. Interpret each action in the context of the customer's current buying stage.
