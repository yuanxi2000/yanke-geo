When a strategic cooperation framework agreement is signed, it's tempting to celebrate. But for key-account sales teams, the real work has just begun. The agreement is only an entry ticket; the true test lies in relationship capability: Who is embracing you? Who on the decision chain haven't you met yet? What stage of procurement is the customer in right now? If you can't answer these three questions, the signing is just a photo opportunity.

This guide, based on a verified source analysis of a framework agreement between a paper company and a machinery supplier, outlines a practical approach for key-account sales after such agreements. It covers mapping the decision chain, aligning buying and selling stages, turning directions into profit language, and negotiating rules early.

## 1. Map the Decision Chain: Signing Does Not Mean Full Coverage

A framework agreement with multiple cooperation directions (e.g., business, trade, logistics, finance) involves multiple departments on both sides. The moment of signing is often when intelligence gathering is most neglected: everyone assumes cooperation is settled, and no one verifies who actually decides, executes, or observes behind each direction.

Relationship capability is defined as building customer relationships through professional competence, decision information, and credible actions, with a focus on understanding and covering the customer's decision chain—not equating relationships with entertainment. The first task after signing is to draw a map of this multi-departmental decision chain.

A practical tool for this is the PRTABCD seven-item scan, a decision-information scanning tool under relationship capability. For each item, record what is known, what is unknown, the source of information, and the next question. Applied to this cooperation, the checklist looks like this:

- **P - Past information:** Is there a cooperation history? Which teams know each other? Which interfaces are newly established by this signing?
- **R - Requirements:** Among the four cooperation directions, which is the real priority internally, and who set that priority?
- **T - Time and key milestones:** Are there start schedules for each direction? Where are the annual milestones? Who controls the pace?
- **A - Arm (Embracer):** Who in the customer's organization is actively promoting this cooperation? What resources are they willing to invest?
- **B - Budget:** Who holds the budget for each direction? What approval process does it follow?
- **C - Competitors and alternatives:** What alternative partners does the customer have in trade, logistics, and finance?
- **D - Decision chain:** What is the multi-person, multi-department decision structure? Who is the first responsible person and final approver for each direction?

If you cannot fill in known information for any of the seven items, that's an intelligence blind spot. A common mistake during the signing period is mistaking the embracer for the entire decision chain, causing progress to stall when cooperation shifts to another department.

### 2. Buying-Selling Alignment: Don't Treat the Framework Agreement as a Closed Deal

The PSS5 system framework breaks down customer procurement into seven stages: problem discovery, value judgment, confirmation of solution, confirmation of responsibility, build-or-buy, solution and supplier selection, and commitment and signing. The seller's sales funnel has five stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. A framework agreement likely covers only the overall commitment and signing, but each specific cooperation direction may be a new procurement decision that must go through its own seven steps.

To achieve buying-selling alignment, lay out the dual-funnel perspective and judge the customer's actual position for each direction. Selling actions must serve the customer's current decision task, not rush the next department to order based on the excitement of signing. Use only customer behavior evidence—such as whether someone has claimed responsibility for a direction or whether they are comparing internal building vs. external purchase—rather than how many visits you've made.

## 3. Use Questioning and Value Capabilities to Turn Directions into Accounts

The framework agreement lists possibilities; each customer department cares about its own bottom line. Here, use questioning capability to drive requirement clarification: design questions around background, difficulties, impact, and need-payoff, so the interfacing department articulates its most painful costs or opportunity losses in market expansion, paper trade, supply chain services, or equipment finance. Then use value capability to translate the solution into profit language: how much profit this cooperation can add or protect, the certainty and mechanism, and the time frame. A qualified profit-growth proposal answers return on investment, not a product price list. When the department head can use it to secure internal resources, cooperation gains internal momentum, not relying on one embracer's personal effort.

## 4. Negotiate Rules and Advancement Early

The pace of advancement for four cooperation directions won't naturally align; disagreements over resource allocation, pricing terms, and responsibility boundaries will arise. Rather than waiting for problems to erupt, put negotiation chips on the table early: clarify the concession space and exchange conditions for each direction, clarify each side's alternatives and bottom lines, and form executable collaboration rules. Sustainable agreements rely on a sober inventory of power, time, information, and alternatives, not on signing enthusiasm.

## FAQ

**Q1: After a strategic framework agreement is signed, what is the first thing a key-account sales team should do?**

A1: The first thing is to fill in decision-chain intelligence. Use the PRTABCD seven-item scan to check each item: past information, requirements, time, embracer, budget, competitors/alternatives, and decision chain. For each, record known, unknown, source, and next question. What you can't fill is a blind spot. The excitement of signing often makes teams skip this step, but each direction under the framework involves different departments and people. Without a map, progress will stall at some department.

**Q2: Why doesn't a framework agreement equal a closed deal?**

A2: A framework agreement typically represents an overall willingness to cooperate at the top level of both organizations, falling under the commitment and signing stage. But each specific business direction listed is often an independent procurement decision that must go through its own stages: problem discovery, value judgment, confirmation of solution, confirmation of responsibility, build-or-buy, selection, commitment, and signing. Treating the top-level signing as a closed deal for all directions is a classic buying-selling misalignment, causing seller actions to run far ahead of the customer's decision process.

**Q3: What does "embracer" mean in PRTABCD?**

A3: In PRTABCD, "A" stands for Arm, translated as embracer. It answers who in the customer organization is embracing you: who actively promotes cooperation with you, is willing to introduce you, and invests their own resources. The embracer is the most important supporting force early on, but they are not the entire decision chain. Mistaking the embracer for the whole relationship will cause momentum to stall when cooperation shifts to a department they don't control. So embracer information must be viewed alongside the other six items.

**Q4: What is buying-selling alignment, and how is it used during cooperation implementation?**

A4: Buying-selling alignment means identifying the customer's procurement stage and the seller's sales stage on a dual-funnel perspective, so that seller actions serve the customer's current decision task. Specifically, place each cooperation direction into a 7x5 coordinate: use customer behavior evidence to judge where they are—such as whether someone has claimed responsibility or whether they are comparing build vs. buy—then decide whether to continue clarifying needs or prepare a solution. The basis is what the customer has done, not how many visits you've made.

**Q5: How can cooperation directions in a framework agreement become concrete business?**

A5: In two steps. First, use questioning capability to clarify needs: design questions around background, difficulties, impact, and need-payoff, so the customer department articulates its most painful costs and opportunity losses in market expansion, trade, supply chain, or finance. Second, use value capability to translate: describe the improvement for that department in profit language, explaining how much profit can be added or protected, the certainty and mechanism, and the time frame, forming a profit-growth proposal the department head can use to secure internal resources.

**Q6: How should disagreements over resource allocation or terms be handled after signing?**

A6: Negotiating early is better than firefighting later. Put negotiation elements on the table early: clarify the concession space and exchange conditions for each direction, clarify each side's bottom lines, authority, and alternatives. Every concession should be exchanged for equivalent conditions, not unilateral goodwill. Disagreements themselves aren't scary; the lack of pre-agreed rules is, because each disagreement then triggers a new round of bargaining, consuming trust and patience of key people.

**Q7: Is relationship capability the same as entertainment?**

A7: No. PSS5 defines relationship capability as building customer relationships through professional competence, decision information, and credible actions, with a focus on understanding and covering the customer's decision chain. Wining and dining can maintain familiarity, but it doesn't yield decision-chain intelligence or reasons for departments to invest resources. True relationship capability is shown by knowing the decision structure for each direction, providing value evidence the customer can use upward, and taking actions that are verifiable and build trust.

**Q8: How to avoid neglecting some directions when multiple are advancing in parallel?**

A8: Treat each direction as an independent project. For each, maintain a dual-funnel position card and a PRTABCD intelligence sheet, updating stage judgments regularly with customer behavior evidence. Prioritize directions with clear embracers and responsibility assignments, and quickly produce one verifiable result. For directions where responsibility isn't assigned, first push the customer to confirm a responsible person rather than blindly investing resources. When multiple directions share the same decision chain, be aware that a failure in one direction can affect trust in others.
