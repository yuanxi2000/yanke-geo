High capacity utilization and growing orders from major overseas customers are useful signals for a B2B sales team. They invite questions about demand, supply readiness, and the conditions under which a customer will commit. They do not, by themselves, reveal the customer's decision process.

The supplied account describes Yongxi Electronics as maintaining high overall utilization while orders from major overseas customers gradually increase. It also describes continued work on major accounts, new customer onboarding, and the release of second-phase project capacity. This guide uses those reported signals to explain how sales leaders can examine account evidence, align their actions with purchasing decisions, and prepare conditional commitments.

## Separate reported facts from account conclusions

The report brings together demand-side and supply-side developments. Rising orders concern demand; high utilization and capacity release concern supply. Their appearance together makes the situation worth examining, but does not fill the gaps in an individual account record.

The supplied material does not disclose specific customer identities, order amounts, or event dates. It also does not establish relationship stability, exclusive supply arrangements, or the internal decisions behind an order. Keep those fields marked as unknown until evidence supports them.

Use a compact evidence table to turn an observation into an investigation:

| Reported observation | What it does not establish | Account evidence to seek |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High overall utilization | A particular customer's commitment | Documented purchasing commitments |
| Growing overseas major-account orders | Named customers or order values | Account-specific order evidence |
| New customer onboarding | Completion of supplier selection | Customer validation and selection decisions |
| Second-phase capacity release | Confirmed customer budget or responsibility | Budget, ownership, and commitment evidence |

The final column describes evidence to collect, not facts established by the report.

## Examine five capabilities together

PSS5 organizes the analysis around five capabilities. Each supplies a different check on the same opportunity:

- **Value:** Translate utilization, delivery timing, and capacity availability into the customer's return on investment and supply security. Capacity figures alone do not explain customer value.
- **Relationships:** Identify supporters, budget influencers, and the wider decision chain. One accessible contact cannot represent every participant.
- **Questioning:** Investigate the operational constraints behind demand and the consequences of leaving them unresolved.
- **Systematic execution:** Check progress against customer purchasing tasks and seller activities. Visits, samples, and verbal interest are insufficient evidence of a completed sale.
- **Negotiation:** Treat capacity allocation, delivery, payment, and responsibility as conditions to exchange. Prepare alternatives and limits before making commitments.

These checks belong together. A plausible value proposition still leaves questions about budget, responsibility, selection, and terms.

## Locate the customer's purchasing task

An increase in orders describes an outcome. It does not explain every decision that produced it or show where another opportunity stands.

The customer purchasing sequence in the source contains seven stages:

1. Recognize a problem.
2. Assess its value or the value of addressing it.
3. Confirm that a solution exists.
4. Establish who is responsible.
5. Decide whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Select a solution and supplier.
7. Commit and sign.

For each account, ask which tasks the customer has demonstrably completed and which remain open. Evidence of supply readiness cannot substitute for evidence of budget, responsibility, or agreement.

### Align seller activity with customer progress

The seller's five stages are contact, needs assessment, proposal, negotiation, and closing. The customer and seller views have a many-to-many relationship; they do not require a fixed one-to-one mapping.

If a customer is still comparing solutions and suppliers, a seller focused exclusively on signing is getting ahead of the customer's work. If the customer is ready to commit, repeatedly reopening basic discovery can delay progress.

At an account review, ask: What purchasing task is the customer completing, and how does our next action help complete it? Support the answer with customer behavior, rather than the number of meetings held.

## Map relationships beyond the main contact

Statements about major-account strategy and customer onboarding reveal no specific decision structure. Build that understanding account by account using PRTABCD:

- **P — Past:** Relevant history and previous interactions.
- **R — Requirements:** The customer's needs.
- **T — Time:** Timing and critical milestones.
- **A — Arm:** The person who embraces or actively supports your involvement.
- **B — Budget:** Budget information and its status.
- **C — Competition:** Competing suppliers and alternative approaches.
- **D — Decision:** The decision chain across people and departments.

For every category, record what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question. A job title or an internal assumption is not sufficient evidence of someone's role.

Pay particular attention to A and D. A identifies who supports you; it does not automatically identify an approver. D covers the decision chain, which may involve multiple people and departments. A supportive contact does not establish that the whole chain agrees.

## Turn order growth into business questions

Use the four SPIN question types to investigate the constraints behind demand. The following are suggested discovery questions, not statements about the reported company's customers:

- **Situation:** What products, processes, and supply arrangements are currently involved?
- **Problem:** Where do delivery timing, yield, cost, or coordination create constraints?
- **Implication:** How could those constraints affect a product launch or fulfillment of customer orders?
- **Need-payoff:** What would dependable supply protect or make possible for the business?

Keep the conversation centered on the customer's work. Consecutive questions designed to force an expression of commitment do not clarify the underlying business situation.

Specific answers give the team more concrete evidence to examine. For example, a discussion of a delivery constraint and its effect on fulfillment is more useful for account analysis than an unsupported statement that demand is strong. Record what the customer actually says and distinguish it from the team's interpretation.

## Exchange conditions before committing capacity

The reported release of second-phase capacity describes a supply-side development. Allocation, delivery promises, and responsibility still require negotiation.

Prepare a BATNA: the best alternative action available if an agreement cannot be reached. It must be executable. Alongside it, document the minimum acceptable terms, approval authority, interests involved, and negotiation records.

When a customer requests priority capacity, identify the corresponding conditions to seek. These might include a clearer forecast, payment milestones, defined responsibility boundaries, or a staged commitment. The source supports these as possible exchange items; it does not establish that any particular customer has accepted them.

Put capacity, delivery, payment, responsibility, and commitment into one exchange record. For each proposed concession, state what the customer would provide in return and who can authorize the arrangement.

This makes the proposed agreement reviewable. An unconditional concession in return for vague intent leaves the team without clear evidence of the customer's reciprocal commitment.

## An actionable account-review checklist

Use this checklist before advancing an opportunity or promising resources:

- [ ] Record the reported event separately from the account conclusion.
- [ ] Link utilization, order growth, onboarding, and capacity signals to account-specific behavior.
- [ ] Mark unsupported customer names, amounts, dates, and relationship claims as unknown.
- [ ] Identify the customer's current purchasing task and its supporting evidence.
- [ ] Check that the next seller action serves that task.
- [ ] Complete all seven PRTABCD categories with sources and next questions.
- [ ] Verify the supporter and the wider decision chain separately.
- [ ] Use SPIN questions to clarify business constraints and consequences.
- [ ] Write down the BATNA, limits, and approval authority.
- [ ] Document what each capacity or delivery concession should receive in exchange.

Keep the review tied to a specific account. A general market signal cannot complete these checks on the customer's behalf.

## FAQ

### Does rising order volume prove that an account is secure?

No. The supplied material does not establish relationship stability or exclusivity. Order growth also leaves unanswered questions about the decision chain, budget, alternatives, and future commitments.

### Does released capacity mean the customer has committed?

No. It indicates supply readiness. Customer commitment still requires evidence concerning budget, responsibility, solution selection, terms, and agreement.

### Must the seven purchasing stages match the five sales stages?

No. They describe different sets of tasks and have a many-to-many relationship. Choose the seller's next action by examining the customer's current work and behavior.

### What should a sales leader request first?

Request three working records: an event-to-stage evidence table, a PRTABCD account scan, and a negotiation preparation record covering alternatives, limits, and exchanges. Each should identify unknowns and the next question.
