When a customer publicly commits hundreds of millions to a new smart-factory project, the announcement is only the starting point. The real work for a key-account sales team is translating that commitment into a verifiable business case the customer's own people can defend internally.

One example: a planned ~425 million transmission-tower smart-factory investment, aiming to boost an operating subsidiary's asset efficiency and competitiveness. Sponsor, amount, and goals are public; build approach, ownership, and supplier selection are not.

That gap is the entire sales problem. The money has moved at the funding layer, but the buying process has not yet reached the point where a quote is meaningful.

## The Money Has Moved, but the Buying Process Has Not

A capital-investment announcement tells you two things reliably: the project has cleared internal funding approval, and the expected outcome has been named publicly. It does not tell you the implementation approach, the timeline, the accountable owner, or the selection criteria.

Treating the announcement as a buying signal is a mistake. Treating it as the first verifiable piece of customer intelligence is not.

What the announcement confirms:

- The project exists and has funding attached.
- The sponsoring entity and the operating entity are identified.
- The intended outcome is defined in the customer's own language.

What remains blank and must be confirmed directly:

- Build-versus-buy: will the customer build internally or procure externally?
- Who owns the result and carries the accountability.
- The criteria by which suppliers will be evaluated.
- The timeline and milestones.

Until those blanks are filled, any pricing conversation is a guess. The discipline here is to separate what is confirmed from what is assumed, and to convert the unknowns into specific questions for specific people.

## Two Layers of Money: Who Allocates and Who Uses

The PSS5 framework distinguishes between two internal constituencies when it comes to money. Box 1 controls funding allocation and approval. Box 2 operates and uses the funds.

This scale of investment sits with Box 1 for approval, but Box 2 must live with and report on it. Selling specifications only to Box 2 without helping build a Box 1 case stalls the project at every gate.

The practical implication: cover both layers. Understand what Box 1 needs to see to release funds, and what Box 2 needs to deliver to justify them.

## Value Capability: Convert Asset Efficiency into Operating Numbers

Value capability means translating a product or service into the customer's investment and operating value. The announcement says "asset-use efficiency." A sales team must reduce that phrase to numbers the customer can check.

Monetization follows a sequence:

1. Identify the operating steps the solution can affect.
2. Quantify the likely improvement.
3. Convert that improvement into revenue, cost, profit, and payback period.

For a smart factory, affected metrics include processing cost per unit, waiting and rework losses, and availability's effect on delivery cycles—figures the customer's finance and operations can verify.

A profit improvement plan (PIP) is the vehicle for this. It answers three questions: how much profit can be added or protected, by what mechanism, and within what time frame. A conventional proposal organized around features, specifications, and price does not survive Box 1 review.

### What a PIP Must Contain

- The financial mechanism, not just the outcome.
- A time frame for realization.
- Numbers the customer can independently check.
- A clear link to the stated efficiency goal.

## Relationship Capability: Scan the Decision Landscape with PRTABCD

Relationship capability is about building trust through professional insight, decision information, and verifiable action—and about understanding and covering the customer's decision chain.

Use PRTABCD as a structured scan. For each item, record what is known, what is unknown, the source, and the next question.

| Item | Focus |
| --- | --- |
| P — Past | The customer's history of equipment and engineering investment, prior suppliers, delivery reputation |
| R — Requirement | What "asset-use efficiency" and "competitiveness" mean at the operating-step level |
| T — Time | Public project cycle and acceptance milestones, plus the customer's internal timetable |
| A — Arm | Who inside the organization is championing this and will speak for you |
| B — Budget | How the funds are structured and released in phases |
| C — Competitor | Other suppliers being compared, plus the internal-build alternative |
| D — Decision | The multi-person, multi-department chain: who evaluates, who approves, who owns the result |

The value of this scan is that it makes blind spots visible. Empty cells become visit questions rather than assumptions.

## Questioning Capability: Surface the Gap with SPIN

Questioning capability relies on intentional question design that lets the customer see the problem and the value of change for themselves. The sequence matters.

- Situation: Which parts of the current production and delivery process depend most on human judgment?
- Problem: What do rework, waiting, and downtime in those steps cost in a year?
- Implication: If those losses continue to accumulate, what does that mean for delivery cycles and customer reputation?
- Need-Payoff: If those steps were stabilized, how far could asset-use efficiency improve?

The common error is skipping Problem and Implication and jumping straight to Need-Payoff. The customer has not yet felt the pain, and the question reads as a pitch.

## System Capability: Locate Yourself on the Dual-View Funnel

System capability places the seller's progression and the buyer's decision process on the same map. The seller side has five stages: contact, requirement, proposal, negotiation, close. The buyer side has seven stages, from problem recognition through commitment and signing. The two axes are many-to-many, not one-to-one.

Position is determined by customer behavior evidence, not by seller optimism:

- Has the customer arranged a technical exchange?
- Has the customer provided evaluation criteria?
- Has budget and accountability been assigned?

Visit counts, verbal assurances, and the seller's own confidence do not count as progress.

## Negotiation Capability: Trade Conditions, Not Price

Negotiation capability requires clarity on power, time, information, and alternatives before designing concessions and exchanges.

Before sitting down, define your BATNA: if no agreement is reached, what is your best alternative action, is it executable, where is your bottom line, and who has authority to confirm it?

Large investments advance through phases, each offering tradeables: payment timing, implementation sequencing, training and spares bundling, later-phase priority. Leverage comes from information and alternatives, not a tough posture.

## Actionable Checklist for This Week

- Select three verifiable operating numbers and translate asset-use efficiency into cost, output, or delivery-cycle improvements. Write the first page of the investment case.
- Fill out a PRTABCD table. For each item, mark known, unknown, source, and next question. Turn empty cells into visit questions.
- Identify who inside the customer organization is the champion (A) and who is accountable for the project's result. Then schedule the next action.
- Confirm whether the customer is leaning toward internal build or external procurement, and what evaluation criteria will be used.
- Prepare a one-page PIP outline that Box 2 could use to brief Box 1.

## FAQ

**Q1: What should sales focus on in an investment announcement like this?**

Key facts: sponsor, scope, amount, goals. The announcement omits responsibility, selection process, and procurement timing. Turn those blanks into an intelligence checklist, not a closing signal.

**Q2: Why is this a funding-layer decision?**

Board approval and a defined investment amount mean the matter has moved from a business-unit idea to the funding and approval layer. PSS5 distinguishes Box 1 (funding allocation and approval) from Box 2 (operations and use of funds). Talking only to Box 2 about equipment parameters, without helping Box 2 build a case for Box 1, makes the project harder to advance.

**Q3: How does value capability apply to a smart-factory project?**

Translate product functions into investment and operating returns. Identify the operating steps the solution affects, quantify the improvement, and convert it into financial language: revenue, cost, profit, payback period. The announcement says asset-use efficiency; sales must reduce that to checkable numbers.

**Q4: How does a PIP differ from a normal proposal?**

A PIP (profit improvement plan) states how much profit can be added or protected, by what mechanism, and when. At this scale, funders want an investment case, not features, parameters, and price.

**Q5: What do A and D mean in PRTABCD?**

A is Arm—who inside the customer organization is championing you and will speak for you. D is Decision—the multi-person, multi-department decision chain, not a single decision-maker. PRTABCD uses seven categories of decision information to surface intelligence blind spots.

**Q6: What is the most common mistake when using SPIN?**

Skipping Problem and Implication and jumping to Need-Payoff. The customer has not yet felt the pain, and the question becomes a pitch. The order is Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-Payoff.

**Q7: How do you judge real progress on a project?**

Look at customer behavior evidence, not your own feelings. System capability places the seller's five stages and the buyer's seven stages on one map. Each buyer step must be evidenced by a customer-side action. Evaluation criteria and assigned budget and accountability matter more than visit counts.

**Q8: What should be prepared before negotiating a large project?**

Clarify power, time, information, and alternatives; design concessions and exchanges. Define your BATNA, bottom line, and decision-maker. Trade conditions—payment timing, sequencing, bundling—rather than cutting price alone.
