A publicly reported computing services contract illustrates large B2B deal structure. After buying computing equipment, a company planned a five-year Computing Services Contract with Company B, renewed annually, at 66.5603 million yuan per year, 1.84% of 2025 operating revenue.

The deal is interesting precisely because it is not dramatic. It is large enough to require serious internal justification, yet small enough that it does not threaten the buyer's survival. It is long enough to signal commitment, yet renegotiated every year. For anyone selling into large accounts, that combination is where the real lessons live.

## What the 1.84% Revenue Share Tells You About the Deal

As reported, the buyer is the company; the provider is Company B. The contract has a five-year term, signed annually, at 66.5603 million yuan per year, 1.84% of 2025 operating revenue. Signing date and full legal names were not disclosed.

Three numbers reveal the buyer's situation: 1.84% of revenue is not life-or-death but not casually approved; 66.5603 million yuan per year exceeds single-department authority, requiring multi-person approval; and a five-year term with annual signing locks in supply while keeping yearly reassessment.

The key lesson: value capability converts product features into an investment, return, and profit improvement the customer understands, explained with business numbers. Selling a service capability priced annually, not machines, changes the pricing unit and purchase rationale.

The annual signing clause is the sharpest design in this contract: the customer expresses long-term commitment through five years, while retaining the right to review value every year through annual renewal. The supplier's value proof does not end once the contract is signed; it must be earned again every year.

## Why a Customer Would Require Annual Signing

Reasoning from the buyer's position, annual signing addresses at least three concerns.

The first is technology risk. Computing equipment and technology iterate quickly, and locking in for five years could mean using outdated resources by year three. Annual renewal preserves the customer's freedom to change solutions.

The second is business risk. If the company's own computing services business underperforms expectations, a long-term contract becomes a sunk burden. Signing annually cuts the risk into five pieces.

The third is negotiating leverage. Annual signing means every year is a small negotiation, and the customer always holds a BATNA — the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, meaning the best fallback action available when the parties cannot reach agreement, such as switching suppliers or adjusting the proportion of self-built capacity.

These points apply to large-account sales. When customers propose annual signing or phased renewal, most salespeople feel distrusted. Actually, a customer willing to sign a five-year framework has accepted your value direction and asks you to re-prove it yearly. The real worry is producing new evidence each year.

## Four Gates a Supplier Must Pass to Win This Kind of Deal

### Value gate

Translate computing services into the business math behind the customer's 1.84% revenue share. Explain how this investment supports revenue, controls cost, and improves profit — rather than listing machine specifications and discounts.

### Relationship gate

A contract worth 66.5603 million yuan per year involves business, finance, technology, and legal roles. Sales must use the seven-item PRTABCD scan to clarify past history, needs, timing, embraceers, budget, competition, and the decision chain — especially identifying who in the customer organization is embracing you.

### Process gate

Use the five sales stages — contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing — against the customer's seven procurement stages to judge whether both sides are synchronized. Avoid rushing to contract terms while the customer is still confirming that a solution even exists.

### Negotiation gate

Since the customer can re-sign every year, the supplier must design concessions and condition exchanges in the first contract, writing service standards, renewal mechanisms, and pricing-adjustment bases clearly — rather than spending all leverage at once.

## Three Actions for Frontline Key-Account Salespeople

First, write proposals in the customer's financial language. This contract anchors to 1.84% of 2025 operating revenue. If your proposal includes verifiable numbers—which business line's revenue or cost it affects—finance and executives can approve more easily. Frame improvements as an investment case.

Second, treat annual signing as the starting point of a renewal campaign, not the end of a signing. On the day of signing, begin accumulating evidence for next year's renewal: service delivery, changes in the customer's business, new demand signals. If you wait until the customer sends a renewal invitation to start preparing, you will have nothing left to concede at the table except price.

Third, beware of treating this kind of announcement as a universal signal. Every large-account purchase has its own budget cycle, decision chain, and alternatives. Seeing someone else sign a 66.5603 million yuan per year contract should send you back to your own account list to check where each deal sits on the dual-perspective funnel and what evidence is missing — not to copy a quotation template.

## A Compact Comparison: Selling Equipment vs. Selling Services

| Dimension | Selling equipment | Selling services |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing unit | One-time purchase | Annual price, e.g., 66.5603 million yuan per year |
| Customer rationale | Asset acquisition | Operating investment |
| Value language | Product features, discounts, asset return | Investment case, profit improvement, payback period |
| Proof cycle | Once at closing | Re-proven at each annual renewal |
| Risk profile for buyer | Capital tied up in an asset | Risk split into annual slices |

## Actionable Checklist

- Identify the customer's revenue or cost line your deal touches, and express it as a verifiable ratio or amount where possible.
- Map every role in the decision chain: who proposes, who reviews, who approves, who can block.
- Use PRTABCD to record what is known, unknown, the source, and the next question for each item.
- Compare your sales stage against the customer's procurement stage before pushing forward.
- Write service standards, acceptance methods, renewal mechanisms, and pricing-adjustment bases into the first contract.
- Prepare your own BATNA: executable alternatives, bottom lines, authority limits, interests, and records.
- Start renewal evidence collection on signing day, not at expiry.
- Estimate the deal's weight in the customer's business using a ratio similar to the 1.84% example, and let that guide resource allocation and contact level.
- Avoid copying another company's quotation structure without checking your own deal's position and missing evidence.

**Q3: How should a supplier respond when the customer requires annual signing?**

A3: Annual signing is a design that preserves the customer's alternatives and annual bargaining power; it need not be seen as distrust. The supplier should write service standards, acceptance methods, renewal mechanisms, and pricing-adjustment bases clearly into the first contract, and continuously accumulate value evidence during performance so that each renewal negotiation has facts to discuss rather than being prepared at the last minute.

**Q4: How is BATNA used in this kind of long-term contract negotiation?**

A4: BATNA is the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best fallback action available when the parties cannot agree. In this case, the customer's alternatives include switching suppliers or adjusting the proportion of self-built capacity. The supplier should also prepare its own executable alternatives, bottom lines, authority limits, interests, and records, and be clear about which conditions can be conceded and which should only be conceded in exchange.

**Q5: How is PRTABCD used when following this kind of large deal?**

A5: PRTABCD uses seven decision-information categories to find intelligence blind spots: P past, R needs, T timing, A embraceers, B budget, C competitors, D decision chain. Record knowns, unknowns, sources, next questions. D reminds you that 66.5603 million yuan per year involves multiple approvers.

**Q6: What does the 1.84% revenue share in this kind of announcement suggest to salespeople?**

A6: It hints at the purchase's scale and approval level inside the customer. An expense that is neither trivial nor dominant often requires cross-department justification but can still move forward — a typical mid-sized large deal. When assessing your own customers, you can use a similar ratio to estimate the project's weight in their business, and thereby decide how many resources to invest and at what level to communicate.

**Q7: What changes are required when moving from selling equipment to selling services?**

A7: Selling equipment discusses one-time purchase and asset return; selling services discusses annual operating investment and continuous output. Sales must change the proposal from a product quotation to an investment case, using financial language such as profit improvement and payback period, while building an annual value review mechanism, because a service contract must re-prove its worth every year.

**Q8: What is the most common mistake when seeing a peer sign a large deal?**

A8: The common mistake is copying results without process: imitating quotation structure or service packaging without checking your customer's seven procurement stages and five sales stages. Return to your opportunity list, verify missing evidence and uncovered decision-chain people, then act.
