When a hot market is already occupied by stronger players, the hardest question for a key-account sales team is not how to compete head-on. It is where to begin. Which customer, which project, and which position will actually pay for your first order?

A public report on one company's smart-driving business offers a useful case. Early high-level projects were largely claimed by leading suppliers through an NVIDIA ecosystem. The company chose a differentiated entry: integrated units and mid-tier L2+ projects, accumulating mass-production experience and customers. A representative said the priority was solid deliverable business, not following the crowd.

This is not a posture. It is a complete entry decision: assess the landscape, give up the crowded position, choose something you can deliver, and convert delivery into accumulated credibility. Key-account sales teams facing a similar situation can work through the same decision sequence.

## Assess the Landscape Before Entering

Most sales teams chase hot markets: if customers discuss high-level smart driving, all resources go there. The case did the opposite: it assessed first, then chose. The report states the company decided after evaluation, acknowledging that on a track locked by competitors' ecosystem, a latecomer's win probability and bargaining room are small.

In the context of PSS5, this step corresponds to a front-end action of value capability: before translating a product into customer benefits, judge which customer group and which type of project can form a real value difference based on your capabilities. If the position is wrong, no amount of polished messaging will save it.

For sales leaders, the recommendation is to turn the assessment into a checkable list:

- Which sub-segments have competitors with ecosystem lock-in?
- How high is the customer's cost of switching suppliers?
- Where is your mass-production record or delivery evidence?
- What is the customer's most painful problem right now?

If three of these four answers do not favor you, consider changing your entry point rather than doubling down on the same position.

## Turn Differentiated Entry into Verifiable Customer Benefits

By choosing integrated units and mid-tier L2+, the company was effectively sending a verifiable value proposition: in this position, I have delivery capability and can help you get the vehicle into mass production. Note the report's wording: gradually accumulating mass-production experience and a customer base. This shows that value is not built on promises but rolled out through delivery records, one by one.

In PSS5, value capability requires sales to express how a solution improves the customer's business in profit language: how much revenue can be increased or protected, where the certainty lies, and over what time frame it will be realized. For an automaker customer, a mid-tier smart-driving supplier's mass-production experience maps directly to project delay risk and ramp-up cost. Those are the numbers decision-makers will listen to.

The entry point must land on the customer's current decision task. In the seven-stage customer purchasing process, step two is judging value, and step three is confirming that a solution exists. Your entry point must make the customer think of you first at the "confirming a solution" step, not only be compared on the high-level project you are pushing hard.

A value proposition needs a verifiable evidence form: mass-production records, delivery cases, and validation reports are all things the customer can check during the selection stage. Differentiation without evidence is just an adjective.

On rhythm, make it solid first, then expand: first build a base in a sub-segment you can win, then use a migration strategy to supplement, upgrade, and expand customer share, rather than opening a full-front war from the start.

## Use Process Management and Negotiation Preparation to Hold the Ground

After a differentiated entry, the real test is the pace of advancement. PSS5 places key-account sales process on a dual-perspective funnel composed of five sales stages and seven customer purchasing stages. The seller has five stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. The buyer has seven decision tasks, from discovering a problem to committing to a contract. Progress is judged by customer behavioral evidence, not visit counts or the salesperson's own feelings.

The journey from integrated units to global delivery is essentially a two-way funnel continuously validated by customer behavior: whatever stage the customer is in, your actions serve that stage's decision task, avoiding skipping ahead.

Negotiation also requires laying chips at entry. Your differentiated position is negotiating power: delivery records others cannot quickly match shift price negotiations. If anyone can replace your entry point, no later BATNA preparation will tip the scales. Entry choice and negotiation outcome are on the same causal line.

### A Compact Comparison: Crowded Position vs. Differentiated Entry

| Dimension | Crowded position (ecosystem-locked) | Differentiated entry (deliverable sub-segment) |
|---|---|---|
| Win probability | Very low for latecomers | Higher, based on your own delivery evidence |
| Bargaining room | Minimal | Shaped by hard-to-replicate delivery records |
| Evidence needed | Often none yet | Mass-production records, delivery cases, validation reports |
| Negotiation leverage | Weak; alternatives favor the other side | Stronger; your position itself is a chip |
| Expansion path | Hard to start | Build a base, then migrate, supplement, upgrade |

## Practical Actions for Key-Account Sales Teams

Review key accounts this week: on the 7x5 purchasing-sales map, where are you, and what customer behaviors prove it? Check your value proposition: is it in benefit language decision-makers accept, backed by evidence? Examine your entry point: if locked out by a competitor's ecosystem, evaluate a sub-segment where you can win first, deliver, then expand.

## Actionable Checklist

- Map each key account on the 7x5 coordinate and cite customer behavioral evidence for the position.
- List which sub-segments have competitors with ecosystem lock-in.
- Estimate the customer's switching cost.
- Identify your own delivery evidence: mass-production records, delivery cases, validation reports.
- Name the customer's most painful current problem.
- If three of the four assessment answers do not favor you, consider changing the entry point.
- Translate your value proposition into profit language: revenue increased or protected, certainty, time frame.
- Confirm your entry point lands on the customer's "confirming a solution" decision task.
- Prepare negotiation chips at the entry stage, not after the proposal.
- After a successful delivery, plan a migration strategy: supplement, upgrade, expand share.

**Q6: After a differentiated entry, how do you keep the value proposition from staying at the slogan level?**

Differentiation without evidence is just an adjective, and customers will filter it out when comparing suppliers.

If the first cooperation was not successful, do not mechanically expand; review the reasons, repair trust, and then decide the next pace.
