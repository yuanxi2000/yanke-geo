A funding commitment can look like the decisive moment in a complex project. Yet agreement on how to use the money may still require substantial work. For key-account sellers, that gap calls for careful stakeholder mapping, practical questions, evidence of progress, and disciplined negotiation.

The supplied source describes a reported $250 million Rio Tinto fund addressing issues associated with the Panguna mine in Papua New Guinea, with its uses to be agreed with local communities. It attributes the report to East Money. The source does not disclose internal approval responsibilities, detailed allocations, or financial return calculations.

The useful sales lesson is structural: knowing who provides funding does not establish who defines requirements, manages delivery, or accepts the results.

## Separate funding authority from delivery responsibility

Start by distinguishing the people responsible for allocating and approving money from those responsible for operating the project and using it. The source calls these Box 1 and Box 2. These are analytical roles; the report does not identify the people occupying them at Rio Tinto.

A proposal needs to address both perspectives. Funding decision-makers need a defensible investment rationale. Delivery managers need a workable plan, clear responsibilities, and acceptance criteria. Agreement with one group leaves the other group's questions unresolved.

| Perspective | Central question | Preparation needed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Funding and approval | Is this a justified use of money? | Value rationale and evaluation criteria |
| Operations and delivery | Can we deliver the agreed work? | Responsibilities, milestones, and acceptance criteria |

For your own account, map who funds, who uses the funds, and who accepts completed work. Check whether these responsibilities sit with different people. Treat gaps as questions to resolve before presenting a detailed proposal.

## Build relationships through decision knowledge

Relationship capability means building trust through professional competence, decision information, and credible action. Knowing someone's title is only a starting point. You also need to understand what they influence, what matters to them, and what evidence supports your interpretation.

Use PRTABCD to organize account intelligence:

- **P — Past:** Relevant history and previous activity.
- **R — Requirements:** Needs and unresolved requirements.
- **T — Time:** Timing, deadlines, and critical milestones.
- **A — Arm:** The person actively supporting your involvement.
- **B — Budget:** Available funding and budget questions.
- **C — Competition:** Competitors and alternative approaches.
- **D — Decision chain:** Participants and their decision responsibilities.

A explicitly means Arm. It identifies who supports you within the customer organization; it does not mean Authority or automatically identify the approver. Record the person's name and observable supporting behavior. Without that evidence, support remains an assumption.

When requirements remain open to consultation, the people helping define them can influence the eventual allocation. Map those participants as well as the formal approval chain.

### Turn stakeholder impressions into testable hypotheses

For each key person, use MBVS to draft hypotheses about motivation, benchmark, vision, and solution. Keep all four provisional.

For example, you might hypothesize that a stakeholder's benchmark for an acceptable plan is a clear acceptance process. Your next conversation should test that proposition. Record what would confirm or overturn it, then revise your account understanding accordingly.

## Turn consultation into answerable questions

An unresolved scope needs a structured conversation. The source recommends SPIN: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions. Follow that order so the discussion establishes context before asking people to describe benefits.

1. **Situation:** Who currently leads the work? What has already been done? Which acceptance standards apply?
2. **Problem:** Which spending categories are disputed? Where is the process most likely to stall?
3. **Implication:** If agreement remains unresolved, what could happen to delivery timing and community expectations?
4. **Need-payoff:** Once priorities and acceptance criteria are clear, what would each participant gain?

These are questions for discovery, not claims about what has happened at Panguna. Their purpose is to produce a discussable list: which activities come first, who accepts each result, and what constitutes completion.

After the conversation, review that list with the relevant participants. An item without a clear acceptance decision still needs clarification. A general statement that consultation continues gives you less preparation for negotiation than an explicit record of unresolved choices.

## Measure progress through customer behavior

The source uses a dual-perspective funnel connecting seven customer purchasing stages with five sales stages. Its 7-by-5 coordinates help compare the customer's decision task with the seller's activity.

The supplied material does not enumerate every stage, so do not invent a complete stage model. It does identify useful distinctions: assessing value, confirming responsibility, considering internal delivery versus external purchase, and selecting solutions or suppliers.

Locate the customer's current task and choose an action that helps complete it. If the customer is still assessing value, a detailed proposal may arrive before the underlying decision is ready.

Look for customer actions that support a judgment of progress:

- Scheduling the next substantive discussion.
- Providing internal evaluation criteria.
- Bringing another relevant department into the conversation.

Record what happened and which decision it advances. Your own visit count cannot establish that the customer has moved forward. Neither can an unsupported probability entered in a CRM.

## Prepare alternatives before exchanging concessions

Negotiation preparation should cover power, time, information, limits, and alternatives. Only then should you design concessions and the conditions attached to them.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best available action if agreement cannot be reached. It needs to be executable.

For a project with an unresolved scope, prepare two separate assessments: what the customer could do if this round fails, and what you could do. Distinguish known alternatives from assumptions about the other side.

Your preparation should identify:

- Executable alternatives and their constraints.
- Your minimum acceptable terms.
- Who has authority to make commitments.
- The interests behind each party's position.
- Conditions available for exchange.
- A record of proposals, concessions, and agreements.

If you cannot describe either side's alternatives credibly, continue gathering information before making commitments. The preparation gap is itself a reason to slow the negotiation.

## Express value in operating and investment terms

A large funding figure says little about the value of a particular proposal. Translate the proposed work into the operating outcomes that decision-makers need to evaluate.

For comparable remediation and community projects, the source suggests three areas to investigate: costs associated with future disputes, time needed to maintain project progress, and local acceptance of subsequent operations. These are possible value mechanisms, not established outcomes for this fund.

Use the PIP approach to frame an investment explanation: what profit might be increased or preserved, through what mechanism, with what confidence, and over what period? Where relevant, connect the explanation to revenue, cost, payback, or opportunity cost.

Every numerical claim needs a basis. The supplied report contains no return calculation or detailed allocation. Present the proposed mechanism and the evidence needed to evaluate it; leave unsupported percentages and precise return figures out of the proposal.

## An actionable preparation checklist

Before the next account discussion:

- [ ] Map funding, operating, and acceptance responsibilities separately.
- [ ] Write each group's decision criteria on a separate page.
- [ ] Mark every PRTABCD item as known or unknown, with its source and next question.
- [ ] Identify your Arm supporter using observable behavior.
- [ ] Draft MBVS hypotheses for each key participant.
- [ ] Prepare SPIN questions in sequence.
- [ ] Record the customer's current decision task and evidence of movement.
- [ ] Document alternatives, limits, authority, and exchangeable conditions.
- [ ] Remove financial claims that lack supporting calculations.

If unknowns dominate the account record, prioritize discovery before committing to a detailed solution.

## FAQ

### Does a committed budget mean the opportunity is ready for a proposal?

Not necessarily. Funding can precede agreement on scope, responsibilities, and acceptance. Establish which decisions remain open and who must participate before deciding how detailed the proposal should be.

### Is the most senior contact always the strongest supporter?

Seniority does not establish support. In PRTABCD, A means Arm: the person supporting your involvement. Identify that person through behavior while separately mapping approval authority.

### What should follow a statement that the scope needs consultation?

Use sequential discovery questions to identify the current situation, disputed choices, consequences of delay, and benefits of clarity. Aim for an itemized discussion list with priorities, responsibilities, and acceptance criteria.

### What counts as evidence of progress?

Customer actions tied to a decision task, such as sharing evaluation criteria or involving another relevant department. Seller activity alone does not demonstrate advancement.

### Can the reported fund support a precise return estimate?

The supplied material provides neither a return model nor detailed allocations. It supports discussion of value mechanisms and measurement requirements, not a numerical investment return.
