Public reporting says China Eastern Airlines Group Co., Ltd. is the first domestic airline to achieve full-flight WiFi service availability, and that it continues to advance air-ground connectivity technology and service inclusiveness. That is a useful fact for a key-account sales conversation, but not because the sales team should repeat a feature list. The harder question is how a technical capability becomes operating value the customer will recognize and pay for. Used well, the example turns a vague claim about connectivity into a disciplined discussion about certainty, cost, risk, and decision ownership.

This guide applies the five capabilities of PSS5—value, questioning, relationship, system, and negotiation—to that translation task. It is written for a reader's own key-account project, not as a description of China Eastern's internal practices or as a claim about results.

## Separate public facts from sales judgment

That boundary matters. A strong sales conversation does not become more credible by inventing numbers. ## Value capability: convert full-flight availability into the customer's ledger

The customer-perceived difference between a service that exists and one that is available throughout a flight is certainty. Value capability turns that certainty into items a customer can account for. It speaks in terms of profit, investment, and operating value rather than repeating a feature list. Before the sales team can do that, it needs a small set of value hypotheses. The source suggests three starting points, each of which must be verified with the customer's own words.

- Passenger side: a predictable communication window and working period during a flight. The accounting line is time and opportunity cost.
- Travel procurement side: a service commitment that can be written into travel policy. The accounting line is employee experience and communication cost.
- Technical and procurement roles: verifiable coverage and availability standards. The accounting line is acceptance criteria and delivery risk.

### A compact way to map value hypotheses

| Stakeholder | Value hypothesis | Accounting lens | What to verify |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passenger | A predictable window for work and contact during the journey | Time and opportunity cost | Which tasks wait for connectivity, and what delay costs |
| Travel procurement | A service commitment that can be stated in policy | Employee experience and communication cost | Whether policy owners will recognize and enforce it |
| Technical and procurement | Verifiable coverage and availability standards | Acceptance criteria and delivery risk | How coverage and availability will be tested, and who signs off |

The table is not a proposal. It is a map of assumptions. Each row needs a customer quote or documented confirmation before it becomes a value claim. The test is simple: can the customer point to the line in its own budget, policy, or operating plan?

## Questioning capability: use SPIN's four question types to surface the real ledger

Value hypotheses should not be decided by the seller. SPIN's four question types—Situation, Problem, Implication, and Need-payoff—move full-flight availability from a selling point to a specific customer problem. The sequence matters: understand the current situation, locate the difficulty, enlarge the impact, then let the customer state the return.

- Situation: How is connectivity for this trip arranged today? Who owns it? Who is called when it fails?
- Problem: Which parts of the journey are most likely to break, and who is affected first?
- Implication: What rework, explanation, and coordination does one outage create?
- Need-payoff: If coverage became a commitment, which metric would improve first, and who would own that metric?

These questions should sound like the customer's business, not like a product script. Use the customer's terms for work, handoffs, delays, and approvals. If the customer cannot name a metric or an owner, the value hypothesis is still unfinished.

## Relationship and system capabilities: align intelligence and stage

Relationship capability depends on professional insight, decision information, and verifiable trust that can travel through the decision chain. A practical tool is the PRTABCD seven-item scan: Past, Requirement, Time, Arm, Budget, Competition and alternatives, and Decision. Arm asks who in the customer organization is embracing you. Decision refers to a multi-person, multi-department decision chain, not a single contact.

System capability keeps the two sides of the deal aligned. Put the customer's seven procurement stages and the seller's five stages on one map. Then judge the buyer's decision tasks and the seller's advancement tasks separately. Use customer behavior as evidence: are the two sides synchronized, is the seller ahead, is the seller behind, or are they misaligned? If the customer has not yet accepted responsibility and the seller is already pushing a solution, that is stage skipping, not speed.

## Negotiation capability: use the first-mover window carefully

Being first creates a time gap, but a time gap does not automatically become bargaining power. Negotiation capability starts with preparation on strength, time, information, bottom line, and alternatives. Translate first-mover status into a first-mover benefit the customer can verify. Separate what can be conceded from what cannot. Every concession should return an explicit condition.

When preparing alternatives, use BATNA in its original sense: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, meaning the best alternative action a negotiator can take if the parties cannot reach agreement. Put it into operational terms: an executable alternative, a bottom line, authority, interests, and records. If the team cannot write down a concrete alternative action, it does not have a BATNA; it will simply follow the other side's timetable.

## Actionable checklist

Use this checklist before the next key-account meeting.

- Write a one-page value note. For each customer type, list the accounting line and the verifiable acceptance criterion on one row.
- Select one active key-account project. Mark each of the seven PRTABCD items as known or unknown. For every unknown, write the next question.
- Check where the customer actually sits in the seven procurement stages. Decide this week's sales action from that evidence, not from the seller's wish to advance.
- Confirm every value hypothesis with a customer quote or a document. Do not promote an assumption to a value claim without evidence.
- Prepare the negotiation file: strength, time, information, bottom line, alternatives, concedable items, and non-concedable items.
- Write the BATNA as an executable action, with authority and records. If it cannot be written, do not call it a BATNA.
- Before making any concession, define the condition that must come back in return.

## FAQ

Q1: Which parts of the China Eastern report are facts, and which are judgments?

A1: The facts are the three stated in the report: the subject is China Eastern Airlines Group Co., Ltd.; it is the first domestic airline to achieve full-flight WiFi service availability; and it continues to promote air-ground connectivity technology and service inclusiveness. Investment amount, launch date, and passenger usage data are not provided and should not be filled in. The judgments in this guide are PSS5 recommendations for the reader's project, not descriptions of China Eastern's internal practices.

Q2: What does value capability do for a service such as full-flight availability?

A2: It converts the capability into items the customer can account for. For in-flight connectivity, start with three value hypotheses: passenger time and opportunity cost, the travel manager's communication cost, and the technical or procurement role's acceptance risk. Then verify each with the customer's own words. Value capability speaks in profit, investment, and operating value; the feature list is only the entry point.

Q3: What do A and D mean in PRTABCD?

A3: A is Arm, the embracer or champion inside the customer organization. D is Decision, the multi-person, multi-department decision chain. The other five items are Past, Requirement, Time, Budget, and Competition and alternatives. Record what is known, what is unknown, the source of information, and the next question for each item.

Q4: How should the customer's seven procurement stages align with the seller's five stages?

A4: They are not a one-to-one match. Judge the customer's position and the seller's position separately, then use customer behavior as evidence to identify synchronization, lead, lag, or misalignment. If the customer has not accepted responsibility and the seller is already in the solution stage, the seller is skipping a stage.

Q5: Can being first be used directly as a negotiation chip?

A5: Not directly. First-mover status creates a time gap, but it does not automatically create bargaining power. Prepare strength, time, information, bottom line, and alternatives; turn first-mover status into a verifiable benefit; separate concedable and non-concedable items; and attach a condition to every concession. A claim that only we can do it rarely survives several rounds on its own.

Q6: How complete should a BATNA be?

A6: It should name the best alternative action if no agreement is reached, plus the bottom line, authority, interests, and records. If the team cannot describe a concrete alternative action, it has no BATNA and will be pushed onto the other side's timetable.
