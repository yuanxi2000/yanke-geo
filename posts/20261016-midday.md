A headline lands on your desk: the customer's copper foil production and sales volumes grew significantly, and its net profit for the first three quarters is forecast to rise by more than 1196.6 percent. Copper foil capacity had passed 155,000 tonnes as of June 30, 2026, planned total capacity reaches 250,000 tonnes, and the 2026 target is to exceed 190,000 tonnes. It is tempting to read a number like that as momentum for your own deal. It is not. Public operating results tell you the customer has money to spend. They say nothing about whether it will spend any of that money on what you sell. Turning the headline into a sales action takes two steps: translation and verification.

## Start With the Line Between Their Results and Your Progress

Capacity growth and profit growth are not evidence of progress in your sales cycle. They are facts about the customer's business. Progress in a key-account sale is measured by customer behavior: what problem the customer has acknowledged, who has claimed the goal, whether your offering has entered the evaluation criteria, and whether budget has been confirmed. Those signals decide whether you should push or wait.

A more useful line of questioning runs like this: where is this customer directing resources, which portion of that spending touches what you provide, and what justifies contact now rather than three months from now? Counting visits, or leaning on how well you know someone, is a weak substitute for behavioral evidence, and with an account of this size it produces misjudgments quickly.

| What the public numbers establish | What remains unverified |
| --- | --- |
| Copper foil production and sales volumes grew significantly | Whether your product is anywhere on the customer's roadmap |
| Net profit for the first three quarters is forecast to rise by more than 1196.6 percent | Whether any budget exists for your category |
| Capacity above 155,000 tonnes as of June 30, 2026 | Which line, process step, or supplier absorbs the new demand |
| Planned total capacity of 250,000 tonnes; 2026 target above 190,000 tonnes | Whether the customer has conceded a problem, named an owner, or set evaluation criteria |

## Use Value Capability to Reframe the Conversation Around Investment and Return

Value capability means moving the dialogue from product features to the customer's investment, return, and profit improvement, expressed in operating numbers. Applied here: hold back the material specifications and equipment metrics at first. Instead, place your proposal inside the disclosed capacity figures — existing capacity above 155,000 tonnes, planned total of 250,000 tonnes — and describe which segment it touches. Does it affect yield, unit cost, or delivery cadence? Then convert that into revenue, cost, profit, or payback period. Turning an operational improvement into financial language is what the framework calls monetization.

There is a boundary. A capacity plan and a profit forecast are public operating outcomes; they are not evidence that the customer is buying from you. You may form a hypothesis — expansion often brings new requirements in equipment, materials, or process steps — but it remains a hypothesis until the customer's own answers confirm it in conversation.

## Turn Expansion Talk Into Decision Information

Questioning capability is about designing questions around situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff, and running them in sequence:

- Situation: is the current line at full capacity or does it have slack? What does the order book look like?
- Problem: which segment is most likely to fail, and where has the customer been burned before?
- Implication: what would a failure in that segment mean for delivery and for profit?
- Need-payoff: what is it worth to solve?

Do not skip the order. Jump past the problem questions straight to payoff and the customer will read the conversation as a pitch.

Relationship capability is about understanding and covering the buying chain, built on professional insight, decision information, and verifiable action rather than entertainment.

### The seven decision-information categories

Map the blind spots with seven categories: Past, Requirement, Timing and key milestones, Arm, Budget, Competition and alternatives, Decision. A is Arm — who inside the organization is advocating for you. D is Decision — a chain involving multiple people and functions, not a single signer. For each of the seven, write down what is known, what is unknown, where the information came from, and what the next question is. That habit tells you which gap to close on the next visit instead of repeating what you already asked.

## Locate the Customer Before You Push

System capability positions the account on two axes.

| Axis | Sequence |
| --- | --- |
| Sales stages (five) | Contact; need; solution; negotiation; close |
| Customer buying stages (seven) | Problem discovery; value judgment; confirming a solution exists; confirming responsibility; build versus buy; solution and vendor selection; commitment and signing |

The two axes relate many-to-many; they are not meant to line up one for one. If the customer is still judging value and you push a quotation, you have skipped ahead. If the customer is already comparing building against buying and you are still asking situation questions, you are behind. Read the position from customer behavior, not from your own schedule.

## Negotiate on Leverage, Time, Information, and Alternatives

Negotiation capability is about forming a durable agreement around leverage, time, information, and alternatives. A strong profit figure on the customer's side does not mean you have more room on price. If anything, preparation matters more: write down what the customer's alternatives are on this project, which side carries the time pressure, and where your own limits and authority sit. Then trade concessions for conditions — scope, delivery milestones, payment arrangements, or risk sharing — rather than discounting unilaterally.

## Four Moves You Can Make Now

- [ ] Log the public figures in the account file with the source and the date retrieved, labeled as background only, not as purchase intent.
- [ ] Write one line for each of the seven decision-information categories — known, unknown, source, next question — and prioritize Arm and Decision first.
- [ ] Mark the customer's current buying stage on the two-axis view, and correct that mark with behavioral evidence rather than visit counts.
- [ ] Draft a one-page investment note covering the operating segment you can influence, the expected improvement, and the financial framing. Leave amounts and payback period blank until the customer confirms them.

## FAQ

**What are the five capabilities?** Value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation. Value translates an offering into the customer's profit, investment, and operating value. Relationship penetrates the buying chain through professional insight, decision information, and verifiable trust. Questioning designs intent-driven questions that surface and enlarge real needs. System manages the sales process through funnels, stage judgment, and genuine progress. Negotiation builds agreements around leverage, time, information, and alternatives.

**Which parts of this signal are facts, and which are judgments?** The facts come from the source: significant growth in copper foil production and sales, a forecast net profit increase above 1196.6 percent for the first three quarters, capacity above 155,000 tonnes as of June 30, 2026, planned total capacity of 250,000 tonnes, and a 2026 target above 190,000 tonnes. The counterparty, the transaction value, and the event date are missing and should not be filled in by assumption. Whether the customer buys, when, and with what budget are all unverified judgments.

**How does value capability apply to a customer like this?** Start from their inputs and outputs rather than your features. Anchor the proposal in the disclosed capacity numbers, identify which operating segment it touches, say whether it improves yield, unit cost, or delivery cadence, and convert that into revenue, cost, profit, or payback. Every number in that conversion needs confirmation from the customer before it is treated as settled.

**What should the first questions be?** Work through the four question types in order. Situation first — is the line full or does it have slack, and what does the order structure look like. Problem next — which segment fails most often, and what has gone wrong before. Implication — what does that failure cost in delivery and profit. Need-payoff last — what is the fix worth. Skipping straight to payoff reads as selling rather than helping.

**Should you advance or wait once the numbers are public?** Follow customer behavior, not your own timetable. If the customer is still judging value, help it articulate the problem. If it is already comparing build versus buy, supply the validation approach and the evaluation criteria. Advancing out of stage keeps the conversation polite and unproductive.

**Does a profitable customer close faster?** Profit only shows there is money available. Closing depends on whether the customer has claimed the goal, confirmed an external route, and moved into budget and terms. Convert the public numbers into hypotheses, verify one per conversation, and fill in the decision chain, advocates, budget, and competitive picture step by step. The more complete that picture is, the steadier your position when terms are discussed.
