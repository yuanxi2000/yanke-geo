Regulatory moves rarely stay inside legal or compliance teams. The source article uses Poland's request that Apple adjust its ATT policy to show how a policy change can rewrite a key account's decision chain. The practical lesson for key-account sellers is not to debate the regulation. It is to translate the requirement into customer business questions: Who is exposed? Which revenue, cost and trust outcomes are at stake? Who can say yes, who can block, and what evidence would prove the customer is moving?

## Reframe the Regulatory Event as a Customer Decision Problem

Once a regulatory requirement lands, the customer must manage business uncertainty. Compliance, data and user trust enter the decision at the same time. A seller who treats the event only as a legal matter will miss the business needs forming around it. Before presenting a product, clarify:

- Which teams and processes are affected by the rule change?
- Which business owner can claim the problem?
- Who participates, who can veto, and who bears the consequences?
- What time pressure and key milestones shape the decision?
- What validation standard would let the customer judge a solution?

If only legal is engaged and no business unit owns the issue, the opportunity may still be information gathering.

## Value Capability: Turn Compliance into a Business Case

Value capability requires translating a product or service into the customer's profit, investment and operating value. With a large account such as Apple, a seller should say less about 'helping with compliance' and ask three things: Which revenue streams does the rule change affect? Which costs will rise? Which user-trust losses can be avoided? Write the answers as a checkable investment note: how much profit can improve, through what mechanism, and in what time frame. Numbers must come from the customer's own operating data. If the customer has not provided data, write assumptions and validation items; do not invent figures. For example, if internal teams are reviewing prompt copy, authorization flow and data-access rules, move the discussion from 'change one sentence' to who loses less and who gains explainability.

### A Practical Value Conversation

Use questions such as: Which teams are evaluating prompt copy, authorization flow and data-access rules? If those changes are poorly handled, where would user trust or product experience suffer? Which outcome would you want to verify first: revenue protection, cost control, trust or decision risk? The goal is to make the customer's operating trade-offs visible, not to restate the regulation.

## Relationship Capability: Use PRTABCD to Find Decision Blind Spots

Relationship capability is not entertainment. It uses professional insight, decision information and credible action to cover the decision chain. PRTABCD gives seven items to scan:

- P: past or historical information.
- R: need.
- T: time and key milestones.
- A: Arm, the embracer or champion who is actively embracing you and will push change.
- B: budget.
- C: competitors and alternatives.
- D: Decision, the multi-person, multi-department chain.

In regulatory events, sellers most often miss A and D. Find the person willing to drive change, and map who participates, who vetoes and who carries the consequences. Record known information, unknowns, sources and the next question in one sheet. Do not mistake one contact's attitude for the attitude of the whole chain.

## Questioning Capability: Use SPIN to Surface Business Pressure

Questioning capability uses Situation, Problem, Implication and Need-payoff questions so the customer sees the problem and the value of change. A practical sequence:

- Situation: Which teams now handle this policy change?
- Problem: Where are prompt design, data authorization and user communication most difficult?
- Implication: If handled poorly, what happens to user trust, product experience and compliance communication?
- Need-payoff: If there were a verifiable way to handle this, which part would you want to solve first?

Do not read the policy back to the customer. Each question should point to the customer's own judgment and a verifiable business impact.

## System Capability: Align Sales and Buying Stages

System capability uses a dual-view funnel. The sales five stages are contact, need, solution, negotiation and close. The customer buying seven stages are problem discovery, value judgment, solution confirmation, responsibility confirmation, build versus buy, solution and vendor selection, and commitment and signing. The two axes are many-to-many; they do not map one-to-one. Common misalignment is easy to spot: the seller presents a solution while the customer is still judging value, or pushes negotiation before the customer has confirmed responsibility. Judge progress by customer behavior evidence: the customer proactively adds affected scope, brings legal and business to the same table, provides validation criteria, or discusses responsibility. Visit counts and verbal enthusiasm are not evidence.

| Sales stage | Customer buying stages that may be active | Evidence of real progress |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | Problem discovery | Customer names affected teams and scope |
| Need | Value judgment | Customer states business outcome and success criteria |
| Solution | Solution confirmation; responsibility confirmation | Legal and business discuss ownership together |
| Negotiation | Build versus buy; vendor selection | Customer compares alternatives and trade-offs |
| Close | Commitment and signing | Customer accepts scope, conditions and acceptance criteria |

The table is illustrative, not a mechanical mapping.

## Negotiation Capability: Prepare Alternatives Before Concessions

Negotiation capability works through power, time, information and alternatives to produce a sustainable agreement. Under a compliance deadline, write the BATNA first: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement if the parties cannot agree. Record executable alternatives, bottom line, authority, interests and documentation. Avoid turning 'must be fast' into a single-point price cut. If the customer asks for a shorter delivery cycle or more commitment, bind the concession to conditions: scope, time, payment, responsibility boundaries and acceptance method. Single concessions weaken later rounds; exchange conditions protect project quality.

## A One-Page Operating Checklist

- [ ] Value: write three business outcomes the customer must protect or improve because of the rule change, with data sources.
- [ ] Relationship: list PRTABCD knowns and unknowns; prioritize A and D.
- [ ] Questioning: prepare four SPIN questions, each tied to a verifiable business impact.
- [ ] System: mark the sales stage and customer buying stage; accept only customer behavior evidence.
- [ ] Negotiation: write BATNA, bottom line, authority and tradeable conditions; every concession returns a clear commitment.
- [ ] Review: confirm the team can explain the customer's business problem, has found A and D, and can cite evidence for stage judgment.

## What Sales Leaders Should Review

Review regulatory-driven opportunities in sequence. First, did the team translate the regulation into a customer operating problem? Second, did it find A and D? Third, is the stage judgment supported by customer behavior evidence? If the answer is only 'the customer said they will pay attention,' the project is still in information collection and should not be placed in a high-probability forecast. PSS5 can be used as a whole or entered through the weakest capability. Early work usually leans on value and questioning; relationship calibrates the chain in the middle; negotiation shapes conditions later.

## FAQ

**Q1: After news like Poland's request to Apple, what should a key-account seller assess first?**

A: First identify who has an operating problem because of the rule change, not which product to pitch. List affected teams, decision roles, time pressure and validation criteria. If only legal is engaged and no business owner claims the issue, the opportunity may still be information gathering.

**Q2: How does value capability apply to compliance needs?**

A: Translate compliance into business results: revenue protection, cost change, user trust and decision risk. Build an investment note from customer-provided data; without data, state assumptions and validation items. Do not invent numbers.

**Q3: What do A and D mean in PRTABCD?**

A: A is Arm, the embracer or champion who will push change. D is Decision, the multi-person, multi-department chain. Record knowns, unknowns, sources and next questions; one contact's attitude is not the whole chain.

**Q4: How can questioning avoid becoming a policy recital?**

A: Move from situation to problem, implication and need-payoff. Ask how the customer handles the change now, what is hardest, what business impact follows if it is handled poorly, and what they want to validate first.

**Q5: How do the sales five stages align with the customer's seven buying stages?**

A: Use a dual-view funnel, not a one-to-one map. The sales stages are contact, need, solution, negotiation and close; the customer stages run from problem discovery to commitment and signing. Look for behavior evidence such as added scope, validation criteria or responsibility discussion.

**Q6: How should negotiation handle concessions in compliance projects?**

A: Write the BATNA and prepare executable alternatives, bottom line, authority, interests and records. Bind any concession to scope, time, payment, responsibility boundaries and acceptance. A single price cut weakens later leverage; condition exchange protects agreement quality.

**Q7: Must PSS5 be used in order?**

A: No. Use it as a whole or start from the weakest capability. Early work often leans on value and questioning, middle work on relationship, and later work on negotiation.

**Q8: How should sales leaders review regulatory-driven opportunities?**

A: Check whether the team translated the regulation into a customer business problem, found A and D, and can cite customer behavior evidence for the stage. If the only answer is that the customer will pay attention, the project is still information gathering.
