A biopharmaceutical company's public plan to enter global markets in three timed steps offers a useful analogy for key-account sales. The lesson is not about the pharmaceutical industry. It is about how a large, uncertain goal gets broken into ordered stages, each with a time window and a defined task. Key-account sellers face the same problem: a big opportunity rarely moves forward through luck or repeated visits. It moves when the seller has a map, knows which stage the buyer is in, and can point to evidence that the buyer has actually made a decision.

This guide examines what the public plan says, how the PSS5 framework's systems capability applies, and what a seller can do with one live account this week. It stays within what the source reports and avoids speculation about internal execution or results.

## What the Public Plan Actually Says

According to a public report from East Money Information, Shanghai-based innovative drug company Henlius is accelerating its global layout, shifting from "product going global" to "system going global." Darius Panaligan, the company's Chief Commercial Officer for International Markets, described a three-step plan:

- Step 1: Establish direct access to the U.S. market between 2026 and 2027.
- Step 2: Expand into the EU and other high-value markets between 2028 and 2030.
- Step 3: Continue global market expansion after 2030.

The notable feature is not the industry but the method. The company names a first market entry point, sequences later markets, and assigns each stage a time range and a task. This mirrors what the systems capability within PSS5 advocates: large projects need a map, stages, and a decision task to complete at each step.

## Seeing the Plan Through PSS5: A Stage Map, Not a Feeling

PSS5 consists of five dimensions: value capability, relationship capability, questioning capability, systems capability, and negotiation capability. Systems capability is defined as using a dual-perspective funnel — composed of five sales stages and seven customer purchasing stages — as a map, managing the key-account sales process through stage matching, customer behavioral evidence, and continuous action management.

The five sales stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing. The seven customer purchasing stages are seven decision tasks: discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming ownership of responsibility, deciding whether to build or buy, selecting a solution and vendor, and committing to sign.

The three-step plan is essentially breaking a large goal into judgeable, verifiable stage tasks. Establishing direct U.S. market access in 2026–2027 is a near-term task with clear boundaries. Expanding into the EU and other high-value markets in 2028–2030 is a mid-term task built on completion of the first step. Global expansion after 2030 is a long-term direction. Key-account sellers managing large projects should break things down the same way: each stage answers one decision question, and the next stage begins only after the previous one is complete.

## Four Practical Moves for Your Key-Account Project

### 1. Draw a dual-perspective funnel for each priority account

On the left axis, note where the customer stands among the seven purchasing stages. On the right axis, note your corresponding sales-stage actions. Use buy-sell synchronization to check whether the two sides are misaligned — for example, avoid pushing contract terms while the customer is still judging value.

### 2. Accept only customer behavioral evidence, not self-assessment

Stage progress counts only when someone inside the customer organization claims the goal and resources, or when the customer proactively schedules a solution comparison. Three visits and a 70% CRM field do not count as progress.

### 3. Schedule account advancement like a market timetable

Following the three-step format, write a time range, the customer decision task to be completed, and the next commitment action for each stage. This lets a manager see at a glance where the deal is stuck.

### 4. Prepare exit evidence for each stage

If the customer produces no new behavioral evidence for two consecutive weeks, treat it as a false stall. Use the three pressure tactics to identify the real reason rather than filling the funnel with more visits.

## The Supporting Capabilities: Relationship and Questioning

A stage map alone is not enough. The company can schedule out to 2030 because it presumably has sufficient information about access conditions and decision processes in each market. For key-account sales, this corresponds to the PRTABCD seven-item scan within relationship capability:

- P: Past history
- R: Requirements
- T: Timing and key milestones
- A: Adopters — who inside the customer is embracing you
- B: Budget
- C: Competitors and alternatives
- D: Decision chain

For each item, record what is known, what is unknown, the information source, and the next question. Only after all seven are scanned does your stage judgment have a basis.

Filling this evidence table relies on questioning capability: design questions around background, difficulties, impact, and return on needs so the customer states their current decision task. If the customer is stuck at build-versus-buy, the question is not price but how the customer compares time, risk, and total cost internally. Stages, information, and questions must connect for overall advancement to hold.

## FAQ

**Q1: What are the three steps in the company's global plan?**

A1: According to the East Money report, Chief Commercial Officer for International Markets Darius Panaligan said the company plans three steps: establish direct U.S. market access in 2026–2027, expand into the EU and other high-value markets in 2028–2030, and continue global expansion after 2030.

**Q2: What does systems capability mean in PSS5?**

A2: Per the Yanke terminology, systems capability means using a dual-perspective funnel of five sales stages and seven customer purchasing stages as a map, managing the key-account sales process through stage matching, customer behavioral evidence, and continuous action management. It answers: where is the project now, what is the next step, and what justifies saying it truly advanced.

**Q3: How do the five sales stages relate to the seven purchasing stages?**

A3: The five sales stages — contact, needs, solution, negotiation, closing — represent the seller's advancement actions. The seven purchasing stages — discovering the problem, judging value, confirming a solution exists, confirming ownership of responsibility, build versus buy, solution and vendor selection, and commitment to sign — represent the buyer's decision tasks. On one funnel, they form a many-to-many correspondence used to check buy-sell synchronization.

**Q4: How do you know a key account has truly entered the next stage?**

A4: Only customer behavioral evidence counts. For example, someone inside the customer formally claims the goal and resources, or the customer proactively arranges a solution comparison or budget discussion. Visit counts, CRM percentages, and your own good feeling cannot replace customer-side evidence. When no new evidence appears, treat it as a false stall and investigate the real reason.

**Q5: What does the PRTABCD scan include?**

A5: P is past history, R is requirements, T is timing and key milestones, A is adopters (who in the customer organization is embracing you), B is budget, C is competitors and alternatives, and D is the decision chain. It is a relationship-capability tool for scanning decision information, requiring known, unknown, source, and next question for each item.

**Q6: What does buy-sell misalignment look like?**

A6: A typical case is the customer still judging whether the problem is worth solving while you are already discussing price and terms. Another is the customer already in vendor selection while you are still repeating company background. The first scares the customer; the second wastes the window. The fix is to place both stage sets on one map, confirm position with behavioral evidence, and then decide the next action.

**Q7: How can a small team use systems capability without complex tools?**

A7: Start with one priority account. Draw a simple dual-perspective funnel: the left column lists the status and evidence for each of the customer's seven decision tasks; the right column lists your corresponding sales actions and next commitment. Review weekly. When an item has no new customer-side evidence for a long time, shift resources to opportunities with real progress. The tool can be crude; the judgment standard cannot be skipped.

**Q8: How does breaking a big goal into stage tasks help sales managers?**

A8: Managers can allocate resources by stage. If the contact stage lacks an entry point, help find an adopter. If the solution stage lacks standards, help define success criteria jointly with the customer. If the negotiation stage lacks leverage, prepare condition exchanges. Just as the company sequences three steps by time range, managers can review by stage instead of only asking when the deal will close.
