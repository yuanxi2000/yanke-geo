A robot demonstration answers whether a machine can perform a task. Factory deployment raises a wider question: can the customer integrate, approve, operate, and maintain it within a working production system? For key-account sales teams, that difference changes what counts as progress.

The supplied source describes the Genie G2 robot connecting directly to MES/EAP systems at JCET's Lingang factory. It uses this deployment to examine complex industrial selling. The practical lesson is to track technical feasibility, customer decisions, organizational ownership, and implementation commitments together. A successful demonstration alone cannot establish all four.

## Treat integration as a milestone with boundaries

MES means manufacturing execution system; EAP refers to equipment automation programs. In the semiconductor assembly and testing environment described by the source, these systems coordinate production and equipment activity. Connecting an external robot therefore involves more than demonstrating an isolated function.

The source highlights protocol compatibility, disconnected data, security, operational stability, and failure rollback as integration concerns. These are useful subjects for discovery because they connect product capability to the customer's operating environment.

Reported integration supports a narrower conclusion than a complete commercial victory: the solution has demonstrated some compatibility with the production environment. It does not, by itself, establish final acceptance, procurement status, financial returns, or readiness for deployment across other sites.

The source interprets integration as evidence of progress through feasibility and sourcing decisions. Treat that interpretation as a hypothesis to confirm with customer evidence, especially before forecasting a contract or expansion.

## Align seller activity with buyer decisions

The source describes a dual-perspective funnel: compare five seller stages with a seven-stage customer purchasing process. The purpose is to expose timing mismatches. A seller may be preparing terms while the customer is still deciding whether the problem justifies investment.

The following comparison captures the practical alignments discussed in the source; it is not a complete enumeration of the seven buyer stages.

| Seller stage | Buyer decision to examine | Evidence to seek |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial contact | Recognizing the problem | Customer agreement on the operating gap |
| Needs assessment | Judging value | A financial model the customer has reviewed |
| Solution development | Assigning responsibility | Named owners for interfaces and production access |
| Negotiation | Making commitments | Agreement on scope, timing, payment, and risk |
| Closing | Formalizing commitments | Contract status and allocated implementation resources |

### Ask what the customer has actually done

For each opportunity review, distinguish seller activity from customer behavior. Sending a proposal is a seller action. A customer assigning an IT owner or completing a budget approval is evidence of a buyer decision.

Where evidence is missing, investigate the unresolved task. The source emphasizes responsibility during solution development: someone must coordinate interface access, production downtime, commissioning, and subsequent operations. Technical agreement can coexist with organizational uncertainty.

Record the next customer action, its owner, and the decision it will resolve. This makes progress easier to assess than descriptions such as "the meeting went well."

## Use PRTABCD to investigate decision gaps

The source presents PRTABCD as a seven-part relationship and information tool. Its purpose is to understand how decisions happen, rather than to substitute social contact for evidence.

- **P — Past:** Ask about earlier automation projects, including disappointments. Determine which concerns the current proposal must address. Do not assume a prior failure occurred.
- **R — Requirement:** Clarify integration requirements alongside data security, network isolation, and applicable customer compliance expectations. Identify requirements that could prevent approval.
- **T — Time:** Understand production ramp-up plans and deployment windows. Establish whether commissioning depends on a particular operational milestone.
- **A — Arm:** Identify internal supporters who can coordinate action. Examine support across technical, financial, and frontline roles, including concerns about changes to work.
- **B — Budget:** Determine the funding source, approval route, and payment process. The source distinguishes technical improvement funding from operating expenditure as questions to investigate.
- **C — Competitor:** Understand the alternatives under consideration and their perceived advantages. Include other approaches where the customer considers them relevant.
- **D — Decision:** Map evaluators, approvers, and the final signatory. Learn which measures matter to each participant.

An incomplete answer is a useful finding. For example, technical enthusiasm without an internal coordinator may leave acceptance work unowned. Capture that gap and agree on an action to resolve it.

Relationship strength, in this approach, means access to relevant information and enough trust to coordinate decisions. It should help an internal supporter explain the proposal's value and responsibilities to colleagues.

## Build financial value from agreed assumptions

The source recommends translating technical features into financial consequences: labor costs, throughput, yield, profit improvement, and payback. This makes the proposal relevant to the customer's investment decision.

Start with a baseline the customer recognizes. Then identify the operational change the proposed solution could produce and the assumptions connecting that change to financial value.

A practical value discussion should establish:

- Which operating measure is expected to change.
- What baseline and measurement period will be used.
- How the customer will translate that change into financial terms.
- Which costs and implementation conditions affect the calculation.
- Who will review the assumptions and results.

The source includes illustrative staffing, throughput, and yield figures. They are examples of financial reasoning, not verified results from the JCET deployment. Do not reuse them as case evidence. Where results are unavailable, present an assumption or an unanswered measurement question.

## Negotiate the delivery conditions as well as price

The source treats negotiation as an exchange involving price, scope, timing, payment, and risk. A concession on one dimension should be considered alongside the others.

Before seeking commitment, clarify who provides interface access, when commissioning can occur, what acceptance requires, and which implementation resources each side will allocate. Contract signature and delivery readiness are separate items to verify.

The source also introduces BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Investigate realistic alternatives for both parties, such as retaining the current process or considering another supplier. Do not present hypothetical alternatives as actual options available in this account, or assume integration creates negotiating leverage without evidence.

## Expand through measured transferability

The source describes compatibility with multiple production lines as a possible foundation for expansion. That creates a question to test: which parts of the initial deployment can transfer, and which require additional work?

Begin by documenting operating results from the first deployment. The source suggests utilization and failure measures as possible evidence. Add financial interpretation only where the measurements support it.

Next, examine another line or site against its own interfaces, requirements, owners, and deployment window. Reusing a robot platform does not establish that these conditions are identical.

A value report can support that discussion by separating observed results, assumptions, and remaining uncertainties. Additional services or functions should follow an identified customer need. Expansion remains a new decision to earn.

## Actionable opportunity checklist

Use this checklist before advancing an industrial automation opportunity:

- [ ] The customer has acknowledged a specific operating problem.
- [ ] The value model uses reviewed baselines and explicit assumptions.
- [ ] Integration, security, compatibility, and rollback questions have owners.
- [ ] Interface access and commissioning responsibilities are assigned.
- [ ] PRTABCD gaps have been recorded with follow-up actions.
- [ ] Budget ownership and the approval route are understood.
- [ ] Scope, payment, timing, acceptance, and risk are addressed.
- [ ] Implementation resources have been confirmed.
- [ ] Customer actions support the recorded opportunity stage.
- [ ] Expansion claims distinguish measured results from expectations.

## FAQ

### Does MES/EAP integration mean the deal is won?

It is meaningful technical evidence, but the supplied material does not establish every commercial or operational commitment. Confirm contract status, acceptance, ownership, and implementation resources separately.

### Why use two perspectives on the funnel?

Seller activity can move ahead of buyer readiness. Comparing them helps identify the decision the customer still needs to complete before the next sales action makes sense.

### What should a team do when the customer agrees verbally but takes no action?

Check whether an earlier decision remains unfinished. Ask about the specific obstacle, such as budget approval, technical uncertainty, or internal coordination. Offer relevant assistance and agree on a concrete customer action.

### How is relationship work different from socializing?

The source defines it through decision information, professional insight, trust, and coordinated action. Its practical value appears when people can resolve requirements, explain value internally, and take responsibility for next steps.

### Can one deployment justify a wider rollout?

It can provide evidence for evaluating a rollout. A wider commitment still requires measured value and confirmation that integration, operational responsibilities, and deployment conditions transfer to the next setting.
