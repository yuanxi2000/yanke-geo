A public statement about a new growth curve gives a key-account team a reason to investigate. It does not establish an approved project, available funding, or a buying decision. The practical task is to turn that statement into questions the customer can answer and evidence the sales team can assess.

The supplied source provides one central fact: Jinko Technology has identified its intelligent computing business as a new growth curve. It supplies no associated counterparty, transaction amount, or event date. This guide uses that limited signal to explain a disciplined account approach through five capabilities: value, relationships, questioning, process, and negotiation. Suggested questions and actions below are recommendations, not descriptions of arrangements already made by the company.

## Establish the fact boundary

Start the account record with three categories: verified facts, working interpretations, and unanswered questions. Keep the original wording and its source attached to each fact.

For this case, the growth-curve statement belongs in the first category. The possibility that the customer is evaluating a new business direction is an interpretation. Project approval, budget ownership, decision authority, and purchasing timing remain questions.

Before proposing a solution, identify which missing information matters most to the next decision. A useful account update should explain what the team knows, what remains uncertain, and which customer interaction could resolve that uncertainty. Do not fill an empty field merely to make the opportunity record look complete.

## Translate the signal into business value

Value capability means connecting a proposed product or service to the customer's profit, investment, and operating priorities. Begin with the business result the new activity is expected to improve.

Organize discovery around three areas:

- **Revenue:** What identifiable sources of income could the business generate? Who would be responsible for the revenue outcome?
- **Cost:** Which costs relate to computing capacity, operations, and delivery? Which figures must the customer confirm?
- **Investment:** How will the customer decide whether the investment is worthwhile? What validation period and exit conditions would apply?

These are questions, not assumed features of Jinko Technology's plans. Ask the customer to explain the mechanism behind an expected benefit before presenting capabilities.

### Make the value question specific

Replace a broad question such as whether the customer wants growth with a decision question: “Which operating result must this initiative improve, and what evidence would demonstrate that improvement?” The answer can then inform the scope of a proposal.

## Map relationships through decision information

Relationship capability concerns understanding and covering the customer's decision chain. The PRTABCD scan identifies seven areas where missing information can obstruct that understanding:

- **Past:** What relevant history should the team understand?
- **Requirements:** What does the customer need to accomplish?
- **Time:** What timing information has the customer confirmed?
- **Arm:** Who embraces the proposed work and is willing to support it?
- **Budget:** Who owns the budget, and what is known about its availability?
- **Competition and alternatives:** What competing approaches or substitutes are under consideration?
- **Decision:** Which people and departments participate in the decision chain?

Here, Arm refers to a supporter. Decision describes a chain involving multiple people or departments, not a single decision-maker. Neither role can be inferred from a job title alone.

For every item, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. The public growth signal does not establish which department owns the target or who would provide resources for a pilot. Those require customer confirmation.

## Use questions to develop the buying rationale

Questioning capability uses intentional discovery to uncover needs and help the customer explain their significance. SPIN provides four types of questions that can be used in sequence:

1. **Situation:** Establish the current position. “Which business unit is leading the new growth initiative?”
2. **Problem:** Explore obstacles. “Which operating measures are most often questioned as the work progresses?”
3. **Implication:** Examine consequences. “If that issue remains unresolved, what would it mean for the business?”
4. **Need-payoff:** Invite the customer to describe the benefit of resolution. “What improvement would make a solution worth pursuing?”

Use the sequence to follow the customer's reasoning. It should not become an interrogation checklist. A response may reveal that an earlier assumption needs revisiting.

A further question from the source is useful for clarifying responsibility: “If a pilot falls short of expectations, how would responsibility be allocated?” Asking does not imply that a pilot has been approved. It tests a decision task that may still be unresolved.

## Assess progress from both sides of the purchase

Process capability combines the seller's five stages with the customer's seven purchasing stages. The two perspectives have a many-to-many relationship; they cannot be aligned mechanically, stage by stage.

| Perspective | Stages | What it helps assess |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Seller | Contact; needs; proposal; negotiation; close | The sales team's current work |
| Customer | Discover the problem; assess value; confirm a solution exists; confirm responsibility; decide whether to build or buy; select solution and supplier; commit and sign | The customer's unresolved purchasing decisions |

A statement about a new growth curve may indicate problem discovery or value assessment. It does not establish that the customer is ready to evaluate a proposal.

Look for customer behavior that supports a stage judgment: willingness to share business data, participation by the department that would use the solution, or inclusion of a pilot in an internal meeting. Record what happened and what it demonstrates.

An expression of interest remains an expression of interest without further evidence. Do not turn it into a closing probability or substitute visit counts for progress on the customer's decisions.

## Prepare alternatives before negotiating conditions

Negotiation capability considers power, time, information, and alternatives when preparing an agreement. Start with BATNA: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement, meaning the best available action if agreement cannot be reached.

Preparation should identify executable alternatives, minimum acceptable conditions, negotiating authority, interests, and the record of discussions. Then consider how conditions could be exchanged across:

- Scope and delivery boundaries.
- Pilot boundaries and evaluation arrangements.
- Payment milestones.
- Allocation of risk.

Where the customer is still comparing internal development with external purchasing, discussion should address total cost, time, and risk. Price alone does not answer that decision.

A staged validation approach is one possible recommendation: begin with a limited pilot, then decide whether to expand against success criteria both parties have confirmed. This is a suggested approach from the source, not evidence that Jinko Technology has adopted it.

## Run an account working session

Before the meeting, collect the public facts and the customer's own statements. During the meeting, assign work across the five capabilities: business questions for value, missing decision information for relationships, SPIN questions for discovery, stage evidence for process, and possible condition exchanges for negotiation.

The purpose is to expose the gaps that matter most. A completed worksheet is less useful than a clear assignment to verify a consequential unknown.

Use this actionable checklist:

- [ ] Separate verified facts from interpretations and unanswered questions.
- [ ] Write the operating result the customer needs to clarify.
- [ ] Scan all seven PRTABCD information areas.
- [ ] Attach a source and next question to each material item.
- [ ] Prepare questions covering situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff.
- [ ] Record seller activity separately from customer purchasing decisions.
- [ ] Identify the customer behavior needed to support the next stage judgment.
- [ ] Prepare alternatives and potential exchanges before discussing concessions.
- [ ] Assign each follow-up an owner, a verification question, and the evidence sought.

Close the session with a small set of specific actions. Each should state who will verify what and in which customer interaction.

## FAQ

### Does a growth-curve announcement establish an opportunity?

It establishes a signal worth investigating. The supplied statement does not confirm an approved project, committed budget, decision chain, or purchase schedule. Those must be verified through contact with the customer.

### What should the first value conversation cover?

Ask which business result the initiative should improve. Explore revenue sources, cost changes, investment criteria, responsibility for outcomes, and the evidence the customer would accept. Use those answers to inform later solution discussions.

### How can the team identify the right people?

Use the PRTABCD scan to locate information gaps. Distinguish a supporter from the wider decision chain, and verify budget ownership. For each unknown, specify a source to consult and a next question.

### What counts as meaningful progress?

Use customer behavior connected to purchasing decisions. Sharing business data, involving users, or bringing a pilot into an internal meeting can provide evidence. Explain what each action establishes instead of assuming it proves readiness to sign.

### What if the customer is still deciding whether to build or buy?

Clarify the total-cost, timing, and risk questions behind that choice. Prepare workable alternatives and consider limited validation with mutually confirmed success criteria. Do not assume that supplier selection has already begun.
