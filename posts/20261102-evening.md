A large customer's strong profit announcement can open a useful sales conversation, but it does not establish that your project deserves funding. The more useful question is how the customer earns its profit, and how your proposal could protect or improve it.

The supplied source describes CNOOC's net profit of 119.1 billion yuan for the first three quarters, with high oil prices as the main support. It does not specify the reporting year. Treat this as a case for understanding purchasing decisions, rather than a current financial update.

For key-account sellers, the practical lesson is straightforward: when earnings depend heavily on commodity prices, proposals need to connect operational improvements with investment decisions. This guide develops that approach through five capabilities: value, relationships, sales process, questioning, and negotiation.

## Start with the customer's profit structure

When higher commodity prices support earnings, a customer may be more willing to consider projects that improve efficiency, sustain production, or reduce risk. When prices fall, the same proposed expenditure may face renewed scrutiny. Strong earnings therefore provide context, not proof of an available project budget.

Before discussing your offering, investigate three questions:

- Which business activities generate the relevant profit, and which would come under pressure first if prices changed?
- Where are costs causing difficulty, and what improvements has the customer already attempted?
- Who is responsible for that profit, and whose performance measures would your proposal affect?

Use the answers to develop a business hypothesis. Identify an operational activity your solution might improve, then ask the customer to validate the connection. Keep an unverified hypothesis separate from an established customer need.

## Value: turn a proposal into an investment explanation

A statement such as 'we improve efficiency' leaves the customer to calculate its significance. A useful investment explanation identifies the required expenditure, the operational activity affected, the expected improvement, the verification method, and the time needed to observe results.

Start with the operational mechanism. Establish what could change, quantify the improvement where evidence permits, and translate it into revenue, cost, profit, payback period, or opportunity cost. Do not supply an attractive number simply because the proposal needs one.

| Proposal element | Product presentation | Investment explanation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Main subject | Features and specifications | The operational activity affected |
| Financial discussion | Purchase price | Investment and potential financial effect |
| Supporting evidence | Capability descriptions | An agreed method for verifying improvement |
| Timing | Delivery schedule | When the business effect could be assessed |

The source calls the resulting document a PIP, or profit growth proposal. It answers three questions: how much profit could be increased or protected, what mechanism and evidence support that expectation, and over what period it could happen.

### Build a one-page PIP

Organize the page around investment, profit impact, and verification. Include the operational mechanism and timeframe within those sections. Where an assumption remains unresolved, identify what information is needed to validate it.

Distinguish between the people who allocate and approve funds and those who operate the business using those funds. Help the operating team explain the proposal in business terms that funding approvers can assess. This gives them something more useful than a product description to carry into an internal budget discussion.

## Relationships: make decision information explicit

Professional credibility and dependable action matter, but relationships also require an accurate understanding of how the organization decides. A friendly contact does not necessarily provide that understanding.

The source uses PRTABCD to organize seven categories of decision information:

- **P — Past:** relevant history and previous attempts to address the issue.
- **R — Requirements:** what the customer needs to improve.
- **T — Time:** timing and important milestones.
- **A — Arm:** the internal supporter willing to help advance the proposal.
- **B — Budget:** the funding situation.
- **C — Competition:** competing suppliers and alternative approaches.
- **D — Decision:** the decision chain across people and departments.

For every category, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. This turns relationship knowledge into something the account team can examine and update.

Two distinctions deserve care. Arm means someone inside the customer organization who actively supports the effort. Decision means the wider decision chain, rather than one person assumed to have final authority.

For customers exposed to price cycles, revisit budget and competition frequently. Earlier information may become less useful as financial conditions or alternatives change.

## Process: follow the customer's purchasing work

The source describes five sales stages: contact, needs, proposal, negotiation, and closing. It also identifies seven customer purchasing stages:

1. Recognize a problem.
2. Assess its value.
3. Confirm that a solution exists.
4. Establish responsibility.
5. Decide whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Select a solution and supplier.
7. Commit and sign.

These perspectives have a many-to-many relationship. They do not form a fixed sequence of matching pairs. A seller may be presenting a proposal while the customer is still deciding whether the problem justifies investment.

Assess progress through customer behavior. Sharing profit-related data for joint analysis suggests work on value assessment. Comparing internal development with external purchasing indicates consideration of the fifth purchasing task.

Before advancing your sales activity, write down the customer's current decision task and the evidence supporting that assessment. Then choose an action that helps complete the task. A finished presentation does not establish that the customer has initiated a budget process.

## Questioning: explore the business effect of price changes

The source applies SPIN through situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions. The purpose is to help the customer articulate the problem and the value of addressing it.

Use the following sequence as a starting point:

- **Situation:** Which external prices affect this business activity's profit, and how is that profit measured internally?
- **Problem:** If prices decline, which operating costs become hardest to absorb?
- **Implication:** If those costs continue rising, which performance measures or annual targets would be affected?
- **Need-payoff:** What would reducing that exposure mean for annual profit?

Follow the customer's answers. If the operational difficulty or financial consequence remains unclear, continue investigating before presenting a solution.

These questions can move the discussion from the price of an offering toward the value of solving a business problem. That shift depends on what the customer establishes during the conversation; it is not a guaranteed result of asking four questions.

## Negotiation: prepare exchanges before making concessions

Strong customer profits can tempt sellers to relax their terms. Weaker profits can push them toward repeated discounts. Neither response substitutes for preparation.

Before negotiating, assess bargaining power, time pressure, available information, your minimum acceptable terms, and your alternatives. Then identify what you can concede and what you need in return.

The source identifies several negotiable conditions: scope, delivery timing, payment arrangements, acceptance criteria, and later expansion. Consider these together when preparing an exchange.

For each possible concession, state its boundary and the corresponding condition you would request. Do not assume that a profitable customer should pay more simply because its earnings are strong. Keep the discussion connected to the proposal's value and the terms both parties can accept.

## Actionable account checklist

Apply the guide to one active opportunity:

- [ ] Describe the relevant profit source and its exposure to price changes.
- [ ] Identify the operational activity your proposal could affect.
- [ ] Confirm who operates that activity and who approves its funding.
- [ ] Draft a one-page PIP covering investment, profit impact, and verification.
- [ ] Mark assumptions requiring customer confirmation.
- [ ] Review all seven PRTABCD categories.
- [ ] Select the three unknowns most likely to affect the decision and prepare follow-up questions.
- [ ] Record behavioral evidence of the customer's current purchasing task.
- [ ] Check that your next action supports that task.
- [ ] Prepare concession boundaries and the conditions you would seek in exchange.

## FAQ

### What should I prepare for a first meeting?

Bring public information about the customer's profit structure, a hypothesis tied to a specific operational activity, and questions to test it. Aim to establish the improvement priority and who is responsible for the affected profit.

### Does a PIP need to be a long document?

No. One page can work if it explains potential profit improvement or protection, the supporting mechanism, and the timeframe. Its financial assumptions and verification method need customer agreement.

### What if the customer will not share enough data?

Record the gap and the next question needed to resolve it. Keep the financial effect provisional instead of presenting an unsupported estimate as an agreed benefit.

### How can I distinguish activity from progress?

Look for customer actions: contributing data, arranging a cross-department review, or examining whether to build or buy. Presentation attendance alone does not establish that a purchasing decision has advanced.

### Should strong customer profits change my pricing approach?

They should inform your understanding of the customer's situation. They do not justify an automatic pricing change. Prepare around value, alternatives, timing, and explicit exchanges of conditions.
