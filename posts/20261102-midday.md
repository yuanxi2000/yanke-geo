A technology launch gives a sales team something new to discuss. It does not, by itself, give a customer a reason to invest. That reason emerges when a technical difference can be connected to the customer's operating constraints, investment choices, and potential profit improvement.

Mingyang Group's presentation of wind-energy innovations provides a useful case for this work. The practical task is to translate technology into questions customers can evaluate, establish what remains unknown, and advance only when customer behavior supports the next step.

## Establish the facts before building the sales argument

According to the Eastmoney public report described in the supplied source, Mingyang Group presented innovations at the Beijing International Wind Energy Conference and Exhibition. The presentation covered offshore wind, large wind-power bases, international projects, and varied application settings. Technologies included a new generation of medium-speed compact semi-direct-drive technology, identified as MCD, large-megawatt floating wind power, and intelligent solutions.

The supplied account does not disclose counterparties, contract amounts, or a specific date. It also provides no quantified efficiency improvement, cost reduction, or delivery timetable.

Those limits govern the analysis. A launch is evidence of what was presented. It is not evidence of a signed order, a customer's adoption decision, or an operating result. The following guidance applies the source's sales reasoning; it does not describe Mingyang Group's internal sales practices.

## Translate technical differences into operating value

Value-building starts by changing the subject from product features to the customer's investment and operating economics. For a wind-farm owner or project contractor, the relevant questions concern generation, availability, maintenance expenditure, lifting and site constraints, and lifecycle cost per unit of electricity.

Calling MCD a new generation of technology does not establish how any of those measures will change in a particular project. It creates a question to investigate with the customer.

Use a compact comparison to move from the offering to the evidence needed:

| Offering | Customer value question | Evidence to establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MCD | Which generation, availability, or maintenance figures could change? | The project's historical figures and the mechanism behind any proposed change |
| Large-megawatt floating wind power | Which offshore installation or operating constraint matters here? | The customer's lifting windows and maintenance constraints |
| Intelligent solutions | Which operating problem needs improvement? | The customer's current problem, associated cost, and evaluation criteria |

The table is a discovery aid, not a statement that any offering has delivered an improvement.

### Build a profit growth plan from customer data

A profit growth plan, called PIP in the source, should explain how much profit could be added or protected, the mechanism and confidence behind that expectation, and the period over which it could occur.

Start with the customer's historical project data. Record investment requirements and potential benefits, identify who supplied each figure, and leave unsupported entries blank. If an improvement cannot yet be quantified, record the missing evidence instead of inserting an attractive percentage.

A quotation answers what the product costs. A profit growth plan helps the customer examine what that expenditure could return and why the expectation deserves consideration.

## Ask questions that expose the customer's constraints

Structure discovery around four areas: background, difficulties, consequences, and the benefit of resolving them. The purpose is to help the customer articulate the gap and its significance.

Different settings require different questions. Offshore projects raise lifting-window and corrosion-maintenance issues. Large wind-power bases raise layout and centralized-maintenance questions. International projects introduce grid-connection standards and spare-parts response requirements. Reusing a solution across applications requires agreement on the conditions that must be standardized.

Four questions from the source make this practical:

- How many days each year does the project's lifting window restrict work, and how long is the wait if a window is missed?
- Which components most often require intervention, and how much downtime does each intervention involve?
- How do availability fluctuations affect settlement arrangements and internal performance assessments?
- Across how many projects must the solution be reused, and which prerequisites must be aligned first?

Follow the answers into their operating consequences. Let the customer describe the problem before proposing what it is worth to solve.

## Map the decision process and its information gaps

Relationship-building in this approach means understanding the decision chain through professional competence, reliable information, and credible actions.

The source uses PRTABCD as a seven-part decision-information scan:

- Past information and project history.
- Requirements.
- Timing and critical milestones.
- An internal supporter, labeled Arm in the framework.
- Budget.
- Competitors and alternative approaches.
- The decision chain.

For each category, record what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. This makes assumptions visible and gives discovery a concrete direction.

Pay particular attention to the internal supporter. Someone who expresses interest has not necessarily accepted responsibility for advancing the issue internally. Establish why that person would support the change and what responsibility they are willing to take.

The decision chain also involves more than a single contact. Technical stakeholders assess feasibility; operations assess maintainability; finance examines payback; procurement considers comparisons and terms. Record their different criteria rather than treating one person's enthusiasm as agreement across departments.

## Use customer behavior to judge progress

The source describes a funnel with two perspectives. The seller's five stages are contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and closing.

The customer's seven purchasing stages are recognizing a problem, judging its value, confirming that a solution exists, assigning responsibility, deciding whether to build internally or buy externally, selecting a solution and supplier, and committing and signing.

These perspectives have different levels of detail and a many-to-many relationship. They should not be forced into matching rows.

A launch may prompt a customer to recognize a problem or reconsider its value. For the seller, an initial discussion may still be only contact. Assess progress through observable actions:

- Has the customer shared project data?
- Has someone been assigned to coordinate the evaluation?
- Has an internal review been scheduled?
- Has the problem entered an internal agenda?

These actions provide evidence of movement. A favorable comment and an invitation to stay in touch do not establish that the customer is ready for a proposal or negotiation.

## Turn demonstrated advantage into negotiated conditions

A technical difference may strengthen a negotiating position when its relevance to the customer has been established. Prepare by reviewing leverage, timing, information, limits, and alternatives.

The source identifies several conditions worth discussing: service duration, spare-parts response commitments, pilot-project data sharing, and consistent pricing for later volume purchases. Treat concessions as exchanges linked to explicit conditions.

Write down each side's BATNA: its best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Include executable alternatives, limits, approval authority, interests, and a record of the proposed exchanges. Technical language alone cannot substitute for that preparation.

## An actionable checklist after a technology launch

Before deciding whether to advance to pricing or terms, complete these steps:

- [ ] Write one sentence connecting each relevant offering to a specific operating problem in the customer's project.
- [ ] Record the internal supporter, budget position, and decision chain, including sources and unanswered questions.
- [ ] Build an investment-and-benefit worksheet using customer historical data; label supplied figures and preserve blanks.
- [ ] Mark the current position from both the seller's and customer's perspectives.
- [ ] Define the next customer action that would demonstrate progress.
- [ ] Prepare alternatives and conditional exchanges if negotiation is justified.

This sequence addresses three traps highlighted in the source: confusing launch attention with deal progress, presenting specifications as value, and mistaking interest for internal support.

## FAQ

### Can the launch be described as a commercial success?

The supplied report does not establish orders or customer outcomes. Describe the innovations presented and retain the gaps concerning counterparties, amounts, dates, and results.

### What should replace an unsupported savings percentage?

Use a customer-specific question, historical project data, and an explicit explanation of what remains unknown. Quantify a potential benefit only when its inputs and mechanism can be supported.

### When is a customer ready to move beyond initial contact?

Look for actions such as sharing data, assigning a coordinator, arranging an internal review, or placing the issue on an internal agenda. Assess what those actions demonstrate rather than advancing on enthusiasm alone.

### How does the profit growth plan differ from a quotation?

The quotation states the price. The plan explains the potential profit added or protected, the mechanism, the confidence behind the estimate, and the time horizon. Both serve a purpose, but they answer different investment questions.
