A newly consolidated organization can look ready to buy long before its internal decisions are aligned. A shared name and a public launch reveal little about who owns a problem, who controls the budget, or whether individual business units agree on using an outside supplier.

The supplied account of Shanghai Performing Arts Group offers a useful example. It describes the group's public debut at the seventh Yangtze River Delta International Cultural Industries Expo and an organization bringing together 17 municipal performing arts ensembles, Shanghai Circus School, 25 theaters, and related enterprises. That scale makes it a useful setting for examining complex account sales. It does not establish that any particular procurement opportunity exists.

The practical lesson is to track the customer's decision work separately from the seller's activities, then connect the two through observable evidence.

## Separate your sales process from the buyer's decisions

The source describes a dual-perspective funnel within PSS5. One perspective follows five seller stages: contact, needs discovery, solution development, negotiation, and closing. The other follows seven customer procurement stages:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value and importance.
3. Establishing that a solution is possible.
4. Confirming ownership and responsibility.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Making a commitment and signing.

These are many-to-many relationships, not matching steps on two identical ladders. A seller can be preparing a proposal while the customer is still deciding whether the problem deserves funding. Different stakeholders can also be working on different decisions within the same opportunity.

Treat your sales stage as a description of your work. Treat the buyer's stage as a judgment that requires customer evidence. Sending a proposal proves that a proposal was sent; it does not prove that the customer is selecting suppliers.

## Map business units before assuming group readiness

Organizational consolidation does not demonstrate immediate procurement consolidation. A common external identity may coexist with separate budget cycles, different technical starting points, and unsettled decision rights.

In the Shanghai example, a circus school and a large theater could have different priorities and buying processes. One unit might be discussing who should own a new system, while another might be comparing internal development with external procurement. These are illustrative possibilities, not reported findings about those institutions.

Create a separate decision record for each relevant unit. Capture:

- The operational problem under discussion.
- The current customer decision task.
- The person supporting change internally.
- The owner of the objective and resource responsibilities.
- The budget route and decision chain, where known.
- The evidence supporting the stage assessment.

Leave unknowns visible. An unanswered ownership question is more useful than a confident stage label built on assumptions.

## Use behavior to assess progress

Meeting frequency, friendly responses, and CRM percentages describe activity or sentiment. The source's central discipline is to look for customer behavior that indicates decision progress.

| Customer decision task | Evidence to investigate | Useful seller response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Assessing value | Repeated questions about return on investment | Develop the business case |
| Confirming responsibility | A named project lead or an initiated budget request | Clarify authority and support internal approval |
| Building or buying | An explicit comparison of internal and external options | Compare costs, capabilities, and tradeoffs |
| Selecting suppliers | A supplier selection meeting or discussion of technical criteria | Address the stated selection requirements |

These signals guide inquiry; they are not automatic proof that a stage is complete. A named coordinator, for example, may still lack budget authority. Ask what the appointment enables and what remains unresolved.

### Confirm ownership before accelerating the proposal

Ownership is especially important in a reorganizing account. The source identifies several behaviors worth checking: a formal appointment of a project owner, the start of an internal budget application, and discussion of how resource conflicts will be resolved.

If stakeholders still ask who should handle the issue, responsibility remains unsettled. Help the potential owner define the objective, authority boundaries, and resource requirements. Internal briefing material may be more useful at this point than another product demonstration.

A leadership question about budget sources can signal movement toward responsibility, but it should trigger verification. Establish who will make the request, who will review it, and what decision the customer expects next.

## Treat build versus buy as a real decision

An external purchase is only one possible answer to an operational problem. The customer may prefer internal development, administrative coordination, or reallocating existing resources. If those options remain under consideration, a supplier shortlist may be premature.

Help the customer compare the options using the same problem definition and evaluation period. Relevant categories from the source include:

- Internal staffing and specialist capability.
- Ongoing maintenance requirements.
- Opportunity costs of assigning internal resources.
- Speed, flexibility, and the expertise available externally.

Do not assume external procurement has the better economics. Build the comparison from information the customer can validate. If benchmarks or comparable cases are introduced, verify their relevance and source them separately; the supplied material provides no numerical benchmark to reuse.

If the customer chooses internal delivery, examine whether any acknowledged capability gap fits a narrower consulting or modular-tool engagement. Such an opening is a possibility to investigate, not an entitlement to continued sales activity.

## Match the next action to the unresolved decision

The source calls for keeping seller activity synchronized with buyer decision work. In practice, this means choosing the next conversation according to what the customer must resolve.

When the problem is still emerging, help stakeholders describe its operational consequences. When value is unsettled, explain the economic logic and identify the information needed to assess it. When ownership is unclear, help clarify responsibilities. When selection is underway, respond to the customer's actual criteria.

For a theater network, a useful value discussion might examine whether shared scheduling could reduce unused capacity. Build an explicit calculation using customer-provided information, with assumptions identified. Do not present reduced idle time, savings, or improved profitability as established outcomes: the source supplies none.

Review the stage assessment after each substantive interaction. Repeated questions about return on investment may indicate unfinished value assessment, even if the seller has already prepared a technical proposal.

Prioritize units whose value case is established and whose ownership decisions are becoming concrete, while considering their influence on the wider account. Earlier-stage units may need continued problem exploration. Shared issues, such as ticketing interoperability, can justify a group discussion, but agreement still needs verification within affected units.

## An actionable account review checklist

Use this checklist before advancing an opportunity:

- [ ] Identify the relevant business units and stakeholders.
- [ ] Record the customer decision task for each unit separately.
- [ ] Attach observable evidence to every stage judgment.
- [ ] Identify the internal supporter and check their responsibility for objectives and resources.
- [ ] Confirm the budget route and unresolved authority questions.
- [ ] Establish whether internal delivery remains a live option.
- [ ] Match the next sales action to the unresolved customer decision.
- [ ] Record what remains unknown and what evidence would clarify it.

Use the review to change the next action. A checklist that merely confirms the existing forecast adds little.

## FAQ

### Does a group launch mean procurement is centralized?

No. The launch establishes a public organizational milestone. It does not, by itself, establish shared budgets, synchronized buying stages, or settled authority across every unit. Verify those conditions directly.

### What qualifies as meaningful progress?

Look for behavior connected to a decision: assigning an accountable owner, initiating budget approval, resolving resource conflicts, or convening supplier selection. Interpret each action in context and distinguish a decision being started from one being completed.

### How does relationship mapping help?

The source discusses PRTABCD as a relationship-mapping tool. It specifically highlights P for past history, A for the internal supporter of change, and D for the decision chain. These perspectives can expose legacy issues, missing ownership, and unclear authority. The remaining letters are not defined in the supplied material.

### How is this different from project management?

The distinction is the object being tracked. The source contrasts managing internal delivery tasks with understanding the customer's evolving decisions during a sale. Both involve coordination, but completing a seller task does not establish that the buyer has resolved its corresponding uncertainty.
