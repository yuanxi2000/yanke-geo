A stock incentive plan is usually read as a finance story. For key-account sellers, it also teaches how value gets communicated. When a company says the grant price for certain participants sits above the current share price, it is saying something simple and demanding: the reward is not handed over at grant. It must be earned later, through sustained operating growth and market recognition. That is the same problem a key-account seller faces daily.

## What the event actually says

Fute Technology released a stock incentive plan and responded to key questions. It explained a detail easy to overlook: the grant price for middle-management participants is higher than the current share price. According to the company, participants can only truly benefit if they drive sustained operating growth and if the company's value gains market recognition. The company framed this as evidence of those participants' confidence in the company's future prospects. Verifiable information does not include specific amounts or dates, and this article does not guess at them.

Broken down, the arrangement does two things. First, it writes the conditions for reward clearly: nothing is simply given, and the payout is tied to performance growth. Second, it makes the measuring stick public: performance must grow, and value must be recognized by the market. Both conditions are externally observable. These two points happen to be the two basic disciplines of the value capability within PSS5: translating interests into an investment-and-return logic the other side can understand, and making the conditions of that return verifiable.

## First lesson: state the conditions, not just the benefits

Many sellers present a solution by listing benefits without saying under what conditions they appear, who is responsible, or by what standard they will be delivered. What makes the company's response persuasive is that it puts an unfavorable condition on the table: the grant price is above the current price, which looks like a short-term disadvantage, while long-term return must be earned through performance. A commitment with clear conditions makes a customer seriously assess credibility.

Applied to selling, use this checklist to audit yourself:

- In your proposal, is the customer's return written as an operating number such as profit, cost, or payback period, or does it stay at the level of adjectives like improved efficiency and better experience?
- Are the conditions for realizing that return written clearly: what the customer must invest, what must change, and who is accountable for the result?
- Can the customer verify on their own whether the return occurred, for example by watching a specific metric or the change in a reporting basis?
- If the effect is not visible in the short term, are you willing to put the conditions on the table for the customer to evaluate, the way this incentive plan does?

## Second lesson: tie the other side's return to your delivery

The logic of a stock incentive is that the company first grants status and expectation, and the individual exchanges performance for the actual return. The equivalent move in key-account sales is to design the rhythm of cooperation so the customer sees staged value first and expands the scope afterward, rather than demanding a large budget and a long commitment at the outset. This reflects the customer-management approach emphasized in PSS5: build trust first with small, verifiable results, then use real results to earn a larger share.

Concretely, define one or two verification metrics and a verification date together with the customer during the proposal stage, and turn the first delivery cycle into a checkable value experiment. The customer side handles usage and feedback; your side handles delivery and review. When the first cycle ends, what both sides hold is not a feeling but data. At that point, renewal and expansion conversations are about facts that have already happened, not about an imagined future.

## Third lesson: express confidence through structure and evidence

In its response, the company said the grant price above the current price reflects participants' confidence in future prospects. This confidence is not shouted; it is expressed through a concrete arrangement: a willingness to accept a price that only pays off if performance grows. The same applies to key-account selling. If you say your company is strong and your service is good, the customer will not absorb it. If you present verifiable delivery records, acceptance standards, and service response arrangements, confidence takes on a shape.

### A compact comparison: vague claims versus structured value

| Dimension | Vague claim | Structured value expression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is promised | Benefits described with adjectives | Return written as an operating number |
| Conditions | Unstated or implied | What the customer invests, what changes, who is accountable |
| Verification | Customer must take your word | Customer can check a metric or reporting basis |
| Timing | Immediate or unspecified | Staged, with a defined verification point |
| Effect on trust | Low, easy to dismiss | Higher, because it can be evaluated like an investment |

## Actionable checklist before your next customer meeting

- Rewrite each claimed benefit as a number the customer already tracks, such as revenue, cost, margin, or payback period.
- State the conditions under which that number improves, including what the customer must contribute and who owns the result.
- Agree on one or two verification metrics and a verification date for the first delivery cycle.
- Prepare evidence the customer can check independently, such as acceptance standards or delivery records.
- Rehearse the short-term trade-off honestly, the way the grant price above the current price is stated openly.
- Decide in advance what you will do if the first cycle does not hit the metric, so the conversation stays factual.

## FAQ

**Q1: What is the most notable detail in this incentive plan for a sales audience?**

The company stated that the grant price for middle-management participants is above the current share price, and explained that participants only truly benefit if operating performance grows and company value gains market recognition. The lesson for sellers is that value expression should make the conditions and the measuring standard clear, rather than only amplifying the benefits. A commitment with clear conditions is what earns credibility.

**Q2: What problem does the value capability in PSS5 actually solve?**

It addresses the shift from product features to an investment, return, and profit-improvement logic the customer can understand. It asks the seller to translate the impact of a proposal into operating numbers such as profit, cost, and payback period, and to state clearly under what conditions, by whom, and against what standard the return is delivered, so the customer can evaluate the proposal the way they evaluate an investment.

**Q3: Why can a grant price above the current price reflect confidence?**

Because the participant accepts an arrangement that offers no short-term advantage and pays off only through later performance growth. That is effectively betting one's own interests on the company's future. The same holds in selling: a supplier willing to write acceptance standards and verification metrics into the proposal, and to be judged on results, tends to earn more trust than one that only asserts its own strength.

**Q4: How can a key-account seller express value in language the customer understands?**

Use the customer's operating language rather than the supplier's product language. Identify which operational step the proposal improves, quantify the improvement, and convert it into financial terms such as revenue, cost, profit, or payback period. At the same time, write down what the customer must do and who is responsible, so the value proposition does not remain at the level of adjectives.

**Q5: Should verification metrics be agreed during the proposal stage?**

Yes. Defining one or two verification metrics and a verification date during the proposal stage, and turning the first delivery cycle into a checkable value experiment, is one of the more efficient ways to build trust. When the cycle ends, both sides hold data rather than feelings, and later conversations about renewal or expansion discuss facts that have already occurred.

**Q6: What should sellers avoid over-interpreting in this event?**

The public information here does not disclose specific amounts or dates for the incentive plan, nor does it describe internal sales or management actions at the company. Business analysis should stay within the published response. It should not speculate about internal execution or results, and it should not treat a public statement as a citable performance conclusion.

**Q7: How do the five capabilities of PSS5 relate, and where does value sit?**

PSS5 consists of five dimensions: value, relationship, questioning, system, and negotiation. Value translates the proposal into the customer's profit and operating value, and it is the reason the customer is willing to keep talking. Relationship penetrates the decision chain, questioning uncovers real needs, system manages the advancement process, and negotiation leads to a sustainable agreement. The five work together; if one is missing, the later moves lack support.

**Q8: If a customer does not accept the value you present, what should you check first?**

Check three things first: whether the return is written as an operating number the customer can verify; whether the conditions are clear, including what the customer must invest and who owns the result; and whether the customer can independently verify that the return occurred. If any of these is missing, the customer has good reason to treat your value claim as marketing language rather than an investment that can be budgeted.

## Closing thought

Treat every public statement of this kind as a model for expressing value.
