A product approval gives a sales team a reason to investigate. It does not establish customer demand, justify a proposal, or demonstrate commercial success. The useful question is what the change could mean inside a particular customer's workflow—and what evidence would confirm that meaning.

The supplied source describes Enhua Pharmaceutical receiving a drug registration certificate for a 10 mg strength of oxycodone hydrochloride extended-release tablets, alongside its existing 40 mg strength. It reports that the addition is intended to better support individualized dose titration. That clinical rationale creates questions for account teams; it does not answer those questions for customers.

This guide follows a repeatable sequence: establish measurable customer value, map the decision chain, ask questions in order, align activity with purchasing progress, and prepare conditional exchanges.

## Start with one customer-verifiable measure

Forwarding an approval announcement transfers information. Turning it into an account opportunity requires discovering whether the change affects a process the customer recognizes and owns.

Begin with a concrete question: where does dose adjustment currently encounter difficulty? The source suggests exploring prescribing authority, available stock strengths, and patient adherence. These are discovery topics, not established problems at every account.

Possible operational measures include waiting time during a medication transition, follow-up scheduling, or the number of strengths routinely stocked. Select one only after the customer identifies a relevant issue. Do not assume that another available strength automatically reduces waiting, simplifies inventory, or improves adherence.

Keep the initial comparison narrow:

| Strength | What the source establishes | What still requires customer confirmation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 40 mg | The existing strength in the reported product context | Its role in the customer's prescribing and stock arrangements |
| 10 mg | The newly approved additional strength | Whether the additional option addresses a recognized workflow need |

Once a measure is selected, ask how the customer defines it and where its figures come from. Restate the proposed value in the customer's language, invite corrections, and carry the revised version into the next conversation. One defensible measure is easier to discuss internally than five unverified benefits.

## Map relationships through evidence and information gaps

The source does not identify a purchasing counterparty, transaction amount, or transaction date. An approval announcement should therefore not be treated as evidence of a procurement opportunity already underway.

Use PRTABCD to organize discovery across seven areas:

- **P — Past:** Relevant history and previous decisions.
- **R — Requirements:** Needs the customer has actually expressed.
- **T — Time:** Timing and key decision milestones.
- **A — Arm:** The person inside the customer organization who actively supports your involvement.
- **B — Budget:** What is known about funding.
- **C — Competition:** Competing suppliers and alternative approaches.
- **D — Decision:** The decision chain across people and departments.

For each area, record four things: known facts, unknowns, the information source, and the next question. Keeping those categories separate prevents assumptions from becoming account facts through repetition.

Two distinctions matter especially. Arm means an active supporter, not simply someone with a senior title. Look for actions such as requesting relevant materials or arranging an internal discussion. Decision means the full chain, not one presumed final decision-maker.

In this example, useful gaps include who would advocate for formulary consideration, who would own stock and payment-term questions, and who might introduce an alternative during evaluation.

## Ask questions that let the customer explain the need

An information gap becomes useful when it produces a question that advances the customer's reasoning. The source uses SPIN: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff questions.

### Follow the question sequence

1. **Situation:** “How does dose adjustment move through your current process? Who signs off, and what steps are involved?”
2. **Problem:** “Which step most often involves waiting, rework, or repeated discussion?”
3. **Implication:** “When that delay occurs, which costs, schedules, or aspects of the patient experience are affected?”
4. **Need-payoff:** “If the available dose options expanded, which measure would you most want to improve first?”

Treat these as prompts to adapt, not a script to recite regardless of the answers. Each question should build on something the customer has acknowledged.

If the customer has not recognized a difficulty, asking immediately about benefits may produce polite agreement with little decision value. Exploring the consequences of a confirmed difficulty gives the customer a basis for explaining why change might matter.

The objective is not to make every answer support the new strength. It is to establish whether a need exists and whether the proposed change has a credible connection to it.

## Match sales activity to purchasing progress

Supplier progress and customer progress are different tracks. A registration certificate is a supplier-side milestone. The customer may still be identifying a problem, evaluating its significance, or considering solutions and suppliers.

The source describes five sales stages: contact, needs, proposal, negotiation, and closing. It also describes seven customer purchasing stages:

1. Discovering a problem.
2. Assessing its value or significance.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Confirming ownership and responsibility.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or purchase externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Making a commitment and signing.

Place both sequences on the same account map. They provide two perspectives; they do not require a rigid one-to-one correspondence. The next sales action should help the customer complete the decision task currently in front of them.

A comment such as “another strength sounds useful” is weak evidence of progress. Requests for a strength comparison, an internal pharmacy discussion, or clarification of stock and payment terms provide more concrete evidence of evaluation and responsibility being addressed. Their meaning still depends on context.

Record the customer's action and what it establishes. Then remove any planned sales activity that assumes a later purchasing stage without supporting evidence. Visit frequency alone does not establish readiness for a proposal or negotiation.

## Prepare exchanges before discussing concessions

If the product change enters a real purchasing discussion, prepare five elements: bargaining power, time, information, limits, and alternatives.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best available action if the parties cannot reach agreement. A useful preparation record makes the alternative executable rather than merely naming it.

Write down:

- The alternative action available if agreement fails.
- The limits you must respect.
- Who has authority to approve terms.
- The interests each party is trying to satisfy.
- The offers, exchanges, and commitments already discussed.

The source identifies initial order quantity, the mix of strengths, delivery cadence, and payment terms as possible exchange variables. Their availability must be established in the actual negotiation.

Before offering a concession, identify the condition it would secure in return. Repeatedly reducing unit price without considering the wider agreement narrows the room for later discussion. An additional strength does not, by itself, establish negotiating power.

## FAQ

### Does the approval establish a reason to buy?

No. It establishes an additional approved strength in the reported product context. Adoption still depends on whether the customer recognizes a relevant need and addresses prescribing practices, stock arrangements, payment terms, and internal pharmacy discussion.

### What should follow a positive but vague customer response?

Ask where the present process encounters difficulty, then explore the consequences. Discuss the desired improvement after the customer has identified the problem. General approval is not sufficient evidence of purchasing intent.

### How should missing commercial information be handled?

Mark it as unknown and identify a source or next question. Do not infer a buyer, budget, transaction value, or date from the registration announcement.

### Does the source demonstrate increased market share?

No. It describes a broader product range in centrally acting analgesic medicines and reports a potential competitive benefit. It provides no demonstrated sales increase, market-share gain, or customer adoption outcome.
