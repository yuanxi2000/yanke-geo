A new business announcement can identify a potential account, but it cannot tell you whether that account is ready to buy. The practical challenge is to understand which decisions the customer has completed, which remain unresolved, and what help would move the next decision forward.

The supplied Chaince Compute case illustrates this distinction. Its reported business scope gives a supplier useful boundaries for discovery. It does not establish a budget, a purchasing team, or an active supplier selection process. This guide uses the case to explain the dual-perspective funnel in PSS5: matching the seller's activities to the customer's purchasing tasks and checking progress through observable customer behavior.

## Start with the boundaries of the evidence

The source reports that Chaince Digital established Chaince Compute, registered in Delaware on November 2, to develop small and medium-sized AI data centers in the United States. It describes a target power capacity of no more than 100 megawatts per project. The supplied material does not specify the registration year.

These facts provide a starting point for account planning. They identify a business direction, a geography, and a capacity constraint. They do not reveal the customer's current procurement stage.

A newly established subsidiary may still be defining responsibilities and decision processes. Treat that possibility as a discovery hypothesis. Before preparing a detailed offer, investigate who owns the project, what decision is pending, and whether the customer has established a reason to proceed.

## Map both sides of the buying process

The dual-perspective funnel connects five selling stages with seven customer purchasing tasks. Its purpose is to make the customer's unfinished decisions visible alongside the seller's planned activities.

The five selling stages are contact, needs discovery, solution development, negotiation, and closing. The customer's seven tasks are recognizing a problem, assessing value, confirming that a solution exists, assigning responsibility, deciding whether to build or buy, selecting a solution and supplier, and committing through agreement and signature.

| Selling stage | Customer purchasing tasks | Useful sales contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact | Recognize a problem; assess value | Clarify the problem and the reason to act |
| Needs discovery | Confirm feasibility; assign responsibility | Test requirements and identify accountable owners |
| Solution development | Decide whether to build or buy; select solutions and suppliers | Compare options and agree evaluation criteria |
| Negotiation | Commit and resolve agreement conditions | Address budget approval and contract risks |
| Closing | Complete commitment and signature | Confirm agreements and implementation handover |

This mapping is dynamic. Feasibility questions can remain open while responsibility is being assigned, and ownership can change during solution evaluation. Use the stages as a working map rather than a rigid sequence.

## Choose an action that serves the pending decision

### Establish the problem and its value

At first contact, investigate whether the customer recognizes a problem worth solving. In the source case, relevant questions concern limitations in the existing approach to obtaining computing capacity and the potential long-term economics of developing data centers.

Do not present those potential advantages as established findings. They are matters for the customer to assess. A useful conversation helps clarify why the proposed direction matters and what would justify further evaluation.

An internal project document can provide evidence that the organization has moved beyond general interest. Read what it actually authorizes: a preliminary investigation is different from an approved purchasing commitment.

### Confirm responsibility and feasibility

When the customer is defining requirements, identify who is responsible for technical specifications and who owns commercial decisions. For the reported project scope, that includes responsibility for power-capacity planning and the per-project ceiling of 100 megawatts.

Useful questions include:

- Who defines and approves the capacity requirement?
- Who is accountable for keeping a proposal within that boundary?
- Who evaluates technical feasibility?
- Who owns the commercial assessment?

These questions help establish whether a detailed proposal has an accountable recipient. They also expose unresolved requirements before significant proposal work begins.

A proof of concept may help test whether a suitable solution exists. Treat it as evidence about the question tested, rather than proof that the entire purchasing decision is complete.

### Support build-or-buy and supplier evaluation

Once the customer is considering alternatives, help make the comparison explicit. The source emphasizes total cost of ownership, or TCO, as a tool for examining build-or-buy choices and competing solutions.

Use a comparison that makes assumptions visible and respects the stated project constraints. Clarify which options are being compared, which requirements each addresses, and which uncertainties still need investigation. Avoid presenting an apparently precise conclusion when the underlying inputs remain unsettled.

Help the customer define evaluation criteria before assuming that a preferred technical approach will become the purchasing standard. If uncertainty remains substantial, third-party advice may support the assessment; the source presents this as an option, not a mandatory step.

### Resolve commitment and handover

Negotiation becomes useful when the customer is addressing the conditions for commitment. Attention then shifts toward budget approval, contractual risk, and the practical details of implementation handover.

A discussion about terms does not establish that every earlier task is finished. Check whether responsibility, feasibility, and selection criteria remain settled. If a prerequisite is unresolved, make that dependency visible and agree the next action needed to address it.

## Measure progress through customer behavior

The source's central management rule is to judge progress through customer behavior, rather than meeting frequency or the seller's confidence. A strong relationship can support access, but access alone does not establish procurement readiness.

Look for evidence tied to a particular decision:

- An internal project document supports an assessment of project value or authorization.
- Named technical and commercial contacts support an assessment of responsibility.
- A request for information or request for proposal can indicate structured evaluation.
- A proof of concept supports an assessment of feasibility.

Interpret each item in context. An information request may still be exploratory, and a technical test may leave commercial ownership unresolved. Record the evidence, the task it supports, and the remaining gap. When evidence is missing, keep the stage assessment provisional.

## Handle uncertainty with shorter feedback loops

The source identifies policy, technical direction, and capital availability as possible sources of change in AI data center projects. Its practical response is to shorten feedback loops: identify an assumption, test it through a customer action, and revise the account plan.

When a customer asks to wait, clarify what information or decision is pending. A pause may reflect an unresolved issue; it should not automatically be treated as avoidance.

Ask what would allow the decision to resume, who owns that prerequisite, and what small action would make progress observable. Depending on the issue, that action might be a technical workshop or access to an internal evaluation document. The aim is a verifiable next step, not pressure for an unsupported commitment.

## An actionable account-review checklist

Use this checklist before advancing an opportunity or committing substantial proposal resources:

- [ ] Separate reported facts from assumptions about the account.
- [ ] Identify the customer's current unfinished purchasing task.
- [ ] Record the customer behavior supporting the stage assessment.
- [ ] Confirm technical and commercial responsibility.
- [ ] Check requirements against the stated capacity boundary.
- [ ] Identify unresolved feasibility or build-or-buy questions.
- [ ] Agree evaluation criteria before refining the offer.
- [ ] Choose a next action that helps complete the pending task.
- [ ] Record its owner and the evidence expected from completion.
- [ ] Revisit the assessment when responsibilities or constraints change.

If several items remain unresolved, make clarification the next sales action.

## FAQ

### How does this differ from a conventional sales funnel?

It adds the customer's purchasing tasks to the seller's stages. A proposal being delivered records seller activity; evidence that the customer has agreed evaluation criteria helps explain purchasing progress.

### Does a new subsidiary imply an early-stage opportunity?

It provides a reason to investigate. The source suggests that a new entity may be establishing processes and responsibilities, but its formation alone does not prove purchasing readiness or a particular internal structure.

### Should a capacity-constrained project receive a detailed quotation immediately?

The capacity boundary helps frame discovery, but it does not replace it. First clarify responsibility, requirements, and the decision the quotation would support. Detail becomes useful when the customer can evaluate it against an agreed task.

### What if senior contacts express strong interest?

Record that interest, then seek evidence of organizational action. Named owners, project documentation, evaluation requests, and feasibility work provide a stronger basis for assessing progress than enthusiasm alone.

### Can the customer revisit an earlier purchasing task?

Yes. The source calls for dynamic stage matching and continued attention to responsibility. When assumptions or owners change, reassess the affected task and adapt the next action accordingly.
