When an investment channel becomes restricted, an established partnership needs a new business case. The client must justify further investment in the relationship, and the seller must demonstrate why its contribution remains valuable under the revised rules. Familiar contacts and a workable historical arrangement cannot answer those questions on their own.

The supplied source discusses restrictions affecting wealth-management investment through trust plans. It describes a substantial adjustment effort and suggests that relying on nonstandard trust assets to enhance returns is unlikely to remain viable in the circumstances reported. For key-account sales, the durable lesson is to treat this as a change in purchasing rules.

The practical response connects five capabilities: questioning, relationship management, value development, sales process management, and negotiation. Each addresses a different part of the client's decision.

## Start with the client's changed purchasing problem

An earlier conversation might have centered on whether the arrangement could move funds into the intended investments. Once that route narrows, the client faces three different questions:

- Which products can still invest through trust plans?
- Could an alternative approach come close to the original return objectives?
- Does the existing partner still deserve a place among the available options?

These questions span investment, risk, compliance, and operations. Procurement alone cannot resolve them. A commercial contact may understand the relationship while lacking authority to settle the revised investment or compliance requirements.

The seller therefore needs to establish what changed inside the client organization before proposing new terms. Confirm the affected product lines, the owner of the adjustment objective, and the people responsible for setting evaluation criteria. Those facts determine what a credible proposal must address.

## Turn adjustment pressure into questions clients can answer

A broad statement about a difficult adjustment program identifies pressure without defining the work. Break that pressure into facts the client can confirm, using four types of questions in sequence.

### Use four question types in order

1. **Background:** Which product lines still invest through trust plans? Which have paused, and who authorized the pause?
2. **Difficulty:** How will the paused products address any return shortfall? Who carries the resulting customer complaints or distribution pressure?
3. **Impact:** If an alternative takes longer to implement, how might pressures on business volume, reputation, and compliance accumulate?
4. **Desired payoff:** If an approach could address return objectives and compliance requirements together, which part should it tackle first?

The final question explores a desired outcome; it does not promise that the outcome is achievable. Keep that distinction explicit when discussing alternatives.

Record answers by product line and responsible person. An unresolved question should lead to a specific follow-up with someone able to answer it. This turns a vague concern into a working assessment.

## Rebuild the decision map

Rule changes can bring additional people into a purchasing decision. Risk, compliance, or operations may now have veto power even if they previously played a limited role. Repeated access to the original contact does not establish access to the full decision process.

The source uses PRTABCD to organize seven areas of decision information:

- **P — Past:** Relevant history of the account and cooperation.
- **R — Requirements:** What the client now needs.
- **T — Time:** The timing that governs the decision.
- **A — Arm:** The person inside the client organization actively supporting your involvement.
- **B — Budget:** The available budget.
- **C — Competitors and alternatives:** Other partners or approaches under consideration.
- **D — Decision:** The people and departments involved in reaching and approving the decision.

For each area, record what is known and its source, then what remains unknown and whom to ask next.

Keep internal support separate from decision authority. Evidence of support could be a contact arranging a risk-assessment meeting. A friendly relationship alone is insufficient evidence. Separately identify who proposes the arrangement, who evaluates it, and who signs. Mark any missing link for follow-up.

## Replace channel access with a business case

A channel connects funds and assets. When access becomes restricted, the client needs to understand the business contribution of the proposed arrangement: whether it could support return objectives, reduce rework and communication costs, or help maintain customer retention. These are questions to substantiate, not outcomes to assume.

| Discussion area | Earlier emphasis | Revised emphasis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Investment route | Can funds reach the assets? | Which routes remain available under the requirements? |
| Partner value | Can the partner provide access? | What business contribution can the partner substantiate? |
| Commercial terms | What is the price? | What scope, conditions, and evidence justify the price? |

The source calls for a profit growth plan, or PIP. Its core is three statements: how much profit the proposal could add or preserve, the mechanism that could produce that contribution, and the conditions under which the estimate holds.

Put the benefit, assumptions, and proposed verification method on one page. If the contribution cannot yet be estimated credibly, identify the missing information. Pricing should follow a sufficiently clear value case.

## Match sales activity to purchasing readiness

The source recommends comparing the client's seven purchasing stages with the seller's five sales stages. It does not supply the individual stage definitions, so this guide does not reconstruct them. The usable principle is to check whether the seller's next action matches the decision task the client is actually undertaking.

For example, submitting a quotation while the client is still establishing whether a workable solution exists creates a mismatch. Help resolve that decision task first.

Measure progress through client behavior: someone accepts ownership of the adjustment objective, provides evaluation criteria, or schedules a verification meeting. Visit counts and verbal assurances do not establish that the purchasing process has advanced.

## Prepare alternatives before negotiating concessions

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best executable action available if the current negotiation fails. A rule change can alter both parties' alternatives. The client might consider building an internal team or selecting a different category of partner. The seller might redirect resources toward another customer group. These are possibilities to assess, not facts to assume about a particular account.

Write a short preparation memo covering the executable alternative, the minimum acceptable terms, your decision authority, both parties' interests, and the negotiation record. This gives concessions a defined boundary. Without a workable alternative, price can become the first concession offered.

Use conditional exchanges to connect concessions with something valuable:

- **Scope for price:** Agree on a limited, verifiable pilot scope before discussing the overall price.
- **Timing for terms:** Align delivery milestones with the client's adjustment schedule in exchange for payment arrangements and resource commitments.
- **Information for assessment access:** Provide compliance and risk explanations to support entry into the client's evaluation process.

Each exchange should specify what each side contributes. Providing information supports assessment; it does not establish that an approach satisfies the client's requirements.

## An actionable account checklist

Use this checklist to prepare the next account discussion:

- [ ] Rank accounts by dependence on the restricted channel.
- [ ] Identify affected product lines and confirm the source of that information.
- [ ] Name the adjustment owner and the people setting evaluation criteria.
- [ ] Refresh all seven PRTABCD areas, with owners for unanswered questions.
- [ ] Record concrete evidence of internal support and map proposal, evaluation, and approval responsibilities.
- [ ] Draft the PIP with benefits, mechanisms, conditions, and verification.
- [ ] Check that the next sales action matches purchasing readiness.
- [ ] Write a BATNA memo for every active negotiation.

Prioritize the most dependent accounts, then address the information gaps that prevent a credible proposal.

## FAQ

### What should happen before a revised proposal?

Confirm which product lines are affected, who owns the adjustment objective, and who defines the evaluation criteria. Without those answers, the proposal may address the wrong problem or reach people unable to approve it.

### Why revisit the decision chain if the relationship is strong?

The original contact may remain influential while additional departments gain approval or veto responsibilities. A strong relationship helps obtain information and access, but the current decision chain still needs to be verified.

### How does a PIP differ from a standard proposal?

It centers the expected profit contribution, the mechanism behind it, and the conditions required. Features and price alone leave the client without a clear basis for judging whether the investment is worthwhile.

### What counts as real project progress?

Observable client actions count: assigning an owner, defining evaluation criteria, or arranging verification. Activity by the seller is useful only insofar as it helps the client complete a decision task.

### Should a discount be the first response to uncertainty?

First clarify value, scope, timing, and alternatives. A concession becomes easier to judge when it is tied to a defined exchange and remains within a documented negotiating boundary.
