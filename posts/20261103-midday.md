A major enterprise project announcement can reveal how a customer makes decisions. It cannot, by itself, establish that a particular sales opportunity has closed. For key-account sellers, the useful question is what the customer has demonstrably completed and which decisions still need evidence.

The supplied analysis of CNNC's Qizhishu ERP project provides a practical example. It describes the ERP initiative as the group's top-priority digital transformation project, with Tongfang-affiliated units participating in delivery. Those facts support a discussion of procurement progress. They do not establish a budget allocation, acceptance signatory, or completed commercial handover.

This guide turns that distinction into a working method for qualifying opportunities, preparing meetings, developing value cases, and entering negotiations.

## Read announcements as evidence with limits

A strategic designation indicates organizational importance. Naming participating delivery units provides evidence about how the organization intends to build the system. Neither fact answers every commercial question.

The source interprets the announcement as evidence that the customer has recognized the problem, considered its value, assigned responsibility, and addressed the build-or-buy question. That is an interpretation of public statements, rather than direct access to the customer's internal approvals.

Keep three questions visible:

- Which budget line will fund the work?
- Who will sign off against the acceptance criteria?
- Who owns the business results after launch?

If these answers remain unknown, record them as unknown. Do not allocate resources as though a completed sale has been verified. An announcement, a priority designation, and the identification of delivery participants each provide partial evidence. Their significance depends on the decision task being assessed.

## Map the customer's seven procurement tasks

Customer procurement involves seven decision tasks. Each requires evidence from the customer, rather than confidence expressed by the seller.

1. **Recognize the problem.** The customer acknowledges a business issue worth addressing.
2. **Assess its value.** The customer judges why resolving the issue matters.
3. **Confirm that a solution exists.** The customer accepts that the problem can be addressed.
4. **Assign responsibility.** Someone within the organization accepts ownership.
5. **Choose whether to build or buy.** The customer determines its sourcing approach.
6. **Select the solution and supplier.** The customer evaluates the available approaches and providers.
7. **Commit and contract.** The customer makes the commitments needed to proceed.

Use these tasks to locate missing evidence. A seller's explanation of business value does not prove that the customer has accepted it. A proposed owner does not prove that the person has taken responsibility. The distinction is between a seller's assertion and an observable customer action.

## Qualify the seller's five stages separately

The seller's funnel has five stages: contact, needs, solution, negotiation, and close. Progress depends on customer behavior, rather than visit counts or a percentage entered in a CRM.

| Seller stage | Customer evidence supporting progress | Insufficient evidence alone |
|---|---|---|
| Contact | Discusses a real business problem and agrees a specific next conversation | A completed visit |
| Needs | Shares an internal understanding of the problem, impact, and ownership | The seller's summary |
| Solution | Helps define success criteria and validation methods | Receipt of a quotation |
| Negotiation | Exchanges conditions on price, scope, timing, payment, and risk | General commercial interest |
| Close | Has the contract, budget, accountable people, and implementation handover contacts in place | A public announcement |

The seven customer tasks and five seller stages do not map one to one. One seller stage may support several customer tasks; one customer task may extend across seller stages. Assess both independently before choosing the next action.

For a supplier that did not participate in the ERP project, the announcement offers a retrospective exercise: identify where customer evidence was missing and determine how to obtain that evidence in a future opportunity.

## Use PRTABCD to expose information gaps

PRTABCD organizes decision information into seven categories: past history, requirements, timing, internal support, budget, competitors and alternatives, and the decision chain.

For every category, record four things: what is known, what remains unknown, the information source, and the next question. An empty field becomes a meeting objective.

### Start with budget, internal support, and decision authority

Budget questions should establish where the money comes from, including whether funding is allocated centrally or raised by a member organization. Strategic importance does not supply that answer.

The A in PRTABCD stands for Arm: the person within the customer organization who supports your approach. Establish who is willing to advocate internally and what evidence that person can use. Support should be verified through behavior.

The D stands for Decision and refers to the decision chain across people and departments. It does not mean a single senior executive. Business teams, finance, IT, and audit may hold veto rights at different points. Map those rights by function.

Also examine competing suppliers and alternatives. The customer may compare external providers, internal development, or continued use of the existing system. Record what the customer is actually considering, distinguishing confirmed information from assumptions.

## Translate strategic priority into a measurable value case

A top-priority project designation expresses strategy. Procurement and finance still need a funding basis and an agreed definition of value.

PIP, a profit growth plan, frames the proposal as an investment explanation: how much profit could be increased or protected, through which mechanism, and over what period. These are questions to substantiate, not blanks the seller should fill with invented figures.

When public information supplies no amounts or timetable, begin with measurement definitions:

- Which operating activities would the proposed change affect?
- Which measures would demonstrate the effect?
- Who would provide the underlying data?
- Under what conditions would the result count as achieved?

Agree these definitions before inserting numbers. Record them in meeting notes accepted by both sides. This gives later estimates a shared basis and keeps the proposal tied to evidence the customer recognizes.

## Test relationship assumptions and prepare alternatives

A group-level decision chain requires more than a relationship with one individual. MBVS offers four categories of hypotheses about the same stakeholder: motivation, benchmarks, vision, and solution.

Write each hypothesis down and test it. Its purpose is to invite correction, rather than assign a fixed label to the person. Use the answers alongside the decision map to understand where support exists and where further verification is needed.

Before negotiation, document BATNA: the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Consider the best available action for each side if agreement fails. Separate verified alternatives from assumptions about the other party.

Put boundaries, authority, executable alternatives, and the supporting record in writing. Then distinguish negotiable conditions from fixed constraints across price, scope, timing, payment, and risk. Entering negotiation without an executable alternative weakens the basis for holding commercial terms.

## FAQ

### Does an announced delivery partner prove the sale is complete?

No. It provides evidence about sourcing and selection. The supplied material does not establish the underlying budget allocation, acceptance authority, or implementation handover arrangements. Verify those separately.

### Must every customer task match a seller stage?

No. The relationship is many to many. Locate each side using its own evidence, then decide whether to advance or help the customer finish its current task.

### How does an internal supporter differ from the decision chain?

The supporter advocates for the approach inside the organization. The decision chain describes the people and functions involved in approval, including possible vetoes. One supportive contact cannot establish the whole chain.

### Can a value discussion proceed without financial figures?

Yes. Agree the affected activities, measures, data owners, and achievement conditions first. Leave amounts and timing unresolved until there is evidence to support them.

### What distinguishes progress from activity?

Progress produces customer evidence: agreed internal objectives, business participation in a solution review, a named acceptance signatory, or an identified budget line. Visits, praise, and events alone do not establish advancement.
