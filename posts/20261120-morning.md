When a manufacturing customer moves production across countries, an account manager may first notice threatened orders, changing specifications, or tighter budgets. Those signals matter, but they do not explain the whole opportunity. The customer may be redesigning its business around continuity, policy exposure, and the ability to operate across several locations.

A useful sales response connects an offer to that redesign. This guide uses the supplied Huaci case and the five capabilities of PSS5—value, relationships, questioning, process discipline, and negotiation—to explain how. The aim is to identify defensible business value, understand the buying decision, and develop commitments that both sides can sustain.

## Read the strategic shift behind the financial pressure

The source describes Huaci facing lower revenue and net profit in the first three quarters of the reporting period, with anti-dumping investigations among the contributing factors. It also describes continued expansion across several regions.

According to that account, the first phase of its Vietnam ceramics project was expected to generate annual production value above RMB 300 million at full capacity. A feasibility study for the second phase had begun, the Thailand base was shipping steadily, and an African project was progressing. These are source-reported conditions and expectations, not independently verified current operating results.

The stated rationale was to reduce geopolitical and policy exposure associated with relying on one overseas base. For account planning, this suggests a shift toward security and resilience. It does not establish that price has stopped mattering or that every expansion project has an available purchasing budget.

## Translate capabilities into operating value

Value begins with the customer's exposure. Equipment specifications, material performance, and service features become commercially meaningful when they connect to investment, returns, or protected profit.

For a customer spreading production across countries, investigate three areas:

- **Risk cost:** Compare possible tariff-related losses and cost volatility under different production arrangements.
- **Delivery continuity:** Examine whether supply and service arrangements can support changes between locations without disrupting production.
- **Compliance support:** Identify relevant certification and documentation needs, and establish what assistance the proposed solution can actually provide.

### Build the value case around explicit assumptions

Start with a specific operating problem, such as delayed commissioning or logistics losses. Establish the customer's baseline, describe the proposed change, and calculate its possible financial effect using customer-confirmed inputs. Separate an estimate from a commitment.

A shorter commissioning period could create value, but the source does not establish a proven reduction. Likewise, certification support should not be presented as a guarantee of a favorable anti-dumping outcome or origin-related benefit. Clarify the scope of assistance before assigning financial value to it.

## Map the decision chain across headquarters and plants

A purchasing contact may be essential without controlling the whole decision. Overseas expansion can involve headquarters strategy, local factory management, supply-chain leadership, finance, and legal or compliance teams.

The source uses PRTABCD to examine relationship coverage. Its discussion highlights A, the embracing stakeholder or internal advocate, and D, the decision chain. The advocate could be an overseas operations executive, supply-chain director, or factory leader; the role should be established through evidence.

Use MBVS to investigate stakeholder motivations without assuming that a job title explains them completely. The source proposes several useful hypotheses:

- Operations may prioritize stable shipments.
- Strategy may prioritize reduced geopolitical exposure.
- Finance may prioritize investment returns.

Validate these priorities in conversation. Record who defines requirements, evaluates proposals, and participates in approval. Professional relevance builds trust when each discussion addresses the stakeholder's actual responsibility. Relationship coverage remains incomplete if local operations support the proposal but headquarters requirements remain unknown.

## Use questions to uncover consequences and priorities

The source recommends SPIN questioning: situation, problem, implication, and need-payoff. Use that sequence to investigate the customer's circumstances without turning assumptions into facts.

**Situation:** What is the current status of the proposed expansion, and which commissioning or training activities remain unresolved?

**Problem:** Where does the present supplier arrangement make a new location difficult to support? Does stable output depend heavily on a particular supplier?

**Implication:** If a project is delayed, how would that affect shipments, cash flow, or the wider production plan? Which consequences can the customer quantify?

**Need-payoff:** What would a standardized approach to replicating overseas production need to achieve to justify investment? Would faster commissioning or local training address the highest-priority constraint?

Questions about delays, supplier dependence, or training gaps are investigative prompts. They do not establish that those problems exist. Let the customer confirm the issue and its significance before proposing a solution. A hypothetical earlier production date should remain a scenario until supported by evidence.

## Match sales activity to purchasing progress

Process discipline means aligning the seller's work with the customer's purchasing stage. The source discusses a seven-stage purchasing model and concentrates on stage five, make-or-buy, and stage six, solution and supplier selection.

During make-or-buy evaluation, develop a total cost of ownership comparison. The purpose is to test whether outsourcing a noncore activity offers a credible advantage over building the capability internally. The conclusion should follow the analysis.

During solution and supplier selection, address the customer's evaluation criteria with technical comparisons and relevant, verifiable evidence. If an appropriate reference case is unavailable, identify that evidence gap rather than implying a proven result.

Judge progress through customer behavior: an invitation to technical review, a request for a detailed quotation, or an arranged senior-management discussion. Meeting frequency alone does not establish purchasing readiness. The source's stage assessment is an interpretation of the case, so confirm the actual stage before advancing the opportunity.

## Negotiate responsibilities, timing, and alternatives

Multi-region supply agreements require discussion of risk allocation as well as price. Clarify delivery expectations, response commitments, payment conditions, and the limits each party can accept.

A commissioning deadline may make response speed especially valuable. If the customer confirms that priority, faster support or flexible payment terms could form part of an exchange for a longer-term commitment. The trade must be deliverable and commercially acceptable.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement. Use it to assess a proposed deal and establish a walk-away position. It is not a threat or a slogan.

Keep two ideas distinct: stronger delivery or compliance support can improve the attractiveness of the offer; the seller's BATNA concerns what it can do if this agreement fails. An attractive proposal does not automatically create a stronger outside alternative. Before negotiating, clarify available information, timing pressures, decision weights, and commercial limits.

## Actionable account checklist

Use this checklist before the next substantive customer discussion:

- [ ] Separate source-reported facts, forecasts, and sales hypotheses.
- [ ] Identify the risk the customer's geographic expansion is intended to reduce.
- [ ] Select one operating problem for a quantified value discussion.
- [ ] Request the inputs needed to evaluate cost, timing, or continuity.
- [ ] Map headquarters, plant, finance, and compliance participation.
- [ ] Validate stakeholder priorities and locate gaps in relationship coverage.
- [ ] Prepare SPIN questions that allow the customer to reject the hypothesis.
- [ ] Confirm the purchasing stage and required evaluation evidence.
- [ ] Define feasible commitments, negotiation limits, and the best available alternative.
- [ ] Agree on a next action tied to the customer's decision process.

## FAQ

### Is geographic expansion necessarily a sales opportunity?

It can create demand for production support and supply-chain redesign. It can also disrupt existing orders. Qualify the opportunity by confirming a need, its priority, and the relevant purchasing process. Expansion alone is insufficient.

### How should an account manager respond to a constrained budget?

Confirm which operating problems remain urgent, then examine the investment case. The source suggests phased investment or modular proposals as possible approaches to cash-flow constraints. Their suitability depends on the customer's requirements and the economics of delivery.

### What if procurement is the only established contact?

Use that relationship to understand the wider decision chain. Identify who owns overseas operations, strategic risk, financial evaluation, and compliance. Confirm how those people participate before treating procurement support as organization-wide agreement.

### What evidence indicates that the opportunity is advancing?

Look for actions connected to evaluation: technical-review participation, detailed quotation requests, or senior-level engagement. Interpret each action in context. None independently guarantees an eventual order.

### Can resilience justify a higher price?

Potentially, if the customer accepts the relevance and value of the proposed continuity, response, or compliance support. Establish that value through explicit assumptions and evidence. Claims of unique capability require substantiation.
