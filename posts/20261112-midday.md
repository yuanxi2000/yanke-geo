Localization becomes a practical sales conversation when the customer can connect investment to operating results. For a production-line modification, that means examining unit cost, delivery timing, operating workload and responsibility for downtime risk. Equipment specifications become more useful when they support that calculation.

The supplied Geely analysis uses overseas production-line modification and commissioning to frame this challenge. Its practical lesson is to help customers establish what an investment could improve, what evidence supports the proposal and who will accept the result. The following guide turns that logic into a repeatable approach for key-account work, without assuming that any particular project has already delivered savings.

## Build an investment case the customer can verify

Start with a one-page investment explanation. Organize it around the customer's operating questions: what money is required, when it is committed, which operating measures could change and how those changes will be accepted.

Include four elements:

- **Investment stages:** Break the proposed expenditure into meaningful phases so the customer can examine funding requirements and how long funds will be committed.
- **Operating effects:** Identify the relevant measures, such as unit cost, delivery lead time, downtime, rework, energy use or labor requirements.
- **Evidence:** Label customer-provided baseline data separately from supplier calculations. Keep estimates identifiable as estimates.
- **Acceptance:** Record the measurement basis, the proposed verification method and the people responsible for confirming results.

Avoid filling missing inputs with confident-looking numbers. An unresolved baseline is a task to complete with the customer. If the customer cannot confirm current downtime or operating workload, record the gap and arrange the necessary discussion before presenting an improvement calculation as an agreed business case.

The aim is to make the proposal reviewable: each proposed benefit should connect an operating change to a measurement and an accountable customer role.

## Prepare different evidence for different decision roles

A key-account project involves distinct questions about funding, daily operations and technical acceptance. Prepare material for each role, while keeping the underlying assumptions consistent.

| Role | Main concern | Useful proposal content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| EB | Funding allocation and approval | Investment phases, funding duration and expected sources of recovery |
| UB | Daily use and operations | Workload changes, downtime implications and actual operating scenarios |
| TB | Technical requirements and acceptance | Parameters, compatibility, data sources and verification methods |

For EB discussions, explain the components of the investment and the proposed route to recovering it. For UB discussions, walk through the work of an operating team and ask the customer to confirm the measurement basis. For TB discussions, resolve questions about compatibility and verification during proposal development.

A common proposal can hold the shared facts, but each role needs a clear answer to its own decision questions.

## Use SPIN questions to turn direction into requirements

A request for localization may express a direction before it defines an executable requirement. Use four types of questions to clarify it.

### Keep the customer's wording in the record

Record the customer's actual answers so that later proposal claims remain traceable to the discussion. The question sequence can follow the conversation rather than a rigid script.

1. **Situation:** What is the current production-line arrangement, and what capacity commitments must it support?
2. **Problem:** Which process currently creates the most difficulty? What happens during that process?
3. **Implication:** How does the difficulty affect delivery, cost or downtime exposure?
4. **Need-payoff:** If that process improved, what would successful operation look like to the customer?

These questions should produce usable requirements. A statement about repeated interruptions needs clarification about the affected process, operating consequences and desired improvement. Preserve uncertainty where the customer cannot yet answer.

Bring the resulting requirements back into the investment explanation. This connects discovery to the financial and operational discussion instead of leaving interview notes disconnected from the proposal.

## Track selling work and buying decisions separately

The source describes five seller stages: contact, needs discovery, proposal, negotiation and closing. It also identifies seven buyer stages:

1. Recognizing a problem.
2. Assessing its value.
3. Confirming that a solution exists.
4. Establishing responsibility.
5. Deciding whether to build internally or buy externally.
6. Selecting a solution and supplier.
7. Making a commitment and signing.

These are two interacting sequences with a many-to-many relationship. A seller's completed proposal does not establish that the customer has resolved responsibility for the operating result.

Check the buyer's position through behavior. Has the customer named internal owners? Has it arranged a discussion of acceptance standards? Has the expenditure entered project documentation or budget materials? Meeting frequency and encouraging statements do not establish those decisions.

A useful review question is: what customer action supports the stage we have recorded? If responsibility remains unresolved, the next task is to clarify ownership with the customer. More quotation detail cannot settle an unassigned operating obligation.

## Scan the decision chain with PRTABCD

Relationship work means understanding how the customer's organization makes decisions and supporting credible action with useful information. The source organizes this intelligence into seven areas:

- **P — Past information:** Relevant history already known about the account or project.
- **R — Requirements:** The needs the customer has expressed.
- **T — Timing:** Deadlines and key project milestones.
- **A — Arm:** The person or people embracing the proposal and willing to help it progress.
- **B — Budget:** What is known about available funding.
- **C — Competitors and alternatives:** Other routes the customer could take.
- **D — Decision:** The decision chain involving multiple people and departments.

For every area, write down what is known, what remains unknown, the information source and the next question. This makes gaps visible without treating assumptions as account intelligence.

Keep A and D distinct. Someone who supports the proposal does not, by that fact alone, represent the entire approval and acceptance chain.

## Prepare conditional exchanges before negotiation

Production-line modifications involve long schedules and multiple interfaces. Prepare for the commercial discussion by examining negotiating power, timing, information and alternatives.

Identify the production milestone behind the customer's time pressure. Establish which terms your team can change, who has authority to approve those changes and what customer commitment would justify each concession.

BATNA means the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: the best executable action available if agreement is not reached. A useful preparation record includes:

- The alternative action and whether it can actually be carried out.
- The minimum acceptable conditions.
- Approval authority for proposed changes.
- The interests each party is trying to protect.
- A record of proposed exchanges and agreed commitments.

Possible alternatives identified in the source include another supplier, phased implementation or a reduced scope. Their availability depends on the party and project; establish feasibility before relying on them.

When price dominates the conversation, return to the customer's cost structure. Use confirmed baseline data to examine relevant operating costs, then discuss what each proposed concession would receive in exchange.

## FAQ

### What if the customer only wants to discuss price?

Use the customer's baseline data to examine the wider cost structure. Depending on the project, relevant items may include rework, downtime, energy and labor. Ask the customer to confirm the comparison basis before using it to support a total-cost discussion.

### How can we tell whether responsibility has been established?

Look for ownership of the proposed operating measures, named coordination and acceptance contacts, and expenditure recorded in project or budget documents. Verbal support alone does not establish those arrangements.

### Should every seller stage match one buyer stage?

No. The relationship is many-to-many. Track the seller's tasks and the buyer's decisions separately, then use customer behavior to judge whether advancement is supported.

### What does Arm mean in PRTABCD?

Arm identifies who within the customer organization embraces the proposal and is willing to help. Decision covers the broader chain of people and departments involved in deciding. Maintain both records.

### What makes a BATNA useful?

It must describe an action the relevant party can execute if negotiations fail. A hypothetical option with unresolved feasibility provides a weak basis for deciding whether to accept terms.
